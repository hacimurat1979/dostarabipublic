Sources, part 8/8 (S)

This section is not a reading summary. It is an attempt to read Dost's words side by side with the findings of physics that do not fit reason; it is our own reading frame. We do not say “the same” or “confirmed”: we only ask, and we say where they part ways. The text is our own rendering of a Turkish original.

  • SEP: Quantum-Bayesian and Pragmatist Views of Quantum Theory — SEP, Quantum-Bayesian and Pragmatist Views of Quantum Theory. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    This entry describes the views that read quantum theory not as a description that tells the world directly, but as a description of our observation, our knowledge, or our interaction with the world. This idea has been expressed in various forms since the 1920s. The entry's focus is a newer branch, Quantum Bayesianism or QBism, and its influences from pragmatist thought. In QBism a quantum state is not the world itself but a belief that shows what a person making a measurement expects from the measurement (we write this as an interpretation of the entry's presentation). The entry also takes up the common objections to this view. This is not an experiment or a theory but a summary of the interpretation literature, and the debate over it continues. What gives one pause is that in this view the 'measurement result' and the 'person making the prediction' become inseparable.

  • SEP: Collapse Theories — SEP, Collapse Theories. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    Quantum mechanics says that before measurement a system can be a combination of several states; but in measurement we see a single result. 'Collapse theories' try to solve this by changing the equation: let measurement not be a separate rule, let collapse be inside the law of physics, spontaneous and objective. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry tells this approach within the framework of the general problems raised by quantum mechanics: is there a world independent of the observer, can we obtain reliable knowledge of it, can some properties really belong to bodies? A section of the entry discusses particular models such as Ghirardi-Rimini-Weber. What is firm: these theories differ from quantum mechanics for now in a few predictions and attempts are being made to test them with experiments. What is disputed: whether they are true, and their superiority over other interpretations. This is a map of interpretations, not an experimental result.

  • SEP: Philosophical Issues in Quantum Theory — SEP, Philosophical Issues in Quantum Theory. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    This is the entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy that offers a general overview of the philosophical problems raised by quantum theory. The text provided is only a one-sentence introduction: the piece is designed as a map of the philosophical issues the theory raises and aims to point to the deeper entries in the encyclopedia. We do not know from the text we have the detailed content of the entry, which problems it lists, or which view it takes; so we do not write them. This is not an experiment or a theory but a pointer source; it counts as a secondhand compilation. Its value for us is that it shows that interpretive questions in quantum theory have a place in the philosophical literature.

    This summary was written, because we could not reach the source's own abstract, only from the general knowledge we are sure of; it should be checked against the source.

  • SEP: Quantum Entanglement and Information — SEP, Quantum Entanglement and Information. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    Two quantum systems can be so related that they cannot be described separately but only as a whole: entanglement. This entry describes how entanglement gives rise to relations with no counterpart in the classical world, that it can be measured, transformed and purified, and that it can be used like a kind of resource. Entangled pairs are used in computation and encryption tasks that cannot be done with classical systems. It also covers its relation to information theory and the debates about what entanglement points to. This is an encyclopedia entry: it presents experimental results, theory and interpretations separately. What gives one pause is that there is a shared wholeness between two things that are far apart and that there is no firm consensus on how this is to be read.

  • SEP: Quantum Field Theory — SEP, Quantum Field Theory. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    This is the entry on quantum field theory in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy; it is not an experiment but an introduction to the theory and its philosophical questions. Quantum field theory is the conceptual and mathematical framework of today's high-energy physics, that is, particle physics; it is also used in other fields such as condensed matter physics. Roughly, it is the form of quantum mechanics that has passed from particles to fields: systems with an infinite number of degrees of freedom. The entry stresses that the picture of the world the theory gives, if taken seriously, does not agree with the classical understanding of particles and fields, nor even with some aspects of quantum mechanics. Among this is the fact that the very concept of 'particle' is problematic. The entry also tells of the theory's present standing and its place among other theories. This is a review source, that is, the interpretation should not be read as a firm verdict.

  • SEP: Identity and Individuality in Quantum Theory — SEP, Identity and Individuality in Quantum Theory. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: does quantum physics allow us to understand particles as individuals of which we can say 'this one' or 'that one' separately? According to the entry, the 'received view' that became widespread in the period when quantum theory was built says that elementary particles cannot be counted as individuals in this sense. On that basis special logical systems have been developed that cannot count individuals and that set up the concept of 'identity' differently. On the other hand, it has also been argued that quantum physics may still be compatible with a metaphysics that speaks of individuals; only in this case these individuals are so indistinguishable from one another that Leibniz's principle of the 'identity of indiscernibles' is violated. This claim has also met objections. The entry is not an experiment but a philosophical inquiry; the conclusions are not firm but disputed. What gives one pause is that the question 'which of the two electrons is which' may lose its meaning.

  • SEP: Being and Becoming in Modern Physics — SEP, Being and Becoming in Modern Physics. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    Is time real, does it flow, do the past and the future exist as much as the present? These questions have been debated for more than two thousand years and there is no resolution. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry says that modern physics has given tools that sharpen these old questions, and puts its focus on that. A sample question: does special relativity show that there is no flow of time, or that the future too is as real as the present? Because in relativity the 'same moment' can change with the person looking, it seems everyone has no common 'now'; what follows from this is disputed. What is firm: the equations of relativity and the measurements of time dilation. What is disputed: what these say about metaphysics, that is, about whether time flows. The entry proceeds by deliberately separating these two areas and lists the arguments of different philosophers.

  • SEP: The Kochen-Specker Theorem — SEP, The Kochen-Specker Theorem. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    The Kochen-Specker theorem shows that a certain kind of 'hidden variable' interpretation of quantum mechanics is impossible. The idea of hidden variables is this: even before a measurement is made, every measurable quantity has a definite value, and the measurement only brings it out. The theorem shows that in systems of three or more dimensions, if you try to assign values to all quantities at once and independently of the way of measuring, you fall into contradiction with the rules of quantum theory. This is a mathematical result, not an experiment, but some aspects of it have later been tested by experiments as well. An important detail: the theorem brings forward the notion of 'context', that is, that the value of a quantity may depend on which other measurements are made together with it. This entry presents the theorem at different depths; detailed proofs are in supplementary documents. What gives one pause is that the intuition 'measurement only reads what is there' cannot be kept in this form.

  • SEP: Quantum Gravity — SEP, Quantum Gravity. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    General relativity describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime; quantum theory describes all the other forces. Quantum gravity is the search for a theory that will cover both, and it has been 'under construction' for more than a hundred years. What is expected is that it will describe how spacetime looks at very small scales (the Planck scale); but that scale is very far from our experiments, and for this reason it is hard to test the proposals by experiment, though some new approaches may change this. In most proposals the gravitational field is also quantized. The entry describes the different approaches and their philosophical questions, for example whether time and space are fundamental. This is a search for a theory; there is no settled result.

  • SEP: Ibn 'Arabi — SEP, Ibn 'Arabi. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    According to the SEP entry, Ibn Arabi (1165-1240) was born in Murcia, and after going to Mecca settled in Damascus and died there; the entry says his writings cover not only Sufism but all the Islamic sciences, from Qur'anic exegesis to jurisprudence, from theology to philosophy. In describing his method it places the concept of tahqiq at the center: the entry reports tahqiq, as the opposite of taqlid, as giving everything its due and realizing the truth in itself (relying on Chittick). In the ontology section, according to what the entry reports, existence is al-Haqq, absolute (unconditioned) existence manifests itself as limited existence, and al-Haqq is known at once through both tanzih (incomparability) and tashbih (similarity). In the imagination section, according to the entry (citing Corbin), reason distinguishes and limits, while imagination sees the self-disclosure of al-Haqq; these are the two eyes of the heart. For the barzakh, the entry gives a description as that which both separates and joins two things and has no two faces of its own, and identifies the greatest barzakh with absolute imagination. The immutable entities, ta'ayyun (a term made current by Qunawi) and the perfect human are also presented in the entry under separate headings, mostly with translated quotations from Ibn Arabi's own texts. The entry is a peer-reviewed entry of the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, first published in 2008, with a substantial revision in December 2025; for this reason it can be read as a summary of the current academic literature. According to the entry's own cautions, Ibn Arabi himself never used the expression wahdat al-wujud (the unity of being) attributed to him, and this formula was attached to him later, first through Ibn Taymiyya's criticism; the entry also mentions that some studies read him as a forerunner of trends in physics or in Western philosophy, and notes that there are studies questioning the validity of such modern lenses and of perennialist readings.
