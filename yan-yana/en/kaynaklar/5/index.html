Sources, part 5/8 (K–P)

This section is not a reading summary. It is an attempt to read Dost's words side by side with the findings of physics that do not fit reason; it is our own reading frame. We do not say “the same” or “confirmed”: we only ask, and we say where they part ways. The text is our own rendering of a Turkish original.

  • Kennard 1927 — Kennard, Zur Quantenmechanik einfacher Bewegungstypen. Z. Phys. 44, 326
    In brief, what does it say?

    The more precisely we know a particle's position, the more uncertain we find its velocity (momentum), and vice versa: the uncertainty principle. Heisenberg had put this forward in 1927 by means of a thought experiment; in the same year Earle Kennard derived the principle by mathematics from within the theory. The result is that the product of the position uncertainty and the momentum uncertainty cannot fall below a certain lower bound (half the reduced Planck constant). This is a result of the theory: it arises not from the crudeness of our measuring instrument but from the structure of the state itself. What gives one pause is this: the bound can be read as a lack of information, or as a property belonging to the state itself; this distinction is a matter of interpretation.

  • Kim 2000 — Kim, Delayed choice quantum eraser. Phys. Rev. Lett. 84, 1
    In brief, what does it say?

    Light passing through two slits forms an interference pattern; if we try to find out which slit it passed through, the pattern is lost. The question was this: what happens if this 'which-path' information is erased afterwards, even after the photon has struck the screen? The team used entangled photon pairs: one (the 'signal') is recorded at the screen, while its twin later reaches a splitter and there either reveals the path information or erases it. When the records were brought together afterwards, the signal photons corresponding to twins whose path information was erased showed an interference pattern among themselves, those whose information was revealed did not. The important point: looking at all the signal photons there is no pattern; the pattern emerges only when they are sorted according to the twin's result. For this reason this experiment does not change the past; saying so is a common misunderstanding. The finding is an experimental result; 'influence on the past' is an interpretation and is not certain as to the real state of affairs.

  • Kimble 2008 — Kimble, The quantum internet. Nature 453, 1023-1030
    In brief, what does it say?

    A short vision paper in Nature that describes the opportunities and challenges of quantum networks.

    Kimble says that quantum networks present a common set of opportunities and challenges for quantum computation, communication and metrology. For a network made up of many nodes and channels to be realized, new scientific capabilities are needed in producing and characterizing quantum coherence and entanglement.

  • Kirchmair 2009 — Kirchmair, State-independent experimental test of quantum contextuality. Nature 460, 494
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: if we assume that the measured properties have definite values before measurement, do we contradict quantum predictions? Quantum contextuality is the fact that the result of measuring a property can change according to which other measurement is made alongside it; that is, the result is not the property's possession by itself. In this work the researchers measured, on trapped ions, an inequality that tests this idea, and it is important that it is 'state-independent': the violation of the inequality appears not for a special state in which the system is prepared but whatever state it is prepared in. The result showed that the picture that assumes predetermined and context-independent values does not agree with experiment. This is an experimental finding. What is to be put in its place is a matter of interpretation. The experiment uses not distant particles as in the Bell test but multiple measurements of a single system.

  • Kitaev 2003 — Kitaev, Fault-tolerant quantum computation by anyons. Annals of Physics 303, 2-30
    In brief, what does it say?

    Kitaev proposed computing by moving excitations called anyons around one another in a two-dimensional system, and that this is inherently fault-tolerant.

    The abstract of the paper is short and clear: a two-dimensional quantum system can be thought of as a quantum computer if it carries anyon excitations. In three dimensions there are only two kinds of fundamental particles, fermions and bosons; in two dimensions "anyons," which occupy a place between them, are possible. When two anyons are exchanged, the state of the system undergoes a definite transformation.

  • Kochen 1967 — Kochen, The Problem of Hidden Variables in Quantum Mechanics. Indiana University Mathematics Journal (J. Math. Mech.) 17, 59-87
    In brief, what does it say?

    The original mathematical paper showing that quantum mechanics contradicts the assumption of noncontextuality.

    Kochen and Specker examine mathematically whether 'hidden variables' can be added to quantum mechanics. A hidden variable theory is the idea that the uncertainty of quantum results comes only from our lack of knowledge and that there is a predetermined value for every measurement.

  • Kocsis 2011 — Kocsis, Observing the Average Trajectories of Single Photons in a Two-Slit Interferometer. Science 332, 1170-1173
    In brief, what does it say?

    The experiment that reconstructs, by weak measurement, the average paths of photons inside a double-slit interference.

    In the double-slit experiment, single photons leave dots on a screen; when enough photons are collected, an interference pattern appears. The question is what path a single photon follows after the slits. In quantum mechanics, measuring the path destroys the pattern. This work, instead of 'measuring' the path, reconstructs the average path by weak measurement.

  • Lamoreaux 1997 — Lamoreaux, Demonstration of the Casimir force in the 0.6 to 6 μm range. Phys. Rev. Lett. 78, 5
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was: is 'empty space' really nothing? According to quantum theory, even in empty space a stirring remains in the lowest state of the electromagnetic field (zero-point fluctuation). The theory said that two conducting surfaces brought very close together would attract each other because they change this stirring; this is called the Casimir force. Lamoreaux measured this very small force on a torsion pendulum, at separations between the surfaces of about 0.6 to 6 micrometers. The result agreed with the theory to about 5 percent. So 'empty space' can give rise to a measurable force. Here the experiment is the existence and size of the force; the interpretation, whether this means 'the vacuum is full' or is another way of describing the surfaces' interaction with each other, is still discussed among physicists. What gives one pause is that a tangible effect was measured 'where there is nothing.'

  • Leifer 2014 — Leifer, Is the quantum state real? An extended review of psi-ontology theorems. Quanta 3, 67
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question of whether the quantum state is something that really exists in the world (ontic) or our knowledge (epistemic) is an old one. In late 2011 Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph put forward a theorem, and it drew much interest and debate. Leifer's long review first tells the background of the subject, then presents the theorem in plain language, then goes over the related work done afterward. It defines what it means for a state to be 'ontic' or 'epistemic' and lists the arguments before the theorem, for and against. An important point: to prove the reality of the state is to place a very hard constraint on realist theories. This is a theoretical review; not an experiment. What is firm: the mathematics of the theorems. What is disputed: whether the assumptions are in place and what conclusion will come out.

  • Lindblad 1976 — Lindblad, On the generators of quantum dynamical semigroups. Communications in Mathematical Physics 48, 119-130
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper that gives the most general form of the generators of quantum dynamical semigroups.

    Lindblad examines the semigroups that describe the time evolution of a quantum system interacting with its environment. A semigroup is a time evolution in which the state after a certain time step can be obtained by applying two half steps in succession; this corresponds to the assumption that the environment is memoryless (Markov).

  • Lloyd 1996 — Lloyd, Universal Quantum Simulators. Science 273, 1073
    In brief, what does it say?

    Lloyd showed that quantum computers can efficiently simulate local quantum systems.

    In 1982 Feynman had intuitively proposed that a quantum computer could imitate quantum systems. Lloyd’s question is whether this is mathematically correct.

  • Ma 2016 — Ma, Delayed-choice gedanken experiments and their realizations. Rev. Mod. Phys. 88, 015005
    In brief, what does it say?

    When light or a particle enters a device with two possible paths, whether it will behave like a wave or like a particle depends on what is measured at the end of the device. Wheeler had asked: 'what if we decide what to measure at the end only after the particle has entered the device?' This review article tells the history of this 'delayed choice' idea and collects its versions carried out in the laboratory, such as having the choice made by a fast random number generator. The finding is this: the results come out as the theory predicts, that is, whether interference appears or not agrees with the final choice. What gives one pause is that this can be read as 'changing the past'. The article objects to this reading: the experimental result is firm, but 'influencing the past' is an interpretation and the usual theory does not need it. An additional topic of the article is the duality between separability and entanglement.

  • Maldacena 1997 — Maldacena, The large N limit of superconformal field theories and supergravity. Adv. Theor. Math. Phys. 2, 231
    In brief, what does it say?

    There are two great frameworks in physics: quantum field theories (the quantum physics of particles) and gravity (Einstein's theory of spacetime). Uniting the two is an unsolved question. Maldacena proposed that a certain kind of quantum field theory (with many components, in the 'large N' limit, of very high symmetry) can carry the same information as a string theory with gravity in a curved spacetime of another dimension. That is, a description without gravity on a surface is like the counterpart of a description with gravity in a depth. The paper puts this forward as a 'conjecture' and supports it with reasons derived from string theory. This is a theoretical proposition; it has no direct experiment. It has passed many tests and mathematical checks and is used very heavily, but whether it is true for our universe is a separate question. Why it gives one pause: that a world with an extra dimension can be fully matched by a description on a boundary with fewer dimensions.

  • Maldacena 2013 — Maldacena, Cool horizons for entangled black holes. Fortschr. Phys. 61, 781
    In brief, what does it say?

    Maldacena and Susskind put forward a proposal that identifies entanglement with a spacetime bridge, and used it as a way out of the firewall paradox.

    General relativity contains solutions in which two distant black holes are connected through the interior region by a bridge, that is, an Einstein–Rosen bridge. The authors interpret these solutions as a maximally entangled state of two black holes; that is, a complex EPR pair.

  • Micadei 2019 — Micadei, Reversing the direction of heat flow using quantum correlations. Nat. Commun. 10, 2456
    In brief, what does it say?

    Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold; this is the established rule of thermodynamics. However, this rule assumes that the two systems are initially unrelated to each other. Micadei and colleagues prepared two spins by nuclear magnetic resonance in a correlated (entangled) state and observed heat flowing from cold to hot. The direction of the flow was determined by a trade-off between the correlations and entropy. This is an experiment. What gives one pause is that the correlations can change the direction without violating the second law of thermodynamics; the law is being re-read with a broader view of information. As an application, heat control at the micro scale has been proposed.

  • Minev 2019 — Minev, To catch and reverse a quantum jump mid-flight. Nature 570, 200
    In brief, what does it say?

    In quantum physics a measurement can give discrete and random outcomes; the 'quantum jump' Bohr proposed in 1913, an atom's sudden transition between two energy levels, is the symbol of this. When jumps would occur was held to be fundamentally unpredictable. The question: is it possible to know when a jump is about to happen? The team, in an artificial atom with three levels (a superconducting circuit), tracked the jump from the ground level to the excited level by monitoring the occupation of an auxiliary level. The finding: every completed jump proceeds with a continuous, coherent and deterministic course. With real-time monitoring and feedback, the team caught a jump midway and reversed it, that is, prevented its completion. The results agree with the predictions of quantum trajectory theory for jumps. This is an experimental finding; the paper says it refutes Bohr's view. What gives one pause is that there turns out to be a course within an event thought to be 'sudden'.

  • Misra 1977 — Misra, The Zeno's paradox in quantum theory. J. Math. Phys. 18, 756
    In brief, what does it say?

    An unstable particle, for example an atomic nucleus about to decay, turns into other particles over time. Misra and Sudarshan asked: what happens if we observe this particle continuously, that is, if we keep looking and asking 'has it decayed?' In their calculation with the mathematics of quantum theory, if the looks are frequent enough, the result is that the particle never decays, something like 'a watched pot never boils'. This result is called the quantum Zeno paradox. The paper is a theory and calculation paper; it rests on the assumption that measurement restarts the state each time. Later, other groups showed in the laboratory that frequent measurement really does slow decay; this is the result not of this paper but of the later experiments. What gives one pause is that looking, that is, measuring, is not merely taking in information but affects the course of the system.

  • Mohideen 1998 — Mohideen, Precision Measurement of the Casimir Force from 0.1 to 0.9 μm. Phys. Rev. Lett. 81, 4549
    In brief, what does it say?

    Mohideen and Roy measured the Casimir force much more precisely, with an atomic force microscope, over a range of 0.1–0.9 μm.

    This work, carried out a few years after Lamoreaux’s measurement, aims to measure the same force at much smaller separations and with smaller uncertainty. The question: does the Casimir force agree with theory at the one percent level once the effects of real materials and of temperature are taken into account?

  • Monroe 1996 — Monroe, A "Schrödinger cat" superposition state of an atom. Science 272, 1131
    In brief, what does it say?

    Monroe and colleagues prepare a single trapped beryllium ion in a superposition of two spatially separated states and verify it by interference.

    According to the paper’s abstract, the authors have produced a “Schrödinger cat”-like state at the level of a single atom. A 9Be+ ion held in a trap is first laser-cooled to its zero-point energy. The ion is then prepared in a superposition of two spatially separate coherent harmonic oscillator states.

  • Moreva 2014 — Moreva, Time from quantum entanglement: An experimental illustration. Phys. Rev. A 89, 052122
    In brief, what does it say?

    For someone looking at the universe from outside, does time flow? According to a proposal by Page and Wootters, the total state of the universe may be unchanging, 'frozen'; time arises from the entanglement of one part of the system (a clock) with the other parts. This paper shows a small scene of this idea in the laboratory. Two photons are prepared in an entangled state; one is used like a clock, the other is measured with it. An observer from inside, attached to the clock photon, sees the other photon change over time. An observer from outside who looks only at the joint state of the two sees no change at all: the state stands still. This is a demonstration experiment: it brings the idea to life in a small setup, it does not show that the universe really works this way. Whether time really arises in this way is a theoretical and open question.

  • Ollivier 2004 — Ollivier, Objective properties from subjective quantum states: Environment as a witness. Physical Review Letters 93, 220401
    In brief, what does it say?

    One of the first formulations that treats the environment not as a passive destroyer but as an active witness that multiplies information.

    The paper asks how objective properties emerge in open quantum systems. An open system is one that interacts with its environment; photons, air molecules or other particles pass by the system and carry a trace of it. In the classical account of decoherence, the environment was seen as a 'reservoir' that selectively destroys quantum coherence. The authors give the environment a second role: a communication channel that carries and broadcasts information about the system.

  • Ozawa 2003 — Ozawa, Universally valid reformulation of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle on noise and disturbance in measurement. Phys. Rev. A 67, 042105
    In brief, what does it say?

    The Heisenberg uncertainty principle is often told like this: the more sharply you measure the position of a particle, the more you disturb its velocity (more precisely its momentum); the product of the two cannot fall below a certain lower bound. Ozawa showed mathematically that this lower bound on the product of 'measurement error and disturbance' does not hold in every case. In the correct relation there are extra terms; if there is a certain link between the measuring instrument and the thing measured, the product can be smaller than Heisenberg's bound. The paper also built a measurement model showing this and proposed an experiment that could be done with light. These are results at the level of theory; the paper itself presents not an experiment but an experimental design. What gives one pause is not the collapse of the principle itself but the correction of its often-told measurement-disturbance form. Other forms, that is, the relations concerning the spreads of the states themselves, remain valid as they are.

  • Page 1983 — Page, Evolution without evolution: Dynamics described by stationary observables. Phys. Rev. D 27, 2885
    In brief, what does it say?

    The equations of physics always take time as a 'parameter'; but if the whole universe is a closed system, who has a clock outside the universe? Don Page and William Wootters offered a proposal: the universe can be static, and change arises from comparing one part of the system with another part (an internal clock). That is, the impression that 'time is passing' is read from the relation between what the clock shows and the rest. This is a theoretical proposal, not an experiment. What gives one pause is that change is not seen in the universe as a whole and that change emerges only from the relation between parts. Whether it is really so is a matter of interpretation and dispute.

  • Page 1993 — Page, Information in black hole radiation. Phys. Rev. Lett. 71, 3743
    In brief, what does it say?

    Page estimated how fast and in what way information would appear if it comes back in the black hole radiation.

    Page's paper begins with a question: if black hole formation and evaporation can be described by an S-matrix, that is, a unitary mapping from the initial state to the final state, then information must come out together with the radiation. When and to what extent does this exit take place?

  • Pauli 1925 — Pauli, Über den Zusammenhang des Abschlusses der Elektronengruppen im Atom mit der Komplexstruktur der Spektren. Z. Phys. 31, 765-783
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper that proposes an exclusion rule by relating the closing of electron shells in atoms to the complex splittings of spectral lines in a magnetic field.

    The paper’s starting point was two separate puzzles. The first was that the shells in the periodic table fill up to 2, 8, 18, 32 electrons and why these numbers close at these values. The second was that spectral lines split in a magnetic field in a more complicated way than expected, that is, the anomalous Zeeman effect. Moreover, it was also unclear why all the electrons in an atom do not gather in the lowest-energy state.

  • Pednault 2019 — Pednault, Leveraging Secondary Storage to Simulate Deep 54-qubit Sycamore Circuits. arXiv:1910.09534 (preprint)
    In brief, what does it say?

    A response arguing that Sycamore circuits could be simulated within days on a classical supercomputer by using secondary storage.

    An earlier work by the authors had shown that secondary storage (disks) can extend the range of quantum circuits that can be practically run in classical simulation. This paper improves those techniques and applies them to Sycamore circuits of 53 and 54 qubits. The paper appeared as a preprint shortly before the publication of Google's Nature paper.
