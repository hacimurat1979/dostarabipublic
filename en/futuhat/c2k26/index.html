The Intent Behind the Sign, the Fire of Satan, and the Illusion of Time

pp. 347–385

Site reading summary

Part XXVI has six chapters. Chapter Fifty-Four defines the Sufis' language of 'signs' (isharat) and contrasts it with the literalist scholars' outward reading; here Abu Yazid al-Bistami's saying 'you took your knowledge from the dead, we took ours from the undying living one' and Abu Madyan's demand for 'fresh meat' are quoted. Chapter Fifty-Five divides thought into four sources — lordly, angelic, of-the-soul, and satanic — and describes how a 'spiritual satan' can twist even a sound principle (like love of the Prophet's household) into excess. Chapter Fifty-Six shows that induction (istiqra) is valid in matters of conduct but a dangerous method for reasoning about God's essence and attributes. Chapters Fifty-Seven and Fifty-Eight separate inspiration (ilham) from knowledge-from-presence (ledunni), pick apart reason's total dependence on the chain of reflection-imagination-senses, and conclude that the 'heart' — unlike reason — is the one organ of perception able to hold together transcendence (tanzih) and likeness (tashbih) at once; this chapter closes with the Pen-Tablet-Throne-Footstool-Lote-Tree hierarchy and a description of the damned as 'neither dying nor living.' Chapter Fifty-Nine argues that time itself has no external existence and is known only through imagination, while still asking how a 'decreed day' (like the Antichrist's year-long day) can be reckoned independently of the ordinary day.

What Is a Sign: Sickness, Claim, and the Veiling of the Real's Face

Chapter Fifty-Four opens with a poem: "The science of signs is a drawing near and a drawing away / its course within you is a return to the origin and a leaning upon it" (p. 347). Dost begins with a definition he quotes from a shaykh in Mahasin al-Majalis: "A sign is a crying out from a distance, disclosing the presence of one's sickness." (p. 347) He is careful to note that he uses 'illa' here not in the rationalists' sense of 'cause' but directly as 'sickness.' For the verifiers, nothing but God exists; what depends for its existence on something else is, in Dost's words, 'as good as nonexistent' (pp. 347-348). A sign sometimes becomes fixed and its ruling appears — in which case who is being signaled to must be clarified.

Jurists and Gnostics: The Two Faces of the Same Verse

Dost sets up the contrast between the 'people of God' and the 'literalist scholars' (jurists) in sharp terms: "God created no one more harsh and severe toward His own people than the literalist scholars." (p. 347) He likens these scholars' stance toward the people of God to that of Pharaoh toward the prophets. He also likens his companions' (the Sufis') turn to the language of signs to Mary's own turn to sign-language when accused of slander (pp. 348-349). A central thesis then follows: "Every verse that has been sent down has two faces: one that the gnostics see within themselves, the other that they see outside themselves." (p. 349) The literalist scholars call this inner meaning not 'tafsir' but 'ishara' (sign), for fear of being accused of unbelief — Dost attributes this to their not knowing the dimensions of God's address. Two sayings attributed to Ali b. Abi Talib are quoted: "This is nothing but the understanding of the Qur'an that God gives to whichever of His servants He wills." (p. 351) and "Had I spoken about Surat al-Fatiha alone, I would have loaded seventy camels." (p. 352)

'Fresh Meat' and 'Dried Meat': The Source of Given Knowledge

The chapter quotes Abu Yazid al-Bistami's words addressed to the literalist scholars: "You took your knowledge from the dead, while we took ours from the undying living one. Those of us like this say, 'my heart conveyed to me from my Lord.' You say, 'so-and-so conveyed to me from so-and-so.' Asked where that person is, you say he died. And where is the one he conveyed from? He too has died." (p. 352) Shaykh Abu Madyan is reported to have voiced the same stance in an image: when chains of transmission were recited to him, he would say, "We do not want dried meat. Go and bring fresh meat." (pp. 352-353) Dost reads this not as a hint of severance but as a direct declaration of preferred source: the one who gives the knowledge has not died and is 'closer to a person than his jugular vein' (p. 353). The example of the Prophet baring his head and chest to the rain also appears in this chapter — when asked why, he reportedly said, "It has just come from my Lord." (p. 353) The chapter closes with an everyday illustration of how a sign operates: when someone in distress calls out to a man named 'Faraj' (the one who relieves), the name itself carries comfort; the Prophet's own reading a good omen into the name of the envoy Suhayl — saying 'the matter will become easy (sahula al-amr)' when he arrived — follows the same logic (pp. 353-354). Dost says: a sign is realized not by the thing signaled itself, but by the intent of the one who signals.

The Four Sources of Thought: Lordly, Angelic, of-the-Soul, Satanic

Chapter Fifty-Five opens with a classification: "Thoughts are of four kinds, with no fifth: lordly thought, angelic thought, thought of the soul, and satanic thought." (p. 354) Dost states that in this chapter he will focus specifically on satanic thought. He first divides satans into spiritual and material; the material kind further splits into human satans and jinn satans (p. 354). Between these two — within the human being's own self — there also forms a 'spiritual satan': when either a jinn or human satan cracks open a general doorway in the heart, the soul deepens through that doorway on its own, giving rise to doubts that even the satan himself did not fully intend (p. 355).

The Spiritual Satan: How a Sound Principle Gets Twisted

Dost says the spiritual satan is seen most often among the Imami Shia: jinn satans first plant love of the Prophet's household — sound in itself as a principle — then drag that love toward two extremes: one group develops a hatred that ends in cursing the Companions, while another goes further still, sliding into disrespect toward prophecy itself, to the point of saying 'the trustworthy one who sent the Trustworthy One was not himself trustworthy' (pp. 355-356). Dost comments: "All these deviations stem from a sound principle, which is loving the Prophet's household." (p. 356) Another example group misuses the hadith 'whoever institutes a good practice receives its reward' by fabricating hadith in the Prophet's name so their innovation will be accepted — and even while doing so, believes itself to be doing good (pp. 356-358). The same motif appears among the people of ascetic discipline and retreat: someone who introduces a practice out of a craving for leadership later attributes it directly to God, turning himself into 'an Ash'ari who accepts necessity' (p. 358). The chapter also relates Iblis coming to Jesus in the form of an old man, saying, "Say, 'there is no god but God'" — to which Jesus replies, "I do not say it because you said it: 'there is no god but God'", and Iblis withdraws in sorrow (pp. 358-359). From this scene Dost draws out the difference between knowing a thing and believing it: happiness lies in whose word you say it on — being linked to the right chain, prophet to prophet.

The Criterion of Discernment: Fire's Restlessness, Earth's Constancy

At this point Dost asks a practical question: is there a sign for telling satanic thought apart from the other kinds? The answer lies in the material of creation: "Satan is greedy, and he was created from the flame of fire. The flame of fire moves swiftly... Iblis's created nature is not to remain in a single state." (p. 359) The human being, by contrast, being created from earth, is constant and settled — carrying the qualities of coldness and dryness. Accordingly, rapidly veering from one thought to another on the same matter is the most reliable sign of a satanic thought (p. 359). Dost then also proposes discerning a thought's source by which legal ruling it matches: a thought about the forbidden or the disliked is necessarily from Satan, one about the permissible is from the soul, and one about the obligatory is, without hesitation, from an angel (p. 361). The recommended is the most delicate zone: when an urge arises to drop a first recommended thought in favor of a second that seems religiously superior, Dost's advice is clear: "Never abandon the first... once you complete the first, begin the second." (p. 361) Someone who holds to this discipline is called one who 'holds Umar's station,' and for such a person, should Satan cross his path in a valley, 'he will surely change his own course' (p. 361).

Induction's Two Domains: Valid in Conduct, Invalid for Essence and Attributes

Chapter Fifty-Six opens with a hadith qudsi transmitted by Imam Muslim: "The angels intercede, the prophets intercede, the believers intercede, and there remains the Most Merciful of the merciful." (p. 362) Dost says that when we examine existence inductively (istiqra), fine traits such as repaying good with good, forgiving wrongs, and overlooking mistakes are always found to flow from 'the true goodnesses,' and this induction does not go wrong: "God has more right to fine character than His creatures do." (p. 363) But he immediately draws a hard boundary: "The soundness of induction here holds in matters of conduct. Faulty induction, however, cannot be used in matters of belief." (p. 363) He demonstrates this with a thought experiment: if we surveyed the agents of acts inductively, we would find them all to be bodies; since the world is the Real's act, this logic would force us to conclude that 'the Real, too, is a body' — "God is exalted and utterly transcendent of any such thing." (p. 363) He shows the same trap operating in the debate over God's attributes: the theologians' formula, that 'the attributes are neither Him nor other than Him,' is secretly the product of an induction drawn from created things — in Dost's words, "This is induction itself." (p. 364)

There Is No Repetition in Self-Disclosure: The Limit of Induction

Dost now tests induction's validity through matter's capacity to accept form: wood accepts the form of a chair or a pulpit but not a shirt, while cloth can do the opposite (pp. 364-365). Junayd's answer, when asked about gnosis and the gnostic, is inserted here: "The color of water is the color of its container." (pp. 364-365) The elements, the celestial spheres, spirits, and finally imagination are surveyed one by one inductively — imagination emerges as the widest of all, since it accepts anything that has a form (p. 365). But when it comes to the unseen realm, that is, to self-disclosure, induction collapses entirely: "God does not disclose Himself to a single person twice in the same form, nor does He appear to two people in a single form." (p. 366) Dost supports this with reports of Gabriel repeatedly coming in the form of Dihya al-Kalbi — implying that even these seemingly repeated appearances are, each time, a different self-disclosure. The chapter closes with this sentence: "So induction cannot be relied upon in any matter whatsoever: not in states, not in stations, not in waystations, and not in self-disclosures." (p. 366)

The Soul: The Isthmus Between Sin and God-Wariness, the Source of the Permissible

Chapter Fifty-Seven opens with a direct quotation of the verse: "By the soul and Him who fashioned it and inspired it with its wickedness and its God-wariness." (p. 367) Dost stresses that the soul does not itself 'produce' either sin or God-wariness; it is merely the locus where either ruling can appear — "the middle isthmus between these two rulings" (p. 367). The permissible is a different category: God does not attribute the thought of the permissible to the soul as 'inspiration,' because the permissible is already the soul's own natural, essential trait — a quality proper to the soul, "like a person's laughing" (p. 367). Concerning the hidden subject in the verse 'inspired it', Dost notes that, on one reading, God-wariness can be attributed to the angel and wickedness to Satan, but that God 'did not gather the two of them under a single pronoun, given the distance of the relation between them' — uniting the angel and Satan with God under the same pronoun would have been 'the utmost limit of disrespect toward God' (pp. 368-369). The Prophet's rebuke to a preacher — "what a bad speaker you are" — for joining himself and God under a single pronoun is the concrete example of this rule of propriety (p. 369).

The Difference Between Inspiration and Knowledge-from-Presence

Dost uses the metaphor of the sun to explain that the divine bestowal never ceases, and is received only according to the recipient's own receptivity: "The sun spreads its rays over existent things and is not stingy toward anyone in spreading them. The locus, however, receives that light only according to its own receptivity." (p. 370) The same verse awakens different meanings in different people, the same self-disclosure takes different forms in different loci of disclosure — showing that deprivation comes not from God but from a person's own limited receptivity (pp. 370-371). The chapter draws this distinction: "Inspiration is transient and accidental; it comes and goes, replaced by another. Knowledge-from-presence, however, is continuously fixed and does not depart." (p. 371) Part of knowledge-from-presence is present in the very nature of creation — as when animals and children know certain benefits and harms; this is not inspiration but 'necessary knowledge,' and the verse 'your Lord revealed to the bee' is an example of this kind (p. 371). Inspiration, on the other hand, is something the servant did not know before being inspired with it, and "Knowledge-from-presence is always correct. Inspiration, however, can be sometimes correct and sometimes mistaken. The correct portion of it is called inspired knowledge, while the mistaken portion is not called inspired knowledge at all, but merely inspiration." (p. 372)

What Does Reason Imitate? The Chain of Reflection, Imagination, and the Senses

Chapter Fifty-Eight opens with a long preamble: through theoretical proofs, reason comes to know the Real's existence, His oneness, the possibility of prophecy — but grows 'hesitant' before some of what the prophet reports, since its own proof had judged these things impossible (pp. 372-374). Dost's central point is that reason cannot know anything by itself: "One of the things that astonishes us most is that a human being imitates his own reflection and theoretical thought." (p. 374) The faculty of reflection serves reason, and reason imitates what reflection gives it; yet reason shows no such imitation toward what God reports in His book and through the tongue of His prophet — Dost calls this "one of the strangest errors to have occurred in the world." (pp. 374-375) A whole chain is then laid out: reason needs the ear (to distinguish sounds), needs the eye (to know colors), imagination needs the senses and memory, and the cogitative faculty needs imagination and the formative faculty (pp. 374-376). Dost concludes: "We have learned that, in itself, there is nothing at all in reason." (p. 376) The text then turns to the people of ascetic discipline and retreat, who withdraw entirely from the chain of sense-imagination-reflection and turn directly to God.

The Heart: Beyond Reasoning, Where Likeness and Transcendence Meet

Having abandoned the sense-imagination-reflection chain entirely, the people of ascetic discipline arrive, for Dost, at 'another kind of knowledge' — "God scattered a divine knowledge upon him from His light... making known that no existent thing can either accept or reject Him." (p. 376) At this point Dost says the verse's phrase "there is a reminder for one who has a heart" points not to reason but to another faculty entirely — the heart — because 'every human being has reason, yet not every human being has been given this faculty the verse calls heart' (p. 377). The heart's defining feature is that it never ceases turning from state to state: "The heart is between two of the All-Merciful's fingers, which He turns however He wills." (p. 377) Reason confines, while the heart does not confine — which is why knowing the Real as the Real can occur through, is why it happens through the heart and not through reason (pp. 377-378). For Dost, God, in the Book and the Sunna, accepts both likeness ("He is the Hearing, the Seeing") and transcendence ("there is nothing like unto Him") together — "So neither declare Him transcendent... nor confine Him! If God withdraws from confinement, He becomes confined in His very absoluteness." (p. 378) Dost's conclusion is that neither a pure transcendence that drops likeness, nor a pure likeness that drops transcendence, can properly declare the Real transcendent: the one faculty able to hold both together at once is the heart.

The Lote-Tree of the Uttermost Boundary: The Pen-Tablet-Throne-Footstool-Lote Hierarchy, and 'Neither Dying Nor Living' in Hell

The second half of the chapter (set off under a heading marked 'vasl') shifts to a very different subject, a cosmological hierarchy: "As for the secrets of the inferring people of inspiration, these secrets cannot pass beyond the Lote-Tree of the Uttermost Boundary. For the deeds of the children of Adam arrive there. Every affair ends where it began." (p. 378) Dost distributes the source of the legal rulings across five ranks: "The obligatory arises from the Pen, the recommended from the Tablet, the forbidden from the Throne, the disliked from the Footstool, and the permissible from the Lote-Tree." (p. 379) Each rank looks upon its own deeds with its own character — for instance, since the Throne is where the All-Merciful establishes Himself, it looks upon those who commit what is forbidden only with an eye of mercy, so 'the final resting place of those who commit forbidden deeds is mercy' (p. 379). The chapter closes with the condition of those in Hell. If the All-Merciful creates a comfort for them where they are, "they thus neither die nor remain alive." (p. 379) Dost explains this through the example of a sick beggar who dreams himself rich and powerful: an outside observer could say he is 'in comfort,' because relative to his own state, that is so (p. 380).

Time: A Relation with No External Existence, Known Through Imagination

Chapter Fifty-Nine also opens with a poem: "If you learn its outcome, time is indeed / real. It is known through imaginings... Time and nature have no external existence." (p. 380) Dost first establishes that the Real is 'the First' (al-Awwal): nothing has priority before Him or alongside Him, and the world's existence must be either through the Real Himself or through 'an added relation' — Dost eliminates both options one by one, showing that all that remains is the world's being 'contingent,' that is, its existence depending on another (pp. 380-382). After this long metaphysical preparation he turns to the actual topic: "The relation of eternity-without-beginning to God is like the relation of time to us. The relation of eternity-without-beginning is a negative description with no external entity (ayn)." (p. 382) Time, likewise, is 'a relation that has no existence, an imaginary existence' — because of anything supposed, one can ask 'when,' and this very question renders time imaginary (p. 382). Dost reads the Prophet's approving response to the question in the hadith, 'where was our Lord before He created the world,' as proof that time is not something existing in itself: "Had time been an existential (wujudi) condition, God's transcendence beyond confinement would not hold." (pp. 382-383)

The Actual Day and the Decreed Day: The Antichrist's Days, the Reckoning of Prayer

Having noted that the word 'time' is used differently among philosophers, theologians, and the Arabs, Dost states his own preference: the Arabs mean by time the day and the night, and this is what he wants to take up in this chapter (p. 383). The span from sunrise to sunset is day, from sunset to sunrise is night, and their sum is 'a day' — and the Qur'an, in certain verses, expands this 'day' to far larger scales: "in a day whose measure is a thousand years of what you count" and "in a day whose measure is fifty thousand years"Meâric 70:4 (p. 383). The chapter cites the hadith on the Antichrist's days: "one of his days will be like a year, one day like a month, one day like a week, and his remaining days like your ordinary days." (pp. 383-384) Aisha's practical question — 'how is prayer to be performed on such a day?' — is resolved by the Prophet's brief answer: "The day is reckoned for prayer." (p. 384) Dost supports this with the example of reckoning prayer times through astronomical instruments (even on cloudy days). The chapter closes by noting that the smallest unit of time is the 'individual instant' — the moment pointed to by the verse "God is upon some task every day" — and, after summarizing an old debate among the rationalists over time's finitude or infinitude, with a note that the meaning of God's name al-Dahr will be taken up later 'in this book' (pp. 384-385).
