One Voice, Seventy Letters: The Descent of the Divine Command and the Concealment of the Malamis

pp. 13–42

Site reading summary

The opening four chapters of Volume XI begin with the angels' dispute, born from the conflict inherent in their own nature. They treat the divine command's descent — from the Name, through Intellect, Soul, the Throne, the Footstool, the heavens, to the earth — taking on the form of each rank it passes through, likened to a single voice dividing into more than seventy letters. The theory of temperament describes the gathering of opposing elements (hot-cold, moist-dry) within a single body through neighboring relation. And the close distinguishes three classes of God's people — the devout, the Sufis who do not conceal their miracles, and the Malamis who conceal their states entirely.

The Angels' Dispute: Conflict Born from the Natural World

After the close of Volume X, Volume XI opens with the Twenty-First Sifr; carrying no explicit reference to the previous volume or announcement of a new theme, it plunges directly into the subject of Chapter 306 (Mosaic, Knowing the Dispute of the Supreme Assembly). The root of the angels' dispute over which act of worship is more excellent is explained: "the angels, too, arose from the natural world, and were created with the property of 'conflict' and enmity... the light from which the angels were created is natural light. Thus the angels are in one respect suited to it, and in another respect discordant with it. This is the reason the Supreme Assembly falls into disagreement on a matter" (pp. 15-16). Expiations are also defined in this chapter — a "cover" against the wound opened by sin (the root of the word being "kufr," to cover) — and the angels' dispute over matters they themselves have not experienced (expiation, ablution, congregational prayer) is said to liken them to "inexperienced physicians."

The Acts Disputed by the Supreme Assembly and the Reason for the Dispute

The prose of Chapter 306 treats the Supreme Assembly's dispute over which act of worship is more excellent. Dost recalls the statement God relates from His Prophet: “I had no knowledge of the Supreme Assembly when they disputed.” The acts disputed are these: expiations, praying in congregation, passing the ablution water between the fingers, following the mosques, and making a habit of sitting in the mosque after prayer (p. 14). “More excellent” has two meanings: which act is dearer to God, and which brings its doer a higher degree in Paradise (p. 14). Dost says that had God informed them of the most excellent act and of the link between the acts and the degrees of Paradise, they would not have disputed: “So it is God who withholds this knowledge from them” (p. 15).

Dost relates the angels' position on this through a comparison: “In disputing such a matter they resemble a company of scholars disputing, say, the question of menstruation, in which they have no experience” (p. 15). Expiations were established to make good the commands and prohibitions of God that the person under obligation has violated; the angels are beings to whose sinlessness God Himself bears witness, described as “doing what they are commanded,” so they have no experience of expiations, ablution, congregational prayer or the acts of the mosque (p. 15). Should the angels' rushing to the gatherings of remembrance be raised as an objection, the answer is that remembrance is not the same as prayer; the angels do not join the congregation like the children of Adam; Gabriel taught the Prophet the prayer by performing it; and waiting in the mosque after prayer is likewise an act belonging not to the angels but to those who pray (p. 16). The reason for the dispute is “their desire, in their inspirations, to call the human being to righteous action and to direct him to the most excellent act” (p. 16).

Expiations: A Shield and Cover Against the Calamity of Sin

Dost defines expiations as a barrier: “Expiations were commanded and established so that there be a barrier between the servant and the things to which he exposes himself. When the human being violates an obligation he was commanded to perform or a prohibition he was commanded to avoid, he is exposed to calamities. When the name al-Muntaqim brings a calamity upon the place where the sin was committed, it finds that the acts that are expiations have covered and surrounded that place” (p. 16). The name that rules over the expiation is al-Ghaffar, while al-Muntaqim finds no breach; “kufr” likewise means covering, and the farmer is called “kafir” because he throws the seed into the earth and covers it (p. 16). Dost cites the hadith on the adulterer: “Faith leaves the adulterer and stays over him like a shadow until it returns to him.” In his words, “Faith is a strong veil, and while faith is present it is no more possible for calamity to work on the servant than to reach him” (pp. 16-17). The limit of expiation is also set: “For expiations do not raise degrees; they only protect from calamities” (p. 17).

Dost says the word “kaffarat” comes in an intensive plural form, which shows that in an act of sin there are many calamities: a sin contains different movements, each movement has its own calamity with God, and for each calamity the act that is its expiation becomes the veil that prevents the calamity from reaching the person. Repentance is an expiation, and the particular calamity it removes is one in which there is no multiplicity or excess, for “the divine commands come to pass according to the divine measures and balances placed in the world, especially in punishments” (p. 17). The pilgrim who shaves his head at hajj or umra has different expiations; whichever he performs, the others fall away, and the expiation of a broken oath is likewise. The angels' dispute may concern which of the alternative expiations is more fitting, or why an act is an expiation at all (pp. 17-18).

The Divine Realities Behind the Acts: Steps, Walking and Sitting in the Mosque

Dost shows that each act disputed by the Supreme Assembly has a source in the divine realities (p. 18). God described Himself with the verb “to hesitate”: “I have not hesitated in anything I did”; in creatures this verb arises from the power of reflection, and its counterpart in the angels is their conflict over this matter. The source of the dispute about carrying the feet and hastening to congregations is these hadiths: “Whoever draws near to Me by a handspan, I draw near to him by a cubit… Whoever comes to Me walking, I go to him running”; “Whoever remembers Me in an assembly, I remember him in an assembly better than it”; “Our Lord descends to the nearest heaven” (p. 18). The source of passing water between the fingers in ablution is this hadith: “My believing servant dislikes death, and I do not wish to delay him”; God described Himself with disliking and with reluctance, and the believer's passing the water between his fingers despite the cold, even unwillingly, comes from this reality (p. 18).

The divine source of sitting in the mosque after prayer is the verse “We shall attend to you, O men and jinn”; God says: “Whoever is in the heavens and on earth asks of Him. Every day He is upon some task.” When the servant finishes the prayer and sits in the mosque to remember his Lord, he passes from his intimate discourse in one state to his intimate discourse in another, and from the station of “We shall attend to you” a balance appears in answer to this act (pp. 18-19). Dost closes the chapter by saying that all these acts about which the Supreme Assembly disputed are connected to divine realities, and notes that listing the relations between acts and divine realities one by one would prolong the speech (p. 19).

One Voice, Seventy Letters: The Descent of the Divine Command

Chapter 307 (bearing a compound title — angels descending from the Muhammadan-Mosaic rank to the Muhammadan Station, the Station of the Angels' Descent) offers a cosmological schema of descent: the divine command, descending from the Name through Intellect, Soul, the Throne, the Footstool, the Lote Tree of the Farthest Boundary, the seven heavens, and finally to the earth, changes by "taking on the form" of each rank it passes through. The author makes this idea concrete through an analogy: "we may liken the state of the divine command to the voice that emerges from the chest, out through the mouth, as a single entity. At this point, no plurality whatsoever is seen in the voice. Then, the points of articulation divide the voice into more than seventy letters. Yet the voice is the same voice" (pp. 20-21).

Ascents from Heaven to Earth: Footstool, Throne, Soul and Intellect

Chapter 307 begins with this explanation: “God has set up ascents (and stairways) from heaven to earth equal in number to His creatures. Every footplace in heaven is filled with an angel glorifying God” (p. 20). There are as many angels on earth; those of the earth never rise to heaven and those of heaven never descend to earth: “Each knows its prayer and glorification.” God has entrusted to spirits He has made ready from among the honored angels the commands He revealed to the heavens, and these commands contain what God wishes to apply in the world of the elements (p. 20). Dost gives the order of the ascents: from the Footstool to the heavens descend affairs of the distinguishing (furqani) kind; from the Throne to the Footstool there are angels descending with an undivided word, and when the word reaches the Footstool it is divided to the measure of what the Merciful wishes to apply from that single word in the world of creation and command. God brings forth from the (universal) Soul to the Throne fine ties (raqa'iq), and these divide into two groups belonging to the Soul's two powers: “The Soul is the Preserved Tablet, and it has two aspects” (p. 20).

The Soul, being the Tablet, turns to the Intellect, being the Pen, and receives knowledge from it; the Intellect in turn conveys knowledge to the Soul by a necessary orientation with no will of its own. The Intellect is necessarily in need of God, and God has an essential aid to the Intellect arising from a willed theophany; in this theophany the Intellect learns detailed knowledge, and “This knowledge increases its poverty and adds incapacity to its incapacity” (p. 20). The divine command, which earlier was in the form of the names, appears in the Intellect's turning to the Soul in the form of the Intellect: “Thus the divine command takes on different forms according to the rank in which it settles and descends, and takes on a color in every way-station” (p. 20). It continues to descend in the Soul's fine ties in the form of the Soul; the fine ties that long and yearn from the Throne receive the command, and it takes on a form belonging to the Throne at the Throne, then ascends by the hands of angels toward the Footstool (pp. 20-21). In the world of creation it is a single thing without division: “This is the first unity of the created. Thus it is divided in respect of command, yet one in respect of creation” (p. 21).

From the Footstool to the Lote Tree and the Seven Heavens: Rivers, the Frequented House and Trusts

Dost relates that the divine command descends from the Footstool to the Lote Tree; if it turns to the world of Paradise it does not leave its place and shows its authority in Paradise in places such as the houris, the trees or the boys; if it descends to the heavens, the angels of the station from which it descended and the strongest light of the stars descend with it and are received by the angels of the Lote Tree, who give them the deeds of believers rising from the earth (p. 21). Of the Lote Tree he says: “The Lote Tree is a tree of light, and the realities of the lofty trees of Paradise and the lowly trees of the earth reach it. As for its roots, it is the zaqqum, while the branches of its roots are all the bitter and poisonous trees in the world of elements. All the sweet and beautiful plants in the world and in Paradise likewise emerge from the Lote Tree” (p. 21).

At the first heaven its inhabitants receive the command saying “welcome”; the spirits of prophets and creatures whose souls were taken in death, the angels created from the aspirations of the gnostics, and the river of life flowing toward Paradise are there. In every divine command there is a trust: the command throws the trust into the river of life and the river carries it to the gardens of Paradise; for the Nile and the Euphrates it leaves the trust God set for them, and its blessing reaches the earth (p. 22). The command enters the Frequented House (al-Bayt al-Ma'mur): “Seventy thousand angels come. These are angels who enter that river every day and never return”; Gabriel dives into the river of life once a day and flaps his wings, from this flapping seventy thousand drops arise, and God creates an angel from each drop (p. 22). Then the command descends to the second heaven and is colored by the form of that heaven's ascent; the spirits of the zodiacal signs and fixed stars accompany it; thus it reaches the seventh heaven, the nearest, and delivers the trusts it brought to those there (pp. 22-23). Dost states: “Thus the divine command is a sum of all the forms it passed through on its way of descent” (p. 23).

The Divine Command's Theophany in Hearts: Thoughts, Messengers and the Three-Year Span

The divine command at last reaches the earth and manifests itself to the hearts of creatures: “Hearts too accept it, according to their capacities. The acceptance of hearts is of many kinds. The thoughts and notions that people find in their hearts arise (from this acceptance)”; people work, desire and move according to these thoughts, and this movement may be obedience, sin or the permitted (p. 23). Dost extends this: mineral, plant, animal, human being, an angel of the earth or an angel of heaven, all movements of the beings of the world come to pass from this theophany descending to the earth; “People find in their hearts thoughts whose source they cannot know; this is their source” (p. 23). When the command, having applied its ruling, wishes to return, its messengers bring it the forms that appeared in those to whom they were sent, and the command, clothed in those forms, ugly or beautiful, goes back by the path it came until it stands before its Lord as a divine name appearing with all the forms (pp. 23-24).

Dost describes how the people of God relate to this descent: the verifier witnesses the descent of the command and, when it leaves the nearest heaven, “its wandering in the air for three years in order to descend,” after which the command appears on earth; every event on earth comes to pass with the completion of a three-year span from the command's descent from heaven at each moment. Most of the people of unveiling see hidden news in this way before it happens and report the events of the coming year (p. 24). The astrologer is right if he knows how to take the effects from the movements; the soothsayer and the diviner likewise report something known before it occurs from this source. The gnostics know that in every existent God has a particular face belonging to that existent: “Thus the gnostics look at things with the eye of the Real and are not mistaken” (pp. 24-25). While the soothsayer and the diviner know especially the events that will occur in the world of the elements, the gnostic learns from the divine command the divine secrets found in all beings, from the first intellect to the earth (p. 25).

The gnostic puts on garments of courtesy from the divine command, and in receiving knowledge from it gains presence before the Real, awe and light, so much so that the angels of the high heavens are astonished before him. God boasts of him to His angels: “This servant has been placed, by your measure, in a low and lowly place. Yet the place has not affected him, his rank has not ruled over him, and the multitude of veils has not veiled him from Me” (p. 25). The angels say, “O God! We glorify You.” Dost says the owner of such a heart can only be one of the Singles (Afrad) among the people of God, that al-Khidr is among them, and that they are on the drinking-place of Muhammad (p. 25).

Gathering Opposites in a Single Body: The Theory of Temperament

Chapter 308 (Muhammadan, Knowing the Mixing of the Universal World) turns to the theory of temperament after discussing the nature of the human being (subtle substance/body/the sum of both). It relates how the four elements (hot-cold, moist-dry) are gathered through "neighboring" relations: "God wished to gather all of them, despite the opposition they contain, within a single body; for this He added heat to dryness, creating from the two of them bitter bile; He brought heat and moisture together, from which blood came into being, making it a neighbor to the other two... so abandon neither of the two!" (pp. 29-30).

The Human Being's Nobility: Essential, or Attained Through Rank

Before Chapter 308's discussion of temperament, the question of whether the human being's nobility is essential (inherent) or attained through rank is treated; the author favors the view that nobility is not essential but is attained through rank.

The prose of Chapter 308 opens by reporting the disagreement about the nature of the human being: one group said he is the “subtle substance” (latifa), another the “body,” another “the sum of the two,” and Dost says “this is the correct view”: “The word ‘human being’ is a name given to the meaning each group expresses” (p. 27). Those who hold that nobility is essential rely on God's having created the human being with His two hands and on the saying “God created the human being according to His form.” The opposing view is: “Had the human being been valuable by his essence, when we saw his essence we should have learned his value too. The reality is not so” (pp. 27-28); nor would the great and valuable human being be superior to others through knowledge and character, since the definition of human being unites both. Dost gives the conclusion: “So what is noble is the rank. One described by this rank gains nobility indirectly”, like reaching the ranks of messengerhood, prophethood, caliphate and kingship (p. 28).

Dost ties the source of nobility to God: “Therefore, unless God makes him noble, no created being is by his essence superior to another. The highest station and rank before God is God's abasing His servant by showing him his servanthood” (p. 28), and says this is found in the verses “I have chosen you for Myself” and “Glory be to Him who made His servant travel by night.” He adds that there is no nobility higher than a craft's attribution to its maker, and that a created being's nobility emerges from the particular face that belongs to him from the Real (p. 28). The final form in which the human being appears is Adam's form, and “there is no form below it”; the felicitous will be created with another creation that admits no pain, sickness or sorrow, the people of Paradise do not age, do not relieve themselves, do not fall ill, are not hungry or thirsty; the people of Hell are in the opposite condition, and the great sinners are taken out of Hell and plunged into the river of life (pp. 28-29).

The Path of Guidance, the Bridge over Hell and the Mixture in Action

Dost tells the reader: “Know that the path you walk on, on which God keeps your feet firm until He brings you to Paradise, is the path of guidance, and you built that path for yourself in the world from your outward and inward good deeds” (p. 29). In worldly life this path has no sensibly perceived form; on the Day of Resurrection a sensibly perceived bridge is drawn over Hell, beginning at the place of standing and ending at the gate of Paradise. When you see it, you understand that the path is a structure you built; this bridge, as the shadow of the reality of the body, has three branches (p. 29). The perfect human being hastens the coming of his resurrection at the place where his death will benefit him and his repentance will be accepted, that is, in worldly life; for in the resurrection of the Hereafter action does not benefit, and it is the place where the recompense for actions done earlier in the world is given (p. 29). Dost says that action in the world is also mixed with elements that contradict the Real's pleasure, and that this is equal to the creation of natural bodies, and thus passes to the discussion of temperament (pp. 29-30).

The Four Humors: Bile, Blood, Phlegm and Black Bile; The Body as the Mount of the Subtle Substance

Dost continues the account of temperament: by bringing together heat and moisture He created blood, by uniting cold and moisture phlegm, and by uniting cold and dryness bitter black (black bile); in each, one opposing quality was placed after its neighbor, and none was set in such a way that an increase in quantity would cause sickness (p. 30). Dost states the wisdom: “Look at God's wisdom in creation! This wisdom has the aim of keeping the body healthy. The body in question is the mount of the subtle substance (latifa), so that it may reach its Lord's summons” (p. 30). He also notes that sickness keeps the body from attending to the knowledge for which the Lord has made the human being responsible and from the deeds that lead to felicity (p. 30).

Righteous Action, 'Illiyyun, Reward and Light

Dost describes the divine spirit's coming to rule over the bodily mount as a pregnancy: “The divine spirit rules over this bodily mount, and when it envelops it the body becomes pregnant. As a result, certain good (righteous) or bad deeds come forth”; the one who brings forth righteous action is called “muhallaq (fully created)” and the other “ghayr muhallaq (incompletely created)” (p. 30). If the deeds are righteous, the mounts raise the human being to the degree of 'Illiyyun: “The good word rises to Him” and “Righteous action raises it”; if they are bad and corrupt, they drop him to the lowest of the low: “Then We cast him down to the lowest of the low” (p. 31). Dost distinguishes reward from light: “Reward (ajr) is what has been earned. If God gives a recompense beyond what is earned, it is not called ‘ajr.’ Such a thing is light and gift”; in the verse “They have their reward and their light,” the reward is what they earned and the light is God's favor. Hence there is no reward unmixed with light (p. 31).

Dost also gives the basis for this in creation: had the natural body, composed of elements and appearing with its sensory spirit, been left independent, “its claim would have destroyed it”; God gave it a divine spirit from the breath of the Merciful, and the human being's subtle substance, appearing as a light, was charged with governing the animal body. For this reason lights are joined to rewards so that divine favor may accompany the servant everywhere, and the world is “the place and way-station of mixture” (p. 31). Among the sciences of the way-station he also counts the knowledge of the letters of meaning (not letters of the alphabet); taking the entry of “min” into the particle “'an” as an example, Dost asks whether one letter can act on another, and about the particles “ma” and “in” being counted as redundant in verse examples (pp. 31-32).

Three Classes of God's People: The Devout, the Sufis, the Malamis

Chapter 309 (Muhammadan, Knowing the Station of the Malamis) divides God's people into three classes: (1) the devout, occupied only with their own deeds, possessing no knowledge of states or stations; (2) the Sufis, who see that acts belong to God and do not conceal their miracles, a group that may fall into pride; (3) the true Malamis, indistinguishable from ordinary people in any behavior, concealing their states entirely — the highest group, among whom Salman al-Farisi is counted. The origin of the name "malamah" (blame) and its exact opposition to Pharaoh's claim to "greatness" is explained: "they are God's elect, who see that their Lord is veiled from His creatures in this world. Since their Lord is veiled, they too are veiled and concealed from creatures... thus their place in this world is unknown" (pp. 36-37).

Malamiyya: The Station of the Prophet and Abu Bakr

Chapter 309 opens with the sentence: “Malamiyya is the station of the Prophet and of Abu Bakr” (p. 33). Among the masters who reached this station Dost counts Hamdun al-Qassar, Abu Sa'id al-Kharraz and Abu Yazid al-Bistami; among those of his own time he names Abd al-Qadir al-Jili (Jilani), Ibn Ja'dun al-Hannawi, Abdallah ibn Tahmest and Abu'l-Abbas al-Hassar, adding that there are “people the pages of the book would not suffice to name” (p. 33). He then moves to the definition of three groups: the people of God are three groups, with no fourth (p. 34).

The Three Groups in Detail: “Who Am I?” When Asked for Prayer, Causes and Need

The first group are those in whom praised acts such as asceticism and turning to God prevail, who have purified their insides of all the bad qualities the Lawgiver blames; just as they see no act above their own, they also have no knowledge of states, stations, knowledge from God's own presence, secrets or unveilings; they are called the “last ones” (servants, 'ubbad). When someone comes to ask one of them for prayer he says “Who am I that I should pray for you? What is my rank?”; the reason for these words is their wish to keep away from self-admiration and their fear of the self's tricks (pp. 34-35). If one of them is occupied with reading, the book he will read is, for example, al-Muhasibi's al-Ri'aya (p. 35). The second class is above this group: they see that all acts belong to God, and showing-off has been wholly erased from them; when something the people of the path guard against is asked of them they say “If you are truthful, do you pray to anyone other than God?” or recite the verse “Say: God! Then leave them” (p. 35). Dost relates that this group also sees that above the state they hold there are states, stations, knowledges, secrets, unveilings and miracles, that their aspiration turns to obtaining them, and that when they reach one they appear among ordinary people with miracles (p. 35).

The third class are those who add supererogatory acts to the five daily prayers: “They are not set apart from the believers who perform God's obligations by any state that they themselves know. They walk in the streets and talk with people” (p. 35). They have no desire for leadership, because lordship has taken hold of their hearts; God teaches them the ranks and the acts and states that deserve those ranks, and they treat each rank according to its requirement. They put causes in their places and know the wisdom of causes (pp. 35-36). On need, Dost says that for them everything is “what is named God,” so they have need of everything and are not needed; “So this group have come to need things, and things have not needed them, for they too are part of things. God needs nothing, while everything needs Him” (p. 36). Dost notes that they see their Master veiled from His creatures in this world, so they too are veiled, and that when the Real discloses Himself in the Hereafter they will be seen (p. 36).

The Two Reasons for the Name Malami and the Difference from the Sufis

Dost describes how servants and Sufis are recognized by ordinary people and how the Malamis differ: servants are known by keeping away from people, by not mixing with them and by withdrawing from care for state and body; Sufis are known by their claims and extraordinary events such as reading thoughts and having prayers answered, and do not shrink from showing a miracle that would make their nearness to God known (pp. 36-37). By contrast, “the Malamis are not set apart from God's creatures by any behavior. They are unrecognized people, and their states are like the states of ordinary people” (p. 37). The name was given them for two reasons: first, their disciples constantly blame themselves before God and see no sincere act in which they could rejoice on their own behalf; rejoicing in an act can come only after the act is accepted, and a disciple cannot know that his act has been accepted. Second, the name is given to the great ones because they conceal their states and their ranks before God (p. 37).

Dost relates that people blame one another because they do not see acts as from God: “Had the veil lifted and they seen that all acts belong to God, the person from whom the act comes would not be criticized, and in the people's eyes all acts would be valuable and beautiful. Had the ranks of this group before God appeared to people, they would have taken them as ‘gods’” (p. 37). Because they are hidden by the veil of habit and custom, they have been given the blame-bearing epithets that ordinary people give one another; this is a particular method, unknown to everyone and belonging to the people of God, and they have no state that sets them apart among ordinary people (p. 37).

The Wise One, the Balance and Not Naming Where Harm Would Result

Dost defines the wise one: among the servants the wise one is he who puts everything in its place; he gives every rightful party his right, does not judge anything according to his aim and desire, and passing desires do not affect him (p. 37). Such a person looks to the balance given him in worldly life: “He never lets drop from his hand the balance set for him in this worldly life. For if he drops the balance, he cannot know the measures. Then he either cheats in weighing or overweighs. Yet God has blamed both” (p. 38). God has appointed a particular state in which to give full weight, which He praised; the wise one, relying on knowledge, may weigh generously in that place, and as long as the balance is in his hand he is not mistaken in any matter regarding God's wisdom about His creatures, and in this state becomes the imam of his time (p. 38).

One thing the balance shows is that the name of God, His Prophet or someone of religious worth before God should not be mentioned in a place where God, the Prophet or those persons would be harmed; as an example Dost gives the mention of the Companions among the Shi'a: such behavior is an invitation to insult the one mentioned, to speak ill of him and subject him to harm in his absence (p. 38). God has described Himself as being harmed, it is said “No one is more patient than God toward harm,” and a sound sacred hadith says: “The son of Adam denied Me, and it did not befit him. He spoke ill of Me, and it did not befit him”; worldly life is the place where repentance and return are accepted, while the Hereafter is not like this (p. 38). He also relates that the Prophet forbade traveling with the Qur'an, that is, the codex, to enemy lands, since unbelievers might show it disrespect. Dost gives the conclusion: “This is the station of the Malami, not of anyone else. Thus the whole Law is the states of the Malami” (pp. 38-39).

The Close: God's Concealment, a Mercy

Chapter 309 closes with an explanation of why God conceals Himself in this world: "for had God shown Himself to human beings in worldly life, the ruling of destiny and decree would have ceased... God's concealment, then, is a mercy directed toward human beings, and a protection for them" (p. 39).

The Divine Root and Its Branch: Grandeur, Miracle and Prodigy

Dost relates that when Aisha was asked about the Prophet's character she said “His character was the Qur'an” and recited the verse “You are of a great character” (p. 39). The divine root on which the Sufis rely is the qualities that are the due of divinity, such as grandeur and loftiness befitting the Real's majesty and station; but one must look at what worldly life requires: servants claiming lordship and contending with God in grandeur are at issue, and Pharaoh said “I am your supreme lord” (p. 39). The Malamis, witnessing this divine principle, say: “We are branches of this root”; for everything that comes to be in the world has a divine support. But not every divine root is a thing for the servant to pride himself on: “For though grandeur is a divine root and principle, when the servant is described by it and makes himself a branch of that root, using it in his interior and depths, he is without doubt one blamed in every respect” (p. 39). The feeling of grandeur may be used in a particular place appointed and permitted for the servant, in a visible form with no spirit, and reverence becomes the praised spirit of that form (p. 39).

Dost says the Sufis hold it obligatory for saints to conceal their miracles, whereas prophets are obliged to show their miracles: “For God gave them the authority to legislate regarding life, property and family, and there must be a proof showing that the ruling established regarding property, life and family belongs to the Lord. For the prophet is of the same kind” (pp. 39-40). As for the saints, who have no authority to legislate or bring a law, God gave them miracles as evidence of their nearness to Him, not for people to learn of it; if a saint shows his miracle to people, he does so out of the pride within him, and such a self is closer to delusion and snare than to miracle (p. 40). Dost calls the Malamis “the superior class,” possessors of the “white hand” in knowing ranks and the conduct a rank deserves, says that Salman al-Farisi was among their most valuable and a Companion of the Prophet in this station, and says: “Malamiyya is a divine station in this world” (p. 40).

The Index of Chapter 309: Wisdom, the Sacrifice of Death, Union and the Vision of God

Dost lists the sciences of the way-station (pp. 40-41): the science of wisdom, of the halting-places (mawaqif), of reckoning, of conjecture, the knowledge of the difference between neglect and delay, and the knowledge of the destiny that determines sins and acts of disobedience. The questions are these: can disobedience be the same as conformity for the human being; if so, does disobedience bring nearness before God because of its being carried out swiftly; is the person near to God veiled by committing a sin? Dost calls this “an important knowledge known to very few of our companions” and says the great philosophers deny this knowledge in general because they do not accept specification and particularization (p. 40). The cause that leads some people to deny the material and spiritual life of the Hereafter is also counted in this way-station; there are two groups: one denies sensory experience in the Hereafter, the other denies the Hereafter in meaning and sense (pp. 40-41).

The index also contains the knowledge of the states of death: what death returns to and what its reality is; the sacrifice of death, its depiction in the world of representation as a radiant ram, where it will be sacrificed, and to whom its life will pass after it is sacrificed. The rank that unites servant and Lord and the theophany that requires the eclipse of the spiritual and sensory stars are also mentioned here; Dost adds: “Those who accept union (ittihad) and indwelling (hulul) arise from here. This place is a rank where feet slip” (p. 41). He notes that he has a treatise on this subject called “al-Isfar 'an Natā'ij al-Asfar.” It is also asked to which divine name people will resort on the Day of Resurrection, whether seeing God is possible in the Hereafter and whether the seeing is with the eye of the head or the heart, and whether the locus of seeing is the reality of the seer or the familiar eye (p. 41). Dost says “this much explanation of this way-station suffices” and closes the chapter with “God speaks the truth and guides to the right path” (p. 41).
