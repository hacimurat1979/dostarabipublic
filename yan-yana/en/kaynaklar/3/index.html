Sources, part 3/8 (D–G)

This section is not a reading summary. It is an attempt to read Dost's words side by side with the findings of physics that do not fit reason; it is our own reading frame. We do not say “the same” or “confirmed”: we only ask, and we say where they part ways. The text is our own rendering of a Turkish original.

  • Dirac 1926 — Dirac, On the theory of quantum mechanics. Proceedings of the Royal Society A 112, 661-677
    In brief, what does it say?

    Dirac derived the same result in the matrix language of quantum mechanics and with antisymmetric wave functions.

    In 1926 Dirac takes Heisenberg's matrix mechanics as a general framework and asks how a system made of identical particles is to be described. When we exchange two identical particles the physical state must not change; this requires that the wave function either remain the same or change sign under the exchange.

  • Dirac 1928 — Dirac, The quantum theory of the electron. Proceedings of the Royal Society A 117, 610-624
    In brief, what does it say?

    Dirac set up a wave equation for the point electron that conforms both to relativity and to the general transformation theory of quantum mechanics.

    The paper begins with a shortcoming. The quantum mechanics of the day, with the electron thought of as a point charge, could not fully describe atomic spectra: the number of observed stable states was twice what the theory gave. Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck had met this doubling by giving the electron a model spinning about its own axis and having a magnetic moment; Pauli had placed this in quantum mechanics. Dirac asks why this model is the one nature chose, and thinks the shortcoming may lie elsewhere: the earlier theories did not conform to relativity.

  • Dressel 2014 — Dressel, Colloquium: Understanding quantum weak values: Basics and applications. Reviews of Modern Physics 86, 307-316
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper is a review written about 25 years after the first definition of the weak value, and it aims to clear up historical confusion. The authors' approach is to introduce the weak value through measurable quantities and through how it is determined in the laboratory.

  • Einstein 1905 — Einstein, Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper. Ann. Phys. 322, 891
    In brief, what does it say?

    If the speed of light is found to be the same for everyone, how do the things that two moving observers call 'at the same time' fit together? In 1905 Einstein set out from two principles: the laws of physics look the same to all observers moving uniformly; the speed of light in a vacuum does not depend on the motion of the source. From this he concluded that simultaneity is not absolute and that length and duration change according to the observer. This is a theory, and it has been tested by many experiments in the following century. What gives one pause is that the 'now' is not single for everyone. What this means is a question of interpretation about the nature of time. (For the details of the electrodynamics part, the article should be consulted.)

  • Einstein 1905 — Einstein, Über einen die Erzeugung und Verwandlung des Lichtes betreffenden heuristischen Gesichtspunkt. Ann. Phys. 322, 132
    In brief, what does it say?

    Einstein sets out to explain the photoelectric effect and related phenomena by supposing that light in vacuum behaves as if made up of shares of energy of a definite size.

    Maxwell's theory describes light as a continuous wave. Matter, on the other hand, consists of separate atoms and electrons. At the start of the paper Einstein recalls the gap between these two descriptions and says that the wave description alone may fall short in phenomena concerning the production and transformation of light. The word 'heuristic' in the title indicates that what is put forward is not a definitive theory but a viewpoint that works.

  • Einstein 1925 — Einstein, Quantentheorie des einatomigen idealen Gases. Zweite Abhandlung. Sitzungsberichte der Preussischen Akademie der Wissenschaften (1925); reprint: Albert Einstein: Akademie-Vorträge, Wiley-VCH 2005, p. 245-257
    In brief, what does it say?

    Einstein applied Bose's counting to a massive ideal gas and predicted that at low temperature the particles could gather in the lowest level.

    Einstein realized that the counting Bose used for light was not peculiar to light and could also be applied to a massive gas. In the works he presented at the Prussian Academy of Sciences in 1924-25, he rederived the properties of an ideal gas by counting the particles as indistinguishable. These works were published in German; the Crossref record we have is of the reprint of this paper by Wiley-VCH in 2005.

  • Einstein 1935 — Einstein, Can quantum-mechanical description of physical reality be considered complete?. Phys. Rev. 47, 777
    In brief, what does it say?

    Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen put this question to quantum theory: is the description the theory gives for a system (the wave function) a complete description of reality? They set a criterion: if we can predict something with certainty without touching it at all, then there is an element of reality that exists in that thing. Then they considered two systems that had interacted with each other. A measurement made on one lets us state with certainty the value of the other, far away. In quantum theory, however, some quantities cannot be definite together. From this the authors concluded: either the theory is incomplete or these quantities do not have reality at the same time; they chose the first, that is, they said the wave function is not a complete description. This is a thought experiment, not a measurement; and years later Bell's theorem and the experiments made the 'local reality' assumption on which this argument rests testable. What gives one pause is that the question of what a system 'is' before it is measured was left open from the very beginning.

  • Englert 1996 — Englert, Fringe visibility and which-way information: an inequality. Phys. Rev. Lett. 77, 2154
    In brief, what does it say?

    In the double-slit experiment, light or a particle can pass through two paths; an interference pattern (light and dark stripes) appears on the screen. The question is this: what happens to the pattern if we try to find out which path the particle took? Englert described this with an inequality, that is, an upper bound: how distinct the stripes are (the clarity of the pattern) and how much of the 'which path' information can be obtained can together reach at most a certain limit. The clearer the pattern, the less path information; as path information increases, the pattern is erased to that degree. This ties the 'wave-particle duality,' formerly stated in words, to a measure. The side that draws attention: the derivation does not rest on the Heisenberg uncertainty principle; so this limit is not a restatement of that principle. This is a theoretical result; experiments have later confirmed this kind of trade-off, but the question 'what is this an expression of' remains open as a matter of interpretation.

  • Everett 1957 — Everett, Relative state formulation of quantum mechanics. Rev. Mod. Phys. 29, 454
    In brief, what does it say?

    Quantum theory says that a system develops over time according to a smooth and definite equation; but at the moment of measurement it adds a separate rule called 'collapse'. Everett does not like this duality. His proposal: let us drop the collapse rule and describe everything, the observer included, with the same equation. Then after the measurement the observer carries not a single result but a separate state associated with each result; no result is 'more real' than the others, each gains meaning relative to the other ('relative state'). This is not an experiment but a proposal for a theory; 'many worlds' is not Everett's own language, this name was given later by others. What gives one pause is that it is an interpretation that cannot be told apart by ordinary experiment, and that what the word 'probability' corresponds to here is still debated. So neither certain nor refuted: one of the interpretations.

  • Facchi 2008 — Facchi, Quantum Zeno dynamics: mathematical and physical aspects. J. Phys. A 41, 493001
    In brief, what does it say?

    If something is observed continuously, does it not change? Quantum theory gives a strange answer: if you measure very often whether a system is still in its initial state, its passage to another state is prevented. This is called the Zeno effect (with reference to Zeno's paradox 'the flying arrow is always at rest somewhere'). This review examines a broader case: if the measurement asks not about a single state of the system but about a set of states, the system does not freeze; it keeps moving within that set and cannot get out of it. For example, if it is asked continuously whether a particle is in a certain region, the particle behaves as if it were in a box with walls in that region. The article treats the mathematics of this and its applications that give the same result by different routes. The effect itself has been shown in theory and in experiments; 'why measurement does this' is a matter of interpretation.

  • Fein 2019 — Fein, Quantum superposition of molecules beyond 25 kDa. Nat. Phys. 15, 1242
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was: is wave-like behavior, that is, superposition and interference, peculiar only to small objects such as electrons and atoms, or does it extend to large molecules as well? The Vienna group's work reports that, with interference experiments on large molecules of many atoms, it demonstrated quantum superposition in a setup where the molecule's mass goes beyond 25,000 atomic mass units (25 kDa). The title says this; we do not give the details and numbers here since we do not have our own summary. The importance of the finding is the demonstration that quantum behavior does not get stuck by itself at a size limit. We do not have the paper's own summary; the generally known framework is given here.

  • Fermi 1926 — Fermi, On the Quantization of the Monoatomic Ideal Gas. Fermi, Rendiconti della Accademia dei Lincei (1926), 'Sulla quantizzazione del gas perfetto monoatomico'; English translation: A. Zannoni, arXiv:cond-mat/9912229

  • Feynman 1949 — Feynman, Space-Time Approach to Quantum Electrodynamics. Physical Review 76, 769-789
    In brief, what does it say?

    Feynman rebuilt QED over spacetime, in the language of the paths particles follow, and gave the rules that simplify the calculations.

    Feynman's paper is another way of presenting QED. In quantum mechanics the probability of a particle going from one point to another is found by adding up the contributions of all possible paths; Feynman applies this to the electromagnetic interaction. Electrons and photons are treated as objects that send and receive one another, like light, in spacetime, and each interaction corresponds by a rule to a definite contribution.

  • Feynman 1965 — Feynman, Richard Feynman. The Character of Physical Law, chapter 6, p. 129: "I think I can safely say that nobody understands quantum mechanics." (Wikiquote entry; second-hand with respect to the book)
    In brief, what does it say?

    This is not an experimental or theoretical paper but a sentence from Feynman's book 'The Character of Physical Law': 'I think I can safely say that nobody understands quantum mechanics.' The book is based on the lectures he gave at Cornell in 1964; we know the sentence not from the book but from the Wikiquote entry, a collection of quotations, so our source is second-hand. Feynman is not saying here that the calculations do not work; quantum theory gives some of the most precise predictions known. What he says is that we have no story that fits our everyday intuition about why these calculations work as they do. This is not a physical finding but an honest statement by a physicist about his own field. Why it matters: even one of the masters of the theory has not said 'I understand'. In this work this sentence stands not as a ground for comparing physics with another door, but only as a statement cited in his own words.

  • Fuchs 2014 — Fuchs, An introduction to QBism with an application to the locality of quantum mechanics. Am. J. Phys. 82, 749
    In brief, what does it say?

    In quantum mechanics, is a 'wave function', a quantum state, directly a state of the world, or is it an expression of the knowledge and expectation of the person who uses it? The interpretation called QBism says the second: a quantum state is an agent's (an observer's) personal belief about probabilities, and a measurement is that agent's gaining of experience. In this paper Fuchs introduces the interpretation and shows an application: he explains why the idea of 'quantum nonlocality', the mysterious connection between two distant measurements, is not a problem in this interpretation. This is a defense of an interpretation, not an experimental result; other interpretations give the same predictions with the same mathematics, and QBism's claims (that it removes paradoxes and pseudo-problems) are disputed. What gives one pause is that it takes seriously the question of where the line between 'reality' and 'knowledge' runs in quantum theory.

  • Gamow 1928 — Gamow, Zur Quantentheorie des Atomkernes. Z. Phys. 51, 204-212
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper that calculates the emission of an alpha particle from an atomic nucleus as the tunneling of the particle through the nucleus’s potential barrier.

    In radioactive alpha decay, half-lives vary over a very wide range: from fractions of a second to billions of years. There was a regular relation (the Geiger–Nuttall relation) between the energy of the emitted alpha particle and the half-life, and it could not be explained. Moreover, the alpha particle’s energy was far below the top of the repulsive electric barrier outside the nucleus; classically the particle would not be expected to get out.

  • Gerlach 1922 — Gerlach, Der experimentelle Nachweis der Richtungsquantelung im Magnetfeld. Z. Phys. 9, 349
    In brief, what does it say?

    In which direction can an atom be turned when it passes through a magnetic field? According to the old physics every direction was possible; that is, the atoms should have spread on the screen like a smear. Some theorists, however, claimed that directions could only have certain values. Gerlach and Stern passed silver atoms through a non-uniform magnetic field and looked at what happened on the screen. Instead of the expected smear, two separate traces appeared: the atoms were as if splitting into only two directions. This is the 'directional quantization' shown by experiment. The real cause of the results (the electron's spin) was not known at that time and was understood a few years later. What gives one pause is that when a measurement is made, discrete rather than continuous results come out. The experimental result is firm; its first interpretation differed from today's explanation. The author's name appears in the source list as Gerlach, but the experiment was done together with Stern.

  • Ghirardi 1986 — Ghirardi, Unified dynamics for microscopic and macroscopic systems. Phys. Rev. D 34, 470
    In brief, what does it say?

    Quantum theory describes a particle as if it could be in several places at once, but in daily life we do not see a table in two places. What happens at the moment of measurement is the theory's most debated question. Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber proposed changing the equation of the theory: let every particle have a chance, at very rare and random times, of spontaneously 'localizing' in one place. For a single particle this is hardly noticed; but for a large body with many particles these localizations multiply one another quickly, so that the body practically stays in a single place and obeys classical physics. The paper sets this up as a model: the microscopic world and large bodies are described with a single dynamics, and no separate rule is needed for measurement. This is a theoretical proposal; it has not been confirmed by experiment, and works that try to narrow its limits through experiments continue. I am not giving detail on the choice of parameters; it is in the paper.

  • Giovannetti 2015 — Giovannetti, Quantum time. Phys. Rev. D 92, 045033
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: how can time be described in quantum theory? In the theory there is no clock that supplies a 'now' from outside for the things being measured. The paper starts from the conditional probability idea put forward earlier by Page and Wootters: time is described by looking at the reading of a clock that is correlated with the rest of the system. The authors give a version of this proposal that withstands the criticisms once leveled at it. What they show is that the model can reproduce the correct statistics of successive measurements made at different moments. This is a theoretical work; not an experiment. What gives one pause is that time can be set up as something arising from relation, instead of being an external parameter.

  • Giustina 2015 — Giustina, Significant-loophole-free test of Bell's theorem with entangled photons. Phys. Rev. Lett. 115, 250401
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: can two particles be bound so specially that when one is measured, the result of the other cannot be explained by something 'predetermined', even though no signal passes between them? The Bell inequality makes this question testable: every account based on local and measurement-independent properties cannot exceed a certain bound, while quantum theory does exceed it. The team produced entangled photon pairs, chose measurement settings quickly and at random at two distant stations, and counted with very sensitive detectors. In old experiments 'loopholes' remained, for example that some photons were never caught; here all the most important holes were closed at the same time. Result: the inequality was exceeded by a margin that is extremely hard to explain by chance. This is an experimental finding. What the finding shows is the collapse of the picture of local and predetermined properties; but what is to be put in its place, that is, the interpretation, is disputed.
