Return, All of You, to God: The Close of Volume XII

pp. 357–393

Site reading summary

The volume's final two chapters move from the teaching that the perfect human is the world's heart and the point's superiority over the circumference, through the Cloud in a cosmological/zodiacal context, through opposites carried together without a change of state, to the great return passage that closes the volume: "return, all of you, to God... return to the being from which you emerged... if God returns, we return."

Read the part

The Perfect Human: The World's Heart, the Point's Superiority

Chapter 361 offers a broad synthesis of the perfect human teaching: Adam's creation by God's two hands, Iblis's refusal to prostrate, the twelve zodiacal signs reflected in twelve human capacities, and their three levels of neighborliness (particular distinction, thing-hood, connection). The title carries "Known from the Muhammadan Rank" (p. 358).

The title of Chapter 361 is "Knowledge of the Station of Sharing with the Real in Decree, from the Muhammadan Rank" (p. 357). Dost takes up God's words to Iblis, "What prevented you from prostrating to what I created with My two hands?": the creation of man with two hands is a particularization and honoring of Adam. He understands the "exalted ones" in "or were you of the exalted?" as spirits beside themselves in God's awe; these are not angels, the angels being the messengers from among these spirits ("a'laka" in Arabic means message and messengership), and all the angels prostrated. Adam is of earth, earth being the lowest of the elements; from this arises "the superiority of the center point of the circle over its circumference," since the point is the principle of the circumference's existence (p. 358).

In the verse "Did they not see that We created for them...", he says the "for them" returns to the perfect humans, who are the aim of the divine address in the world, that creation is ascribed to the divine hands, and that the plural "hands" means all the divine names: "Every hand in the world is God's hand." He notes that two hands are mentioned only in the creation of Adam, and that this hand is "an isthmus between the plural and the single" (pp. 358-359). The perfect human is the heart of the body of the world, which means everything other than God; in the sacred hadith God says "My earth and My heaven did not contain Me, but the heart of My believing servant contained Me." The heart is called "qalb" (that which turns) because it passes from form to form, and by the verse "Every day He is engaged in some affair" (p. 359).

Dost says that neither verse nor hadith mentions that any created being was given the power to say "Be," the exception being the perfect human; this power was exercised once at Tabuk on the Prophet's behalf, when he said "Abu Dharr, be!" and he was (p. 360). He relates a report that a letter from God comes to the people of Paradise: "When I say to a thing 'Be,' it is; and when you say 'Be' to a thing, it will be." He notes that generality is given only to the perfect human, who carries the divine secret, and that though God describes everything as prostrating, He gave none of them power to command and forbid in the world (p. 360). Taking up the verse "There is no group of three but He is the fourth of them," he says the Real, however the number increases, is always the one beyond it, and unlike the companionship of created things, He has no like of His kind (p. 361).

Nature, Soul and the Creation of Man; the Vicegerent

Dost says nature is the shadow of the universal soul, called the Preserved Tablet, that this shadow extended to the essence of all prime matter, and that the whole body appeared as darkness; for this reason the body is likened to a "black rug," while the soul, being lower than the intellect in luminosity, is called "green emerald." "Nothing comes out of non-existence; only forms and accidents appear, through composition and dissolution." (pp. 361-362) In the verse "He directs the affair and details the signs," he explains that directing belongs to measuring and detailing to bringing into being, as the shoemaker measures and cuts the leather, measure being what comes before separating (p. 362). When God created the world He made man's form parallel to the form of the world; the place of the animal human before the perfect human is the same as that of the monkey before the animal human (p. 363).

In detailing creation, he relates that the four elements were created beneath the sphere, that the first celestial motion was the motion of fire, that the jinn were created within this flame, that the stars spread their rays upon the pillars, and that mineral, plant and animal were born in this way (pp. 363-364). God took clayey earth, mixed it with water, kneaded it with His two hands in a manner befitting Him, and left it for a time; the touch of hot air fermented this clay and made it "aged mud" (p. 364). He says animals do not know where their knowledge, such as the spider's and the bee's, comes from, while man knows that he obtains his knowledge by thought and planning: "The sole reason man is called 'man' is this knowledge." The perfect human adds to this the use of the divine names given to the animal human (p. 365).

He says the perfect human is God's shadow among His creatures, from among the creatures, and for this reason a vicegerent. He notes that "vicegerent" and "vicegerency" are two feminine words, the feminine being the locus of generation and creation, and counts it a warning that when God said "I am going to create a vicegerent on earth" He did not say "man" (pp. 365-366). He explains the words "In whatever form He willed He composed you," which come after "O man, what has deceived you about your generous Lord, who created you, shaped you and gave you balance": if He wills He creates you in the form of perfection and makes you His vicegerent in the world; if He wills, in the form of the animal. The perfect human is made of shaping, balance, the breathing of the spirit and the word "Be"; the verse "The likeness of Jesus with God is as the likeness of Adam" likens vicegerent to vicegerent (p. 366).

The Twelve Signs, the Three Ranks of Neighborhood and Taste

Dost relates that in the verse "By the heaven with its constellations" God divided the Atlas sphere into twelve and revealed to each sign its own command. He says that by his nature man belongs to the world of composition, and is also the cream and essence of nature: "Indeed man is the spirit of the world. When he leaves the world, the world remains as dregs." He gives the example of milk losing its worth when its cream is taken out. For the effect of the signs, twelve aptitudes were created in man; the perfect human appears through them (pp. 366-367). Neighborhood occurs in three ranks: the rank of particularization (in the animal human, the realities of the world; in the perfect human, in addition, the allotment of the divine names), the rank of thingness (instruments; the perfect human's instrument is resolve), and the rank of connection and union with the Real (pp. 367-368).

He describes the third rank as the Real's appearing as man's hearing and sight when man passes away from himself through connection, and calls this "taste." He relates from his own experience: while invoking God by the name "Allah" he felt a burning in the senses, "Now He was, and I was not"; he felt the burning on his tongue and invoked for six hours or so, then God ended his tongue, and he invoked Him not by himself but by the presence before him (p. 368). He ties the sacred hadith "I become his hearing, his hand and his tongue" to this state and gives these verses: "Beware! To invoke God with God burns / This is a judgment tried for certain." (p. 368) He adds that this connection occurs without anything like indwelling, encompassing or substitution (p. 369).

The Cloud's Second Face: Returning in a Cosmological Context

In Chapter 361, the concept of the Cloud (Amâ) appears in the context of the sign of Gemini and the nature of air. "This matter accords with the Prophet's statement in the hadith 'the Lord was in the Cloud.' While the word Lord is definite, the Cloud means 'a fine cloud with no air above or below it.'" (pp. 370-371).

The Twelve Effects and the Knowledges of the Chapter

Listing the twelve effects of the signs, in the second he explains that likeness in language does not require sharing in essential traits, and then the scope of the oath: "God may swear only by Himself, and one may swear only by God." He says that in the oaths sworn by the sun, the forenoon, the night and the olive, what is meant is their lord (pp. 369-370). In the fourth effect he takes up the invocation "Allah, Allah": "The name 'Allah' was our invocation and that of the shaykh into whose presence we entered. Among invocations none is more beneficial." (p. 372) In the sixth he says God was able to create Adam without means, yet created him by kneading his clay with His two hands and shaping it, bringing things into being by an order; in the seventh the hadith "Where is God?" and the slave girl's pointing to the sky; in the eighth the verse "Ask Him," that is, asking the one who has tasted about the reality of a thing; in the ninth the verse "I created jinn and humans only to worship Me" (pp. 373-375).

In the ninth effect, Dost says that reading "jinn" as man's inward and "man" as his outward particularizes the verse to man: the hypocrite worships outwardly and not inwardly, the believer in both, the unbeliever in neither (p. 375). In the tenth he notes that the prophets explain to the servants the revelation sent down from God, the explanation being the detailing of what was sent down, not what was sent down itself (p. 376). At the end of the chapter, as usual, what is learned from the station is listed: the cause that requires the believer to remain in bliss; the difference of limit between the verses "Wash your hands up to the elbows" and "Complete the fast until the night"; covenants and trusts; the pleasure given by inherited wealth and by earned wealth; the craftsman's craft; the wisdom of the continuity of torment; and the mujtahid's erring or being right (pp. 376-379).

The Prostration of the Heart, the Prostration of the Face, and Motion

The title of Chapter 362 is "Knowledge of the Prostration of the Heart and of the Face, the Whole and the Part" (p. 380). Dost says this station is called "the station of firm establishment," "the station to which the affair of everything other than God returns" and "the station of immunity." God created the heart from the world of the unseen and the face from the world of the witnessed; the direction He set for the face is the qibla, and for the heart it is Himself: "If the heart prostrates in dependence on unveiling, it never lifts its head from its prostration in this world or the next; if it prostrates without unveiling, it lifts its head." This lifting is called "heedlessness of God"; the state of one who does not lift his head is the state of Abu Bakr (pp. 380-381). He adds that the world is always prostrating, since prostration is an essential attribute of the world, and that one without unveiling supposes he has lifted his head (p. 381).

Dost divides the phenomena into four, motion, rest, conjunction and separation, defines them, and relates the disagreement about who brings motion into being (pp. 381-382). On voluntary motion he recalls the verse "You do not will, except that God wills" and says: "God is the very will of every possessor of will." According to unveiling, the possessor of will is God, and the will the servant finds in himself is none other than the Real's will (p. 382). "There is no rest in the world in any way. The world, in this life and the next, outwardly and inwardly, passes continually from one state to another." He presents the source of motions as divine attributes such as the Real's descent to the nearest heaven every night, His settling on the created Throne, and His being in the Cloud when there was no Throne (pp. 382-383).

Opposites Carried Without a Change of State

In Chapter 362, this statement appears: "we may cite black and white, opposed to each other, yet sharing in being color." (p. 359) Further on, a verse appears: "see how it hides within its opposite / for nothing else appears but it." (p. 383).

Return, All of You, to God: The Volume's Closing Union

Chapter 362 contains the return passage that closes the volume: "God says: 'return, all of you, to God.' That is, return to the being from which you emerged... one can return to God only by means of God, for He is the companion on the journey. If God returns, we return. For returning belongs to the one who holds judgment. Judgment belongs to God, and God returns to His servants so that they too may return." (pp. 384-385) The chapter continues with the verse "the return is to your Lord" (p. 385).

After the verse "Return all together to God," Dost explains how the return takes place among the names: the name al-Mudill bewilders man, and then the name al-Hadi guides; the servant, by repenting, moves from al-Mudill to al-Hadi (p. 385). He answers the question "with whom does it join when it comes together" as taking God as a friend, and relates the sacred hadith "My servant, I was ill and you did not visit Me": "Did you not know that so-and-so, My servant, was ill? You did not visit him; had you visited him, you would have found Me there." (p. 385) Tying separation to the phrase "whoever is hostile to someone on My path," he says the name al-Hadi separates from the names al-Mudill, al-Darr and al-Nafi' (pp. 385-386).

He says the command is limited to four realities, the first, the last, the outward and the inward, so knowledge rests on four things. The one firmly established performs the obligatory acts and the supererogatory ones; the heart has six directions, and each direction has a face in the heart (p. 387). He explains that pronouns are stronger than names, that in the verse "You were the watcher over them" the pronoun goes from hidden to manifest; he relates the saying he saw from Abu Yazid, "My I-ness is your I-ness!", and notes that he has a short treatise on pronouns called Kitab al-Huwa (pp. 387-388). He says motion is the motion of the face, the one who moves lies behind the veil of becoming, and that after relating God's descent to the nearest heaven in the last third of the night, the hadith also gives the result: "Is there anyone repenting? Is there anyone asking?" (pp. 388-389).

Among the knowledges of the station he lists: limited and unlimited supplication, the knowledge of motion and of cause and result, the state of one who speaks supposing he knows what he does not, whether bewilderment brings happiness or wretchedness and the burning the bewildered finds in his inward, the arrangement of proofs, the knowledge of the strange, and that the Real's oneness is established only in His divinity. He recalls the verse "Wherever you turn, there is the face of God" and asks whether turning away from God's face is possible (pp. 390-391). He cites the verse "To Him all things return" and the hadith "These are your deeds, they are returned to you" (p. 391). He relates that a righteous man, when a man complained of his many dependents, told him to find any whose provision was not from God and put him out of the house, and when the man said "the provision of all of them is God's," replied: "Then what is it to you whether they are few or many?" (pp. 391-392). The volume closes with the sentence "The twenty-fourth book has ended" (p. 393).

The Close of Volume XII

Chapter 362 continues with the customary list of "this is known from this station" and closes with the usual formula (p. 393), then comes a single sentence: "The twenty-fourth Sifr has ended." (p. 393) With this sentence Volume XII also closes; the volume consists of two Sifrs.
