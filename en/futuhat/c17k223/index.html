Thirty-Third Sifr: The Stations of the Divine Names

Volume XVII, pp. 13–217

Site reading summary

The Thirty-Third Sifr is the continuation of Chapter 558: it takes up one by one, from al-Wadud to as-Sabur, the last fifty-four of the stations of the divine names that Dost limited to one hundred. Having completed the hundred stations, he says "God is single and loves the single" and adds as the hundred-and-first "The Station of Stations That Gathers the Most Beautiful Names" (p. 191). In this last section the open names and the pronoun names are distinguished; the passage where God says "I was ill and you did not visit me, I was hungry and you did not feed me, I was thirsty and you did not give me water" is also found here.

The Sifr's Structure: Each Name a Distinct Station

This sifr takes up divine names one by one, treating each as a distinct "station." Each station is treated together with the human who is the "abd" (servant/devotee) of that name.

The sifr begins with al-Wadud and ends with as-Sabur; between them are fifty-four stations, which together with the forty-six stations of the Thirty-Second Sifr complete the hundred stations Dost enumerates. Most of the stations are devoted to a single name, while some treat together names of the same root or of close meaning (al-Fard, al-Witr and al-Ahad; al-Wahid and al-Ahad; al-Qadir, al-Qadir and al-Muqtadir; al-Mu'ti and al-Mani'); al-Awwal, al-Akhir, az-Zahir and al-Batin, by contrast, are separate stations. At the end of the sifr Dost says: “We too have mentioned one hundred stations, and by mentioning ‘the station of stations’ we will make the number of stations odd. Thus there will be one hundred and one, for God is single and loves the single.” (p. 191)

From al-Wadud to al-Khalifa

al-Wadud — the Station of the Constancy of Love (pp. 13–18). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Wadud. Dost enumerates four states of love: “Hawa, wudd, hubb and 'ishq!” (p. 13) Hawa is love's first falling into the heart and is derived from the expression meaning “the sliding of a star”; wudd is love becoming steadfast, hubb is purity in love and its passing beyond the lover's will; 'ishq comes from the word meaning ivy (p. 14). “How could the craftsman not love his craft?” (p. 14), he says, and he relates the revelation to a prophet: “Son of Adam! I created things for you and you for Myself.” “This station is the station of concern, affection and continuity.” (p. 14) He then says: “God has not disclosed Himself in the name al-Jamil to any created being other than the human being. As far as I know, He has disclosed Himself with this name in the human being.” (p. 16) Listing lovers such as Qays and Layla, Jamil and Buthayna, he says “All of these are the seats and stopping places of the Real's self-disclosure” (p. 16): “We love God in His places of self-disclosure.” (p. 16)

al-Majid — the Station of Glory (pp. 18–23). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Majid. Dost starts from the hadith that when the one praying says “Master of the Day of Judgment”, the Real says “My servant has glorified Me” (p. 19); he relates that the day of judgment is the day of requital and that this world too is an abode of requital (p. 20). He explains the verse about the Qur'an that “falsehood does not come to it from before it or from behind it” (p. 21) in connection with inspirations and revelations, and asks: “Could there be any greater glory and magnificence than the servant's confessing that his Lord is the Master of the Day of Judgment?” (p. 21) He mentions that some of the people of unveiling said “subhani” and another said “Ana Allah” (p. 22).

al-Hayiyy — the Station of Modesty (pp. 23–25). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Hayiyy or 'Abd al-Mustahyi. Dost mentions the verse “God is not ashamed to strike a parable even of a gnat” and the hadith “Modesty is part of faith” (p. 23). He relates that at the resurrection the Lord confirms the old man who denies his faults and orders him taken to the Garden, and says to the angels: “I was ashamed to call him a liar in the face of his old age.” (p. 24) And he says: “Every station has two faces: one face looks to the Real and one face looks to the servant.” (p. 24)

as-Sakhi — the Station of Generosity (pp. 25–27). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd as-Sakhi. “Sakha' means giving ('ata') in the measure that is needed.” (p. 25) Dost distinguishes the kinds of giving: karam is giving after being asked, jud is giving without being asked, sakha' is giving in the measure of need, and ithar is giving to another what one oneself needs (p. 26). Here he gives an example of a name that may not be given: “God is al-Wahhab, al-Karim, al-Jawad, as-Sakhi, but it cannot be said that He is al-Mu'thir (the one who gives to another what he himself needs).” (p. 26) On perfection he relates the Prophet's saying: “Many men reached perfection, while among women Mary daughter of 'Imran and Asiya the wife of Pharaoh reached perfection.” (p. 27) “In the station (hadra) of generosity there are the scents of the station of wisdom.” (p. 27)

at-Tayyib — the Station of the Good and Pure (pp. 27–30). “The possessor of this station is called 'Abd at-Tayyib; at-Tayyib means the one who separates the ugly from the beautiful.” (p. 28) He says that by this separation God, as at-Tayyib, assigns “good men to good women” and, as al-Hakim, assigns the ugly to the ugly (p. 28). He relates that those who are not qualified by any quality, such as Abu Yazid al-Bistami, seek God encompassing the six directions, and says: “the highest of definitions is absoluteness, which is itself a delimitation.” (p. 29) He cites Abu Madyan's saying “The secret of life has spread through all existents” and adds: “Because the opening of expression was not given to him, there is a deficiency of statement in this expression of Abu Madyan.” (p. 29) He closes the station thus: “at-Tayyib is one of the names that indicate delimitation.” (p. 29)

al-Muhsin — the Station of Excellence (pp. 30–31). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Muhsin, or 'Abd al-Muhsan. Dost relates the definition in the hadith of Gabriel: “Excellence is that you worship God as though you see Him” (p. 30). He distinguishes from this the excellence stated in the verse: “Excellence (as stated in the verse) is that you really see Him in the way He is shown to your soul.” (p. 31) He relates that the servant speaks of his Lord with his state according to what he finds in his belief, and that the Real discloses Himself in the form of belief according to the rank of that state: “The Real, while being all of these, is at the same time behind them all.” (p. 31) And he ends thus: “So ‘the station of excellence’ means seeing and witnessing.” (p. 31)

ad-Dahr — the Station of Time (pp. 31–35). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd ad-Dahr. The station starts from the hadith “Do not curse time (dahr), for God is time”; Dost says that with this saying the Prophet counted dahr as God's He-ness (p. 32). Of those who say “Nothing but time destroys us” he says: “When they said ‘dahr’ they meant time. Thus they were right in using the name and mistaken in the meaning.” (p. 32) “Dahr is an intelligible reality for all (units of) time, and for this reason that reality has been expressed as the presence of dahr.” (p. 32) He relates that dahr holds the ruling of eternity without beginning and without end, and that according to most its ruling appears in endlessness (pp. 32–33). The station describes the marriage arising from night and day being made to enter one another: the one who marries, above, is the sky; the one married, below, is the earth; their union is expressed as “maqalid” (keys/locks), and by this opening what is in the treasuries of generosity comes forth: “And the treasuries of generosity are dahr.” (p. 34)

as-Sahib — the Station of Companionship (pp. 35–40). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd as-Sahib. He mentions the Prophet's words in his travel prayer, “You are the Companion on the journey”, and the verse “He is with you wherever you are” (p. 36). “Moreover, while the world is a perpetual traveller (guest), God is the one who accompanies it and its companion.” (p. 37) He enumerates five rights that a person must observe in every breath on a journey; the fifth is the right of God who accompanies him on the journey (pp. 38–39). Of the people of unveiling and presence he says: “By oath, the gnostic suffers a harsher torment than anyone; at every moment he calls himself to account to fulfil these five rights that God has shown.” (p. 39) He mentions the acknowledgement at the covenant of Alast and says that the veiled scholar fears sins, while the gnostic, because of his witnessing, fears unbelief (pp. 39–40).

al-Khalifa — the Station of Vicegerency (pp. 41–43). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Khalifa. In his travel prayer, after saying “You are the Companion on the journey”, the Prophet said “You are the vicegerent in the family” (p. 41) and named God a vicegerent. Dost states the difference between this vicegerency and agency as follows: “God is vicegerent by His own determination, not by the traveller's acceptance, as in the agency we will speak of later!” (p. 42) He says that Moses' words “Evil is the way you have succeeded me after me” (p. 42) are not addressed to Aaron, whom he had left as his vicegerent.

From al-Jamil to al-Haqq

al-Jamil — the Station of Beauty (pp. 43–47). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Jamil. Dost starts from the hadith “God is beautiful and loves beauty”, found in the Book of Faith of Muslim's Sahih, and mentions the verse “Take your adornment at every place of prayer” (p. 43). He distinguishes beauty and adornment thus: “Beauty is undoubtedly loved for its own sake. Yet when the beauty of adornment is added to it, there is beauty upon beauty like light upon light, and love upon love comes about.” (p. 43) He states what one who loves God for His beauty must know: “His beauty is something seen from the beauty of the world. God created the world in His own form. Thus whoever loves the world for its beauty has in reality loved God.” (p. 44) “So God loved Himself, then loved and willed to see Himself in another.” (p. 44) He describes the awe of beauty thus: “Beauty by its essence inspires awe. The Real, however, is not overawed by anything.” (p. 46) He says that God described Himself as having modesty towards the servant He meets: “So for God modesty takes the place of awe in the created being.” (p. 46)

al-Mus'ir — the Station of Price-Setting (pp. 47–51). “The possessor of this station is called 'Abd al-Mus'ir. This station holds sway over the station of provision.” (p. 47) These are the provisions that are owned and become the object of buying and selling; this station is the station in which their values are determined (p. 47). He relates the verse “Do not strike likenesses for God” and that when the Prophet was asked to fix prices on goods he said “It is God who fixes the price” (p. 48). He treats the prohibition of bidding over one's brother's bid, and marriage as a sale in which the dower is the price (p. 49). He explains the verse “God has bought from the believers their souls and their wealth” by saying that in this sale the seller is the soul (p. 49), and mentions the hadith of Jabir b. 'Abd Allah selling his camel to the Prophet (p. 50).

al-Qarib, al-Aqrab — the Station of Nearness (pp. 51–55). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Aqrab and 'Abd al-Qarib. Dost says that God is al-Qarib because He is nearer to us than the jugular vein, and states: “God is the one who is near through His descent.” (p. 51) As the Prophet reported, God descends from the Throne to the nearest heaven; wherever we are, He is with us (p. 51). He describes nearness through form: “When God expressed His nearness to us in His law, He linked it to His having created us in His form. By creating us in His form He placed us in the abode of likes; and two likes are opposites of one another.” (p. 52) He says that nearness, according to all the intelligent and the learned, gives in general a weariness in which there is no rest, the only exception being those to whom God shows who the doer is (p. 54).

al-Mu'ti — the Station of Giving (pp. 55–59). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Mu'ti. Dost mentions the hadith that charity, as it is given, falls into the hand of the All-Merciful (p. 56), and divides giving into two: “For God, ‘giving’ is divided into two: obligatory giving and the giving of beneficence. God's giving existence to the world is beneficence, while His giving each existent in the world its creation is obligatory.” (p. 57) He relates that even Iblis covets the mercy of beneficence and that the people of the Fire are given blessings there (pp. 57–58), and says this in a verse: “The garden of the people of the Fire is in the fire” (p. 58). He concludes the station thus: “So just as creation is continuous, giving is continuous too!” (p. 59)

ash-Shafi — the Station of Healing (pp. 59–63). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd ash-Shafi. Starting from Abraham's words “When I am ill, it is He who heals me”, he says: “ash-Shafi means the one who removes illnesses and grants aims. For illnesses appear only through the loss of aims.” (p. 60) He compares Abu Bakr's saying “The physician made me ill” with Abraham's words: Abu Bakr's saying is more accurate, but “you see that Abraham's words are more courteous, for no one's courtesy reaches the courtesy of prophethood.” (p. 62) Here he mentions Khidr's words “I wished to damage it” and “your Lord willed that they reach maturity” (p. 62). After an explanation in which he links the order of the caliphate to lifespans, he closes the station thus: “All of these are divine healings. Using them removes the illness of bigotry and the zeal of the Jahiliyya.” (p. 63)

al-Fard, al-Witr, al-Ahad — the Station of Singularity (pp. 63–66). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Fard, 'Abd al-Witr, 'Abd al-Ahad. He mentions the Prophet's saying “God is single (witr) and loves the single” (p. 64) and his doing his affairs in odd numbers. He describes how the one is distinct from the pair: “Accordingly we say that the sixth of five is one; whereas God is not the sixth of six.” (pp. 64–65) He relates the hadith “God has ninety-nine names, one less than a hundred; whoever enumerates them enters the Garden”, and links the Prophet's subtracting “one” (wahid) from the hundred, not “witr” or “fard”, to the fact that there can be partnership in singularity and oddness but not in unicity (p. 65). In a verse he says: “In existence there is only the pair, look! The Lord exists together with the lorded-over” (p. 65).

ar-Rafiq — the Station of Companionship in Gentleness (pp. 66–68). “The possessor of this station is called 'Abd ar-Rafiq, and in what it indicates this name is the brother of as-Sahib.” (p. 67) He relates that when the Prophet was given the choice at the time of his death, the only word heard from him was “the Highest Companion” (p. 67). He describes death thus: “Yet death is not a meeting; it is only the witnessing of ar-Rafiq, whom our eyes had been prevented from seeing.” (p. 67) “Because intimacy, compassion and qualities of the same meaning are found in the companion and in companionship, matters relating to prophethood have been assigned to the name Rafiq.” (p. 68)

al-Ba'ith — the Station of Sending and Raising (pp. 68–73). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Ba'ith. Dost mentions the verses “He it is who sent among the unlettered a messenger from among them” and “God will raise those in the graves” (p. 69), and says: “So sending and raising (ba'th) are continuous in this world, in the hereafter and in the barzakh.” (p. 69) He says that the prophets move not among the common subjects but among the kings, that is, the rational souls (pp. 69–70). He relates that each group adopts a god according to the thought that dominates it: “You will see no one who worships a god that is not created.” (p. 73) He states that the name al-Ba'ith sends thoughts as messengers to the inward and prophets to the outward, and that following the prophet in the outward is a condition (p. 73).

al-Haqq — the Station of the Real (pp. 74–77). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Haqq. Dost says that what is meant in the verse “What is there beyond the Real but misguidance?” is the created being: “Misguidance means bewilderment, while through the Real (and reality) the ruling of misguidance appears.” (p. 74) In a verse he says: “Existence itself is a real light / The existence of the created is a shadow following it” (p. 74). “The Real is existence itself, while creation (the created) has delimited Him by absoluteness.” (p. 74) “So just as knowing God is bewilderment, knowing the creatures is bewilderment too.” (p. 75) He says that thinking about God's essence is forbidden and that thought has been directed towards the creatures (p. 75). Of the forms of self-disclosure: “The form is real with respect to its coming and false with respect to its going.” (p. 76) He asks that the man of God who said “Ana Allah” be excused, for a person acts according to the ruling of the self-disclosure that comes to him (p. 77).

From al-Wakil to al-Qayyum

al-Wakil — the Station of Trusteeship (pp. 78–80). “The possessor of this station is called 'Abd al-Wakil, and through this divine name ownership and possession appear on behalf of the created. For we made God our trustee only so that He would manage the affairs proper to us.” (p. 78) Dost says that it is we who gave God the knowledge about ourselves: “So in the state of fixity we gave God the knowledge about ourselves.” (p. 78) In a verse he says “Do not blame a trustee / Blame the one who appointed him trustee” (p. 79). He relates that the prophet is over us the trustee of the trustee, and says: “So the station of trusteeship is a divine station, and like the spreading of life through the world — for which reason everything in the world is alive — it has spread through the whole world.” (p. 80) “This station is the station of surrender and of entrusting one's affairs.” (p. 80)

al-Qawi — the Station of Strength (pp. 81–85). “This station is a mixed station, and its possessor is called 'Abd al-Qawi.” (p. 81) He says that the strength of the strength we find in ourselves belongs to God, and that we also find weakness in ourselves: “For this reason the strength in us is created strength.” (p. 81) He explains the meaning of the weakness in the verse “He created you from weakness” thus: “We understood that it means not being independent (mustaqill) in creation.” (p. 82) He says that help comes about through being receptive to being created and through preparedness, and so “we understood that the power is not compelling” (p. 82). Speaking of imagination joining the impossible to the senses, he says: “In our view, just as we know nothing in existence easier than joining the impossible to the possible, we know nothing harder than joining the possible to the impossible.” (p. 84)

al-Matin — the Station of Firmness (pp. 85–87). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Matin. Dost defines the firm as one who, in a matter requiring fixity, is not shaken because of his composure and gravity (p. 86). He describes the firmness of God thus: “So God's firmness means His veil, and for this reason He is not known. The Real that the servant's heart has contained is the Real that accepts the effects of doubts.” (p. 86) He says that knowing what is relied upon is not-knowing itself, and relates Abu Bakr's saying: “Incapacity to attain perception is a perception.” (p. 86)

an-Nasir — the Station of Help (pp. 87–91). “The possessor of this station is called 'Abd al-Wali. Wali means helper, and for this reason you may also call him 'Abd an-Nasir.” (p. 87) He says that the light in the verse “God is the friend of those who believe; He brings them out of the darknesses into the light” is the light of witnessing (p. 87). He mentions the verse “Helping the believers is a duty upon Us” and relates that in God's speech the believers divide into two groups: those who believe in God and reject the false deities, and those who believe in falsehood and reject God (p. 88). He says the believer is one who does not turn his back until he wins or is killed: “For this reason, because of the strength of his faith in the Real, no prophet has ever been defeated.” (p. 89)

al-Hamid — the Station of Praise (pp. 91–95). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Hamid. He mentions that this name of the fa'il pattern means both the praised and the praiser, and mentions the banner of praise, the names given to Adam and the praised station (pp. 91–92). He says that the outcome of praising a created being returns, together with the praises, to the one who created it: “So there is none praised but God.” (p. 92) He recounts the day he wrote the station: “On the day I wrote this station in this book, my friend Sayf ad-Din ibn al-Amir al-'Aziz — may God be pleased with him — told me that he had seen the governor of the city beating a man violently.” (p. 92) He relates the hadith: “The phrase al-hamdu lillah (praise belongs to God) fills the scale.” (p. 93) He enumerates three modes of praise; he closes the station with two expressions of praise: “Praise belongs to God who bestows blessings and beneficence” and “Praise belongs to Him in every state” (p. 94).

al-Muhsi — the Station of Enumeration (pp. 95–98). “This station, whose possessor is called 'Abd al-Muhsi, is the station of encompassing or — though not the same — the station that is its brother.” (p. 95) Dost describes the divine register of existence: its head is the Pen, which is the first intellect, and its imam is the Preserved Tablet; the scribes come in the register rank upon rank with their pens, and in the hadith of the Night Journey the Prophet says “I ascended to a place where I heard the scratching of the pens” (p. 95). He distinguishes enumeration from encompassing: “Encompassing holds sway in general over the existent or the non-existent, that is, over everything known, while enumeration can be only of the existent.” (p. 96) “The possibles are infinite, and so the divine names are infinite too.” (p. 96) He says that the individuals of the human species multiplied because of the multiplicity of the divine names, and relates the Prophet's prayer: “O God! I ask You by every name with which You have named Yourself.” (p. 97)

al-Mubdi' and al-Mu'id — the Stations of Originating and Restoring (pp. 98–101). The possessor of the station of al-Mubdi' is called 'Abd al-Mubdi'. “The first rank is the rank of that which is necessary through itself, while the second rank is the rank of that whose existence is necessary through another; and that which is necessary through another is the possible.” (p. 98) He says that the ruling of beginning is continuous in each of the possibles: “So al-Mubdi' originates continually.” (p. 98) The possessor of the station of al-Mu'id is called 'Abd al-Mu'id; Dost says that God renews a thing after its passing by creating its likes: “Thus God is the one who restores, but He does not renew the very same thing that has gone and passed away. Such a thing cannot be, and existence is too vast for such a thing to be!” (p. 99) “So the ruling of restoration is not outside the Real, and its ruling appears in the Real, not in the created creation.” (p. 101)

al-Muhyi and al-Mumit — the Stations of Giving Life and Causing Death (pp. 101–105). The possessor of the station of al-Muhyi is called 'Abd al-Muhyi. Dost says that the life in things is an effusion spread over them from the life of the Real, that everything is alive in the state of fixity, and that had it no life it could not hear the word “Be” (p. 102). al-Hayy is among the names of God, but death is not one of His names: “death is the dismissal of one governor and the appointment of another governor, for the world cannot remain without a governor.” (p. 102) “So — if you have understood — death is not the opposite of life!” (p. 102) The possessor of the station of al-Mumit is called 'Abd al-Mumit: “Death means moving from the abode of this world to the abode of the hereafter; death does not in reality mean life's separating from a thing.” (p. 103) He relates that after the gates are closed death will be brought in the form of a shining ram and sacrificed between the Garden and the Fire: “The sacrifice of death in the hereafter is good news for both groups.” (p. 105)

al-Hayy — the Station of Life (pp. 105–106). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Hayy. Dost says that because “being Self-Subsisting” is one of the inseparable properties of al-Hayy, the two are mentioned together in the verse, and states: “So everything known is alive, for the known (ma'lum) is what gives knowledge to the one who knows it; and this is so even in non-existence.” (p. 106) “al-Hayy is the name through which everything it sees by its essence finds life; nothing remains unaware of Him and everything finds life through Him.” (p. 106)

al-Qayyum — the Station of Self-Subsistence (pp. 106–109). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Qayyum. Dost mentions the verse “God stands over every soul with what it has earned” and says that because everything known is alive, everything bears a quality of standing (p. 107). “Being self-subsisting is, by its essence, a quality spread through everything.” (p. 108) For this reason, he says, God commanded “Stand up for God in worship”, and states: “So one marvels at those who say that ‘being self-subsisting’ is a trait a human being cannot attain!” (p. 108) He relates that the letter alif makes the letters stand while itself not being a letter (p. 108). He reports that on the night he wrote this section he saw in a dream a yellow leaf that came to him from the Real, written on both sides, that he read it by moonlight, and that he records its verse part and forgot its prose part (pp. 108–109).

From al-Wajid to al-Batin

al-Wajid — the Station of Finding, the Station of the Word “Be” (pp. 110–114). Dost titles the station “The Station of Wijdan — Wajd/Wujud — the Station of the Word ‘Be (Kun)’” (p. 110); its possessor is called 'Abd al-Wajid. Through the example of Abu Jahl he relates that the Real did not bring into existence the faith demanded of him, and says: “God is al-Wajid through the word kun (be).” (p. 110) He says that whatever is outside the command “Be” is not of the station of finding (p. 111). He distinguishes the servant's speech from God's speech: “The servant always speaks through God, and in that case the realization of what is demanded is not necessary.” (p. 113) When God speaks without taking the servant as intermediary, that word inevitably comes about (p. 113). “Know that everyone who seeks looks for and seeks something he does not possess; what is at hand is not sought. The Real seeks from the possible its being brought into existence (takwin).” (p. 113)

al-Wahid, al-Ahad — the Station of Unification (pp. 114–117). “The possessor of this station is called 'Abd al-Wahid when the name is meant, and 'Abd al-Ahad when the attribute is meant.” (p. 114) “God is one in His divinity, so the station of God is the one.” (p. 115) He says that the meaning attributed to the essence of the Real is called an attribute by the theologians and a relation by the verifiers, and states his own preference: “The most correct is that they are names, for the law has not used the term attribute or relation concerning God, but has used the term name, saying: ‘To God belong the most beautiful names.’” (p. 116) He says these are names and relations given to intelligible realities that do not exist outwardly: “So the divine essence does not become many through them, for a thing can become many only through what really exists, not through rulings, correlations and relations.” (p. 116) He cites a verse of Abu al-'Atahiya and says in a verse: “There is neither unification nor multiplicity” (p. 117).

as-Samad — the Station of the Everlasting Refuge (pp. 117–121). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd as-Samad. “The station of samadiyya means the station of taking refuge and of relying. In other words, it is the station in which every needy thing relies on a particular thing.” (p. 117) Dost describes the treasuries with God and the things stored in them: “So the poorest of the poor are those things stored in the treasuries.” (p. 118) He turns to the reader: “Know that the treasuries are with God. You too are a treasury and you too are with Him. Yet your heart contains Him.” (p. 119) In this station he also treats placing the sutra in prayer not straight ahead but slightly to the right or left (p. 120).

al-Qadir, al-Qadir, al-Muqtadir — the Station of Power (pp. 121–124). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Qadir, 'Abd al-Qadir and 'Abd al-Muqtadir. “The sole effect of this station is the Real's giving existence to the possibles He wills to exist.” (p. 121) God says “Be” to the thing and hides His power by the word “Be”; the possible hears the command and obeys: “So the first thing that occurs in the possible is obedience to God by hearing and coming into existence.” (p. 121) “Precedence belongs to mercy, and just as mercy has precedence, the obedience of the possible is what comes first and last.” (p. 121) He links the servant's being caught between two inspirations to the ruling of divine hesitation: “Every hesitation in the world is the very hesitation of the Real.” (pp. 122–123) He distinguishes the two names thus: “God is Qadir with respect to the world of command and Muqtadir with respect to the world of creation.” (p. 124) After the example of a commander who has a thief's hand cut off with a knife: “If God creates by means of an instrument He is Muqtadir; when He creates without an instrument He is Qadir.” (p. 124)

al-Muqaddim and al-Mu'akhkhir — the Stations of Bringing Forward and Putting Back (pp. 124–126). The possessor of the station of al-Muqaddim is called 'Abd al-Muqaddim. Dost says that the existence of a giver of preponderance, who brings one possible before the others, is established by proof from this station, and that this proof is stronger than al-Ash'ari's proof from time (p. 125); he mentions the caliphate, prophethood, messengership and command as examples of bringing forward (p. 125). The possessor of the station of al-Mu'akhkhir is called 'Abd al-Mu'akhkhir: “No one has been put back intentionally, just as no one has been brought forward intentionally; every matter that has occurred has occurred indirectly.” (p. 126) “For this reason al-Muqaddim and al-Mu'akhkhir are mentioned as a pair among the most beautiful names.” (p. 126)

al-Awwal — the Station of Firstness (pp. 127–128). “The possessor of this station is called 'Abd al-Awwal, and is sometimes also referred to by the kunya abu al-waqt (father of the moment).” (p. 127) Dost lists the firsts: among humans Adam, among the gardens 'Adn, among spirits the first intellect, among bodies the Throne, among the elements water, among shapes the circle (p. 127). He mentions the first poem attributed to Adam and speaks of a treatise on firsts (awwaliyyat) that he wrote in Malatya or in Mecca (p. 128). “The first house of worship set up for people is the Ka'ba. The first divine name in rank is al-Hayy.” (p. 128)

al-Akhir — the Station of Lastness (pp. 128–131). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Akhir. Dost says that the clearest cause of remaining behind is time (p. 128). On the order of the four caliphs he states his own view: “In my view, the one who became caliph first did not become caliph because he was superior to the others. God knows best, but it is understood that precedence is connected with the appointed terms of life.” (p. 129) With the verse “The whole matter returns to Him” he says that God is the Last just as He is the First: “Between the First and the Last the stations of all the divine names appear.” (p. 130) “So when God is first, the perfect human being is last, for he is in the second rank and is the vicegerent.” (p. 130)

az-Zahir — the Station of Manifestation (pp. 131–135). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd az-Zahir, and his title is “az-Zahir bi-amri'llah” (p. 131). He says that the knowledge this station gives is the manifestation of the rulings of God's most beautiful names and of the rulings of our own realities in the existence of the Real: “God is behind what becomes manifest.” (p. 131) He voices the bewilderment arising from the seer and the seen being one thing: “Who are we? Who is He?” (p. 132) He relates Moses' request to see and the self-disclosure to the mountain: “I do not know whether the mountain was shattered because of seeing the Real or before the seeing.” (p. 132) Of the views he says: “In this matter the whole affair is bewilderment. All of them are true; none of them is false!” (p. 134)

al-Batin — the Station of Inwardness (pp. 135–139). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Batin. “Just as manifestation is proper to Him, inwardness is proper to us. Yet inwardness is also proper to Him.” (p. 135) “Inwardness is a locus in which bringing into being and birth take place, and the things that come forth have come from it.” (p. 136) He explains the verse “Eyes do not perceive Him, and He perceives the eyes”: “God is the eyes themselves, and He has undoubtedly perceived Himself.” (p. 137) He says that of the names al-Latif and al-Khabir in the continuation of the verse, the second points to the knowledge of tasting (p. 138). “Just as the hearts of the gnostics are the tombs of the Real, their bodies are the places where He discloses Himself.” (p. 139) He relates a dream of his: “In a dream I had seen the Prophet dead on the walls of the mosque of Seville.” (p. 139) He says that when he inquired into the site of the mosque he learned it had been taken by force, and understood that what he had seen in the dream was that the Prophet's law had died there (p. 139).

From at-Tawwab to as-Sabur

at-Tawwab — the Station of Repentance (pp. 139–142). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd at-Tawwab; those who repent repent from this station. “The first turning belongs to Him. Then God turns to His servants so that they may turn.” (p. 140) He says that when the servant turns to the Real in repentance, the Real's second turning has acceptance as its aim: “God does not accept the sins of His servants, while He accepts their repentance and acts of obedience, and this is an outcome of His mercy towards His servants.” (p. 140) He says that punishing the repentant is from the name al-Hakim and not punishing from the name ar-Rahim, and links to this the verses saying “Tawwab and Rahim” and “Tawwab and Hakim” (p. 141). In a verse he says “What is the greatest of repentances? Repentance from repentance” (p. 141). “The one of absolute generosity is he who answers evil with good, for one who does good receives good with good.” (p. 142)

al-'Afuw — the Station of Pardon (pp. 143–145). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-'Afuw. “This station resembles the station of majesty in combining two opposites, and this station likewise indicates the little and the much at once.” (p. 143) He relates with a sound hadith that the Real does not tire in giving: “God does not tire until you tire. When you stop, He too stops.” (p. 143) He brings the two meanings together thus: “So God is al-'Afuw with respect to giving little punishment, and al-'Afuw too with respect to making forgiveness and pardon abundant.” (p. 145) He says that God has also commanded us to forgive and pardon those who wrong us (p. 145).

ar-Ra'uf — the Station of Tenderness (pp. 145–148). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd ar-Ra'uf. He says that God described Muhammad as “tender and merciful towards the believers” (p. 146), limited this quality by faith, but did not limit faith to believing in a particular thing: “So this limitation is a limitation within absoluteness.” (p. 146) He shows with verses that the person of faith can believe in the true and in the false (p. 146). “Know that in hearts tenderness resembles attraction and concern.” (p. 146) In this station he treats the prohibition of showing leniency in the penalty for fornication, and that the prescribed penalty is a purification and, for the thief, an example (p. 147).

al-Wali — the Station of Governance and Leadership (pp. 148–152). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Wali and 'Abd al-Waliyy. “'Abd al-Wali means the one who carries out affairs by himself.” (p. 148) He says that if another carries out the affairs by his command, he is not a governor or an imam (p. 148). He distinguishes the wretched and the felicitous thus: “the shaqi, that is, the wretched, means the one whose purification is postponed to the abode of the hereafter, while the sa'id, that is, the felicitous, is the one who is purified in this world.” (p. 149) He mentions that Abraham was told “I have made you an imam for people” and “My covenant does not reach the wrongdoers” (p. 150). He relates something said to him: “I was told: ‘The perfect human being is the one who judges among the divine names.’” (p. 151) He links to this the angels' being commanded to prostrate to Adam (p. 151).

al-Jami' — the Station of Gathering (pp. 152–158). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Jami'. Starting from the verse “who gathers people on a day about which there is no doubt”, he says that God gathers in Himself His knowledge of the world together with His knowledge of Himself (p. 153). From this station appears the genus of genera: the known, then the mentioned, then the thing (p. 153). “So just as multiplicity must be found in the one, the one must be found in multiplicity.” (p. 153) He relates that of God one cannot say “the third of three”, but one can say “the third of two” (p. 154). Comparing unification and multiplicity, he says that God made the world His partner in existence (p. 155), and addresses the one who professes unity: “O professor of unity! Where are you going? Your profession of unity is the proof that you associate. For unification can appear only from one who unifies.” (p. 156) In this station he also mentions the verse “We created everything in pairs” and Adam and Eve (p. 156).

al-Ghani — the Station of Independence (pp. 158–162). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Ghani and 'Abd al-Mughni. He relates that from this station the Prophet said “Wealth is not abundance of goods but wealth of the soul” (p. 158). “Know that the first degree of wealth is to be content with what exists and to be satisfied with it.” (p. 158) The human being “is poor by his essence, for he is possible; and indirectly he is rich, for he is rich because of the form upon which he was created” (p. 159). He says that independence indicates excessive nearness: “And excessive nearness is a veil, like excessive distance.” (p. 159) He explains the story of the blind man in the sura 'Abasa thus: “God had brought the blind man to that gathering, and the reason was to make clear the blindness of the leaders with whom the Prophet was conversing.” (p. 162)

al-Mu'ti and al-Mani' — the Station of Giving and Withholding (pp. 163–167). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Mu'ti; from one aspect he is also called 'Abd al-Mani'. “Know that you are the station of man', that is, of withholding! For divine generosity is unconditioned, while withholding means not accepting.” (p. 165) To the objection that God has also withheld the one who does not know from knowing, he answers: “Knowing God is impossible, so knowledge of Him is not-knowing Him.” (p. 166) He says that each person's knowledge is his share of knowledge about his Lord: “So in existence there is no one withheld from knowledge of God; neither the one who does not know Him nor the one who knows Him is withheld!” (p. 166) “Accordingly there is no void in existence; if there were a void, withholding too would be possible.” (p. 166)

ad-Darr — the Station of Harm (pp. 168–170). “The possessor of this station is called 'Abd ad-Darr (servant of the One who harms). Accordingly God and the perfect human being are co-wives (darra) to one another.” (p. 168) For no one other than the one He created in the divine form has contended with Him over the form: “He is the first to harm, for He harmed Himself.” (p. 168) “Every harm in the world means preventing the attaining of an aim.” (p. 168) He says that the Lawgiver made this world and the hereafter co-wives to one another, and turns to the reader: “You are the hereafter!” (p. 169) He describes the universal reality that brings the Real and creation together as “partnership in the names”: God has named you with the names by which He named Himself; you are knowing and He is knowing, but you are originated in time, and knowledge's relation to you is originated while its relation to Him is eternal (p. 170).

an-Nafi' — the Station of Benefit (pp. 170–171). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd an-Nafi'. He says that the benefit of this station is sometimes only the removal of harm and sometimes a benefit added to the removal of harm: “Benefit means the one who has an aim attaining his aim. An aim is a willing.” (p. 170) He relates that an aim is always concerned with something that does not exist, with respect to ruling or to existence (pp. 170–171).

an-Nur — the Station of Light (pp. 171–174). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd an-Nur. The station opens with a verse: “Light is two: the light of knowledge and of action” (p. 171). He mentions the verse “God is the light of the heavens and the earth” and the hadith of His being the servant's foot: “While a person walks in society with his foot, in reality he walks with his Lord.” (p. 172) “Just as the light of existence drives away the darkness of non-existence, the light of knowledge drives away the darkness of ignorance.” (p. 172) He relates that lights come in degrees, sensory (sun, moon, lamp) and intellectual (knowledge, unveiling) (p. 173). He explains the expression “light upon light” as created light upon the light of the essence, and says: “And the ruling of the light of the law stands above the light of the intellect.” (p. 174)

al-Hadi — the Station of Guidance (pp. 174–180). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Hadi. “God's guidance means explanation and clarification.” (p. 175) He says that this explanation is the revelation the prophets brought from God, not what the intellect explains by proof according to its own claim: “Whoever tries to make his intellect, his speculative thought and his own proof judge over the law has not been sincere with himself.” (p. 175) “So there is no one of lower aspiration than one who knows that there are higher stations and does not desire them.” (p. 177) “God is praised only by His names, and His exaltedness is described by His names. On the other hand, what His names indicate (the meanings) is learned through us.” (p. 178) He says that the world appeared from the names ar-Rahim and al-Barr: “So blessing is existence, while torment is the non-existence of that blessing; torment is not an existing state.” (p. 179) He distinguishes explanatory guidance as testing and enabling guidance as the guidance of the prophets (p. 180).

al-Badi' — the Station of Origination (pp. 180–186). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Badi'. Starting from the verse “The Originator of the heavens and the earth”, he says: “Ibda' means the ‘particular face (wajh khass)’ that is found in each thing and makes it distinct from others.” (p. 181) He mentions the verse “They invented monasticism” and says that created beings are not originators, the possessors of the power of imagination and the mathematicians being set apart from this (pp. 181–182). “In this context the levels of existence are four: existence outside (in entity), existence at the level of the mind, existence at the levels of indication and of utterance!” (p. 182) “Whiteness is one thing in all white things, motion one thing in all moving things; you must understand this!” (p. 184) Through the form seen in a mirror he describes the relation of the Real and the world: “So the form seen is neither your very self nor other than you! The same holds in the relation between the existence of the world and the Real.” (p. 185)

al-Warith — the Station of Inheritance (pp. 186–188). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd al-Warith. He mentions the verse “We shall inherit the earth and those upon it”, and says: “In other words, God is not an heir, but the one who determines the heirs.” (p. 186) He says that a person can become an heir only when the owner of the property dies, while God is heir to both together (p. 187). “So God inherits from us by way of death, while we inherit from Him by declaring His transcendence.” (p. 187)

as-Sabur — the Station of Patience (pp. 188–191). The possessor of the station is called 'Abd as-Sabur. Dost mentions the verse “those who harm God and His messenger” and the hadith “No one is more patient in the face of harm than God” (p. 189); he says that God, though able to punish, granted respite through the name al-Halim. “God has not been patient towards anyone; He has only been patient with Himself, by being patient towards the ruling of one of His names.” (p. 189) He links the skins' saying “God, who makes everything speak, made us speak” to harm appearing through speech and to the one who makes speak being God (p. 189). He relates the sound report: “The son of Adam has been disrespectful to Me, and this did not befit him; the son of Adam has called Me a liar, and doing so did not befit him.” (p. 190)

The Station of Stations That Gathers the Most Beautiful Names: Open Names, Pronoun Names and the Enumeration of the Names

As he finishes the hundred stations after the station of as-Sabur, Dost says this about the names: “Among God's names there are names we know just as there are names we do not know. It is permissible to make some of the names names of God, while it is not permissible to give others to Him as names — because in custom they carry a sense of disrespect; as courtesy before God requires, we have kept silent about this part. Yet some of these names occur in the Noble Qur'an by way of implication.” (p. 191) He also mentions the acts from which no name has been made, and the names of things whose ruling is attributed to God but whose name is not given to Him: in the verse “garments that protect you from the heat” it is God who protects, and the garment is the deputy (p. 191). “Accordingly, everything that is needed is a name of God.” (p. 191)

The last section of the sifr bears the title “The Station of Stations That Gathers the Most Beautiful Names” (pp. 192–217) and opens with the verses “To Him belong the most beautiful names” and “Call upon God or call upon the All-Merciful” (p. 192). In this section Dost divides the names into their kinds, describes the courtesy to be observed regarding acts attributed to God, treats the relation of the names to the possibles, which are God's dominion, and then enumerates the most beautiful names one by one.

Open names and pronoun names (p. 192). Dost divides the names thus: “Know that some of God's names are known names; they are the open names. Some of the names, however, are pronoun names, such as the first-, second- and third-person singular, dual and plural pronouns.” (p. 192) For the plural pronoun he gives as examples the verses “We sent down” and “Truly We”: “Sometimes pronouns such as I, you and he are used.” (p. 192) He defines a third kind thus: “Some names are names indicated by acts from which no open name has appeared.” (p. 192) The examples are the verses “God mocked them” and “God makes a mockery of them”. The fourth kind: “Some names are names of deputyship belonging to God. They have served as deputies of the name Allah.” (p. 192) “Every act attributed to a possible is in reality God's deputy.” (p. 192)

Courtesy in attributing acts, and the ruling of the pronouns (pp. 192–193). He says that a praised act is attributed to God, while an act requiring blame is not: Abraham said “He heals me”, and when illness was in question he said “When I am ill”, not “when He makes me ill” (p. 192). Khidr too attributed the damaging to himself, said “Your Lord willed” in the praised matter concerning the two orphans, and where praise and blame occur together used the plural pronoun, saying “We wished”: “Such is the state of those who have courtesy.” (p. 193) He sets out the ruling of the pronouns thus: “If God uses the plural pronoun of Himself, it refers to the names of plurality, because of the presence in the matter mentioned of the rulings of different and many names. If God uses a pronoun indicating two (the dual), this pronoun goes back to His essence and to the relation belonging to a particular name. When He uses the singular pronoun, it returns to a particular name or to the essence.” (p. 193) He shows the permissible and the impermissible with an example: “Among the specified ones are names such as al-Faliq (the Cleaver) and al-Ja'il (the Maker, the One who brings into being), while the names Mustahzi' and Sakhir (the Mocker, the One who ridicules) are not.” (p. 193) God mocks whichever of His servants He wills and plots against them, “Yet because of all these acts He is not named with a name.” (p. 193)

Dominion and infinity (pp. 194–199). He says that what God's speech attributes to Him in general they attribute summarily, what it attributes in detail they attribute in detail, and they add nothing (p. 194). He mentions Abu Yazid al-Bistami's saying “I have no attribute” (p. 195). “Know that this station that gathers all the stations contains God's dominion. His dominion is nothing other than the possibles, and they are our existences.” (pp. 195–196) He relates the holy hadith: “Were you all upon the heart of the most God-fearing man, that would add nothing to My dominion.” (p. 196) He describes with the example of a ship sunk in the ocean that what enters existence is finite while the fixed entities are infinite (p. 197); he relates the bird in the story of Moses and Khidr: “What my knowledge and your knowledge take away from God's knowledge is as much as the bird takes away from the sea with its beak.” (p. 198) He says that when fire is taken from a lamp with wicks nothing is lessened of the light, and that the same holds for knowledge and learning (p. 199). He says that the formalist scholars know the words and meanings of the names, while “the people of God take on their traits, and thereby the names can be given to them too” (p. 199).

The series of correspondences (pp. 200–204). Dost lists one after another what we did in response to each quality with which God described Himself: “God condescended to us by describing Himself with the qualities of created beings, and we believed and trusted in this word He attributed to Himself.” (p. 200) When God made known that He is in the qibla of the one who prays, we imagined Him thus; when we were commanded to take Him as trustee over our family on a journey, we took Him as trustee (p. 200). The series continues: “When God said that He is in the heart of His sick servant who takes refuge in Him and is in distress, we visited the sick.” (p. 203) We gave water to the thirsty and fed the hungry (p. 203); in this series he also mentions the words of those who give the lie to God and are disrespectful to Him: “by calling Him a liar and being disrespectful we harmed Him.” (p. 204)

The enumeration of the names (pp. 204–216). After saying that God described Himself only with the most beautiful names and set apart what He attributed to Himself by calling it “most beautiful”, Dost enumerates the names, stating for each in what respect it is given to God: “In respect of His He-ness and essence He is Allah; in respect of His mercy that encompasses everything, Rahman; in respect of the mercy He made obligatory upon Himself for His repentant servants, Rahim; in respect of His bringing into existence the welfare of His creatures, Rabb; in respect of His being the owner of the dominion of the heavens and the earth, al-Malik.” (p. 204) The enumeration continues with al-Quddus, as-Salam, al-Mu'min, al-Muhaymin and al-'Aziz; he distinguishes al-Ghaffar, al-Ghafir and al-Ghafur by different respects, and al-'Alim, al-'Alim and al-'Allam by His knowing the things known, Himself and the unseen (pp. 204–205).

The passage comes at a point in this section where the names are listed one after another (p. 208). Dost says that God, having been exalted, then descended (tenezzül), and is with His servants wherever they are in a manner befitting His majesty: “Thus He descends so far as to say to His servant: ‘I was ill and you did not visit Me; I was hungry and you did not feed Me; I was thirsty and you did not give Me water.’” (p. 208) He then adds: “Before His servants God has placed Himself in the rank of His servants, and that is the ruling of this divine name.” (p. 208)

The continuation of the enumeration (pp. 210–216). Of al-Ba'ith: “In this respect al-Ba'ith is one of the most secret of the names the Real has made known to His servants.” (p. 210) Of al-Qawi and al-Matin: “And a manifestation of divine strength is His having created the world of imagination. The joining of opposites appears in the world of imagination.” (p. 211) “So in reality what possesses the (divine) form is imagination.” (p. 212) Of al-Mubdi' he says that everything that appears in the world is created in the second rank and that there is no third rank (p. 213); of al-Mumit: “God causes what He created to die in the second moment, which comes after the moment of its existence.” (p. 213) Meanwhile he relates an event: “While I was writing this chapter I heard a verse being recited from one of the walls of the house. I did not see who was reciting it, but I heard the voice, and I did not know whom it was addressing.” (p. 213) He describes al-Muntaqim's purifying through penalties in this world and hidden punishment: “So much so that the pain a mother suffers in nursing her baby also falls into this category.” (p. 215) He says that al-'Afuw, like al-Jalil, is one of the names with opposite meanings (p. 215); the enumeration ends with al-Badi', ad-Darr and an-Nafi', an-Nur, al-Hadi, al-Baqi, al-Warith, ar-Rashid and as-Sabur (p. 216).

The close of the chapter: a general ruling on the pronouns (pp. 216–217). Dost ends Chapter 558 thus: “In this chapter we have expressed some of the matters contained in the general part, which is ‘the station of stations’. This chapter was the chapter of the names. We will content ourselves with giving a general ruling about the pronouns: in the pronouns that the Prophet transmitted from God or that occur in God's Book, we look at the story and at the pronoun and judge about the pronoun according to what the situation in the story mentioned requires. We add nothing to it and take nothing from it.” (pp. 216–217) “While this subject is a vast chapter, we have limited it, as we said before.” (p. 217) The sifr then closes with the sentence “The Thirty-Third Sifr has ended.” (p. 217); on the same page the Thirty-Fourth Sifr and Chapter 559 begin.
