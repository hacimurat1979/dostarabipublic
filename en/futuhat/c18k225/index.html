Sifr 35 — From Open Shirk to the Greatest Disease

Vol. XVIII, Thirty-Fifth Sifr, pp. 14–175

Site reading summary

Volume XVIII of the Futuhat opens with the Thirty-Fifth Sifr. The sifr is the continuation of Chapter 559 and keeps listing secrets with the same "Among these is …" formula: first with chapter numbers, from the 255th chapter to the 464th, then under unnumbered headings. Towards the end of the sifr (pp. 153–167) four headings come. Under "Open and Hidden Shirk", open shirk is defined as "the shirk that one's act brings about through a means" and hidden shirk as "trusting in the tool and the means in a matter that can be done with a tool", and it is said that "in this respect everyone is a mushrik"; in the same place a second saying is quoted: "What is meant by shirk here is accepting a second god beside God." Then come the headings on immersion in blessings being blindness, on the veils of one who breaks his word about a trust increasing, and on turning away being the greatest disease. The chapter is completed with the end of the thirty-fifth sifr.

Open and Hidden Shirk

Towards the end of the sifr (pp. 153–154) comes an entry headed "Open and Hidden Shirk". The entry, introduced with "He said", reads: "Open shirk is the shirk that one's act brings about through a means, while hidden shirk is trusting in the tool and the means in a matter that can be done with a tool. In this respect everyone is a mushrik. For everyone possesses knowledge, and only knowledge brings about shirk, while the Real demands knowledge." The same entry says: "because they have no knowledge of the shirk they are in, God has not attributed shirk to them." Then a second saying is quoted: "What is meant by shirk here is accepting a second god beside God. Such conduct is utter ignorance, for there is no other god."

The Greatest Disease: Turning Away

The closing theme of the sifr is the most severe disease of hearts: turning away. Ibn Arabī first says that turning away in an absolute sense is impossible — every turning away is an orientation toward something. But there is a specific turning away: turning one's face from the signs God has placed in the universe as His evidence. When this disease takes root, medicine no longer helps. In a poem that follows, Ibn Arabī says: 'When He turns His face I fall ill / Would that I knew who has made me ill like this.'

Unnumbered Entries (7): Remembrance, Proofs, the Imam, the Messiah and the Religion of the Prophets (pp. 166–175)

Dost continues listing the entries: an untitled entry (first line “When the ends are in the self”): “You too must turn away from one who turns away from the remembrance of God” (p. 166); an untitled entry (first line “The remembrance of remembrance is safe from the stratagem”): “The remembrance of remembrance is like ‘the praise of praise’” (p. 167); an untitled entry (first line “Beware! The attribute of the Real appears in the created”); an untitled entry (first line “Proofs are veils, drawn veils”): “One who stays with the proof is deprived of what it points to” (p. 168); “One Who Knows His Deed Is Seen Has Not Worshipped the Created”; “One Who Seeks Forgiveness for His Sin Has Acted on His Knowledge”; “What the One Who Sees the Carpet Encompasses”: “Those seated on the carpet do not take their eyes off the owner of the carpet even for a moment” (p. 170); “The Knowledge Assigned to Particular Sealhood”; “Strong Light as an Obstacle”; “The Rank of the Imam Among the Creatures”: “The malik, that is, the sovereign, is without doubt a mamlūk, that is, a slave” (p. 172).

“The Difference Between the Messiah and a ‘messiah’”: “The Messiah is also one who wipes his own eye” (p. 172); “The Trait of One Who Knows the Names of the Names”; “The Knowledge of Secrets and Lights”: “Lights are the witnessed realm, and the Real is light, and so He is witnessed and seen. Secrets are the unseen, and the He-ness belongs to them” (p. 173); “The Religion of the Prophets Is One, Without Addition, Yet the Laws Differ from One Another and Have a Common Denominator”: “What is meant is to uphold the religion and not to fall into division in it” (p. 174).

After the last entry the text ends: “This much explanation of this chapter is sufficient for those who know it and to whom wisdom and the power of exposition have been given” (p. 175); then: “The chapter was completed by the author’s own hand, with the end of the thirty-fifth sifr of the book. Praise belongs to God” (p. 175).
