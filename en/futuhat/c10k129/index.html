The Camel Through the Eye of the Needle: Bodies Inclined Toward the Circle

pp. 174–217

Site reading summary

Of the four chapters, two are Muhammadan and two Mosaic; after treating the greater passing through the lesser, the human being as God's greatest name, and the unmediated, beyond-reason bestowal that is unlettered knowledge, the final chapter closes with a teaching in Dost's own words: every body God has created is inclined not toward straight but toward circular motion, and the closer each draws to its own root, the more it turns circular.

Read the part

Two Lights, the Greater-Lesser Paradox

Following Part CXXVIII's "half of the inanimate" synthesis, Chapter 286 (Muhammadan, the Station of One Addressed by 'Be' Who Yet Resists and Does Not Come to Be) opens with a distinction between two kinds of light: a "radiant light" that blinds the eye, and a "lightless light" within which the manifestation itself is seen clearly (pp. 174-175). Dost recounts, grounding it in his own treatise Fana fi'l-Mushahada, a witnessing he experienced upon entering this way-station: "In this manifestation I saw the lesser expand so that the greater might enter. In this, the greater did not lose its greatness, nor the lesser its smallness. This is like the camel passing through the eye of a needle, and this entering is — not imaginal, but — sensory" (p. 176). The close follows: the end of the way-station "contains its own beginning" through a witnessing that inverts the servant's initial assumption of being the Real's locus of manifestation — coming to know with certainty that he is not (pp. 176-177).

The Kinds of Secrets, Tastes, and the Clear Proof

In Chapter 286 Dost divides the secrets given to the people of this path: some need no divine permission to be disclosed, while the disclosure of others depends on permission, and "if you disclose these secrets without permission, you will see the consequence." He reports that this happened to him, but that "I met my consequence only in being reproved […] I was not punished." A third kind of secret God gives to no one through an intermediary, and permission to convey it is not granted even when sought: "These secrets consist of tastes, and it is not possible for another to learn them merely through 'expression.'" His examples are longing and yearning, and the pleasure of sexual intercourse, of which the impotent man is deprived of knowledge; he adds: "All knowledge perceived by the senses likewise cannot be conveyed or expressed to another through reason alone, unless it is perceived by the same sense" (p. 176).

In the same chapter Dost divides God's clear proof (bayyina) in His servants into two kinds. The true bayyina is the proof a person carries within himself from his Lord; sometimes the proof is placed with another on a person's behalf, and if he accepts it by imitation, it is not a clear proof for him and brings him no benefit. Imitation may apply to the rulings the prophets brought from God, but "there is no imitation in the proofs and evidences that show the prophet's truthfulness." From this he draws: "A thing can benefit you only if it is within you!"; likewise the true cause of punishment is not its causes but "the presence of pain within you" (p. 177). The proof that comes to the heart from the Real is a traveler and a messenger: "Accept whatever this messenger from the Real accepts, rely on whatever he shows, and negate whatever he negates!" Some of what the messenger gives is settled (stations) and some is not (states); and the people of the stations divide into those freed from the sway of their desire and those not freed (p. 179).

Forms of Deeds, and the Spirit and Body of the Word "Be"

From this way-station, Dost says, the servant builds his deeds as "forms existing in act" and is in act a creator in them: the servant keeps building the form, "until at last he finds it before him, sensibly. He looks at it and rejoices." The form is capital for the servant, and what arises from it is like profit (p. 180). The way-station also yields essential generosity in which the servant has no will: the servant would give his Lord what He wants "even if He had not asked for it (in words)." He explains that the word "Be" contains a spirit and a body: the body comes into being alive from the letters k-w-n; the spirit is divine permission and is like Jesus's blowing — the bird came to life through that bodily blowing in Jesus. Nothing comes into being from the body when it is not joined to the spirit; "an act can never be attributed to the dead, and there is no reasoning in it. The living is not so" (pp. 180-181).

The Attribution of Deeds and the Recompense of Those Who Conceal the Secrets

He describes the attribution of deeds to servants thus: prayer, fasting, and struggle appear in the one who prays, fasts, and fights, and God attributes the act to him and rewards him; "The creating of the acts does not belong to them. Creating belongs to God. See how strange the matter is! And yet this contains a decisive contradiction. But it is necessary to believe in both contradictory paths" (p. 181). He says the way-station also contains the recompense of those who conceal the Real's secrets: they are made trustees of the secrets and do not disclose them without divine permission; "Their recompense is majesty, grandeur, and awe, while in this world it is fear, contraction, and desolation (loneliness), and in the states, effacement." The reward of one who discloses the trust by God's command is to settle, through the name the Lord, in the station of neighborliness with God (p. 182).

Samadani Manifestation: Selfhood and Turning-Back

Chapter 287 (Muhammadan, the Station of Samadani Manifestation) treats the concept of inaba (turning back to the Real) through the example of Abu Yazid al-Bistami, along with the terminology of selfhood/ipseity/identity (ananiyya/anniyya/huwiyya); the number of manifestations is given as 2,008 (2,000 swiftly vanishing + 8 special + 166 abiding). While Sufis dispute whether a deficiency can occur within manifestation itself, Dost states his own view: the deficiency lies not in the manifestation, but in the one perceiving it. The chapter relates two stories: Shaykh Abu Zakariyya al-Bajai's words at the moment of death, "I have lost what I found" (pp. 186-187), and Abd al-Karim al-Wahshi's account, told amid a shipwreck, of a bird created from the utterance "this is the decree of the Mighty, the Powerful" (pp. 191-192) — alongside the teaching of angels being created from words (an angel of repentance, an angel of foul speech).

Inaba, Selfhood, and the Two Kinds of Sending-Down

In Chapter 287 Dost refers the reader to the heart section of his book Mawaqi' al-Nujum for the way-station's own knowledges, and says that inaba means "the gathering of the essence (jam' al-dhat)" and that the knower reaches this station through a true witnessing after the human locus of manifestation (p. 183). Just as grammarians, mathematicians, physicians, theologians, and jurists coined terms for their own sciences, the Sufis use the terms huwiyya, anniyya, and ananiyya, he notes: "Selfhood means the reality from the standpoint of pure oneness (ahadiyya). Ananiyya means the reality of absolute oneness, which is gathering (jam') itself." He divides the way-stations of the unseen into two: those whose effects appear in the world of witnessing, so that one infers them from these effects, and those whose effects do not appear and which are learned only through the Real's informing; the way-station of selfhood belongs to the first (pp. 183-184). He says what is treated in this way-station is not a descent (tanzil) toward the servants who know but a divine sending-down (inzal), and divides it into two: a sending-down "from the unseen to the unseen," without detail, which tells of the place and worth of what He will carry out among His creatures, and one that descends into the servant's heart, "from gathering to gathering," in which the knower gives the detail of what the Real sends down and carries out on behalf of creatures (p. 184).

Divine Informing, Ranks, and Deficiency in Manifestation

Dost gives two reports that this knowledge comes through divine informing: Abu'l-Badr relates that his shaykh Abd al-Qadir said, "When something is added to it, the hadith (sunna) comes to me and informs me of what has been added or newly brought about"; the same is said of the month, Friday, and the day. In the Maghrib, the month of Ramadan would come to Shaykh Abu Ya'za and tell him which deeds were accepted and whose deeds were accepted (pp. 184-185). Saying that creatures stand in rank upon rank according to their kinds — some of the messengers, prophets, verifiers, and knowers excel others — he ties this way-station's excellence to its two thousand and eight manifestations: two thousand vanish swiftly and give no general knowledge, while the hundred and sixty-six are abiding, and "this knowledge is abstract, pure, fixed. It is unshakable and bears no doubt" (p. 185). He reports his companions' disagreement over whether a manifestation can be deficient: in natural forms, a deficient manifestation sometimes occurs in the element of fire, then a second element is added and the elements reach completion in four manifestations; Dost's answer is: "For us it is not so. An increasing or decreasing manifestation is unthinkable here" (p. 185).

The Descent of the Spirit, the Angels, and the Names of Revelation

Dost reports that luminous spirits descend into the knowers' hearts with divine commands, affairs, and goodness, that this descent brings the servant from the distancing veils to the nearest rank, and that "in the end God takes him as a friend, with the removal of intermediaries." When the divine descents leave the heart and the Real has not yet taken charge of its affair, the heart waits in a state like the pause between two halts, like the sleep between sense and imagination; "This state is, for that heart, the station of bewilderment" (p. 186). Relying on the verse "He sends down the Spirit of His command upon whom He wills," he says intermediaries are removed in this way-station: "The revelation sent down is the Spirit itself. The one who casts it is God, and no other! This Spirit is not the angel but the very essence of the angels." The angels do not recognize this Spirit, for it is not of their kind: the angel is a spirit of light, while this is a spirit that is not carried and is not luminous (p. 187). Angels may descend to the messengers; the angel is then "the messenger of the messenger." What is conveyed is called law and revelation; if it belongs to the Real by the ruling of attribute, it is called the Qur'an, the Furqan, the Torah, the Psalms, the Gospel, and the Scrolls; if it belongs to the Real by the ruling of act, it is called hadith, report, opinion, and sunna (p. 187).

Veiled Knowledge, the Pronoun Huwa, and Lifespans

One of the matters the way-station contains is the superiority of veiled knowledge over seen knowledge. Dost divides veiled knowledge into that which contains the forms of words and that which does not; for the first he gives the ambiguous verses as an example, since their knowledge is reached with certainty only through the Real's informing (p. 188). He describes the third-person pronoun (huwa) taking the place of what appears: the pronoun is the spirit of the form, and by the verse "The keys of the unseen are with Him," what is with Him is likewise unknown, like the pronoun. Forms are attributed to the unseen as though it were a place; "When the form is lifted, the unseen is lifted too. For the veil has been removed […] Now there is neither unseen nor witnessed!" (p. 189). On lifespans, he says God created a lifespan for every form in the world, while substances have no term; the lifespan belonging to a substance is its connection to a form. He states that God is "creating continually at every breath," that for some things (accidents) the duration of abiding equals the duration of existence, and that this is the shortest duration in the world; the reason is: "to make the substances need God at every breath" (pp. 189-190).

First Recitation: The Human as God's Greatest Name

Chapter 288 (Mosaic, the Station of First Recitation) examines "gathering" (jam', meaning refinement/adab) as God's first command to His servant, and develops the thesis: "You are His greatest name" (p. 196) — the human being himself is read as God's most comprehensive name. The numerical symbolism of letters (alif-lam-alif) is matched to the stages of human creation (refined essence / sperm-drop / clinging clot). The chapter closes with a grammatical classification of prophetic names (fully declinable / undeclinable / partially declinable) — a classification mapped directly onto differences of rank: fully-disposing names (like Muhammad), restricted names (like Adam), and names in between (like Moses, Jesus, John).

Adab, the Name the Lord, and Glorification

In Chapter 288 Dost opens "gathering" (jam'), God's first command to His servant, with the hadith "God refined me" (addabani), saying "Adab means gathering all goodness": God created the servant in this world as "one who works and collects"; whoever gathers what he was commanded is happy, while the bad servant who betrays the trust, gives it to one unfit for it, and gathers what is forbidden feels great fear at the reckoning-register (pp. 193-194). The finest thing a human being gathers in worldly life is "knowledge of God and taking on the character of His names, standing within the limit his servanthood requires, and fulfilling his Master's right"; its station among the divine names is the Lord (al-Rabb), and in this way-station God has given the letter "ba" great authority, for He made it an intermediary between God and the servant (p. 194). He explains the command "Glorify the name of your Lord, the Most High": "You can declare Him transcendent only by His own names, not by the name of any of His creatures! We learn His names only from Him." By unveiling-knowledge one begins with the names of transcendence, by reason with the names of act; Dost counts his own self as the first object he sees and says he praises the Real by the act in himself. He cites in this context Abu Bakr's saying, "Incapacity to perceive is a perception" (pp. 194-195).

The Greatest Name, Alif-Lam, and the Animal's Knowledge

Dost reports that when Bistami was asked what the greatest name is, he said, "You name the smallest, and I will tell you the greatest," and that in Murcia Shaykh Abu Ahmad Sayyid Ban, asked the same, threw a stone and signaled "you are God's greatest name." His reasoning: "You are the greatest and clearest proof of God"; for God created the human being "in His own form and with His two hands," and did not do so with other beings (pp. 195-196). He explains the likeness of alif and lam through numbers: alif holds the rank of firstness and the number one belongs to it, lam is second in rank and the first of the tens, and three is the first odd number; the stages of human creation are counted as "an essence drawn from clay," the sperm-drop, and the clinging clot, the clot being the third rank, that of oddness (fardiyya), to which multiplicity belongs (p. 196). Dost does not find correct the claim of some of his companions that the "form" is the essence and seven attributes, since the animal too is plainly living, knowing, willing, able, speaking, hearing, and seeing; he says, "The animal was created upon knowledge, and revelation is sent to it," citing the verse "Your Lord inspired the bee" (p. 197).

The First Intellect, Determination, and the Perfect Human

On the First Intellect, Dost says God created it and gave it knowledges that let it excel other beings, but did not state that it was created in the form; when He created the Perfect Human, He gave him the rank of the First Intellect and taught him "what the intellect does not know of the reality concerning the form" (p. 198). He describes the knowledge that joins determination (taqdir) and bringing into being: Huwa wished to see Himself in His perfection, looked at the fixed entities, and "found no one other than the reality of the Perfect Human who could give this rank of selfhood"; none of what was created before the human being — intellect, soul, dust-substance (haba'), body, sphere, element, and the engendered — could give the rank of perfection (p. 199). He explains the name "human" (insan) as derived from intimacy (uns) with the suffix "-an," and notes that while Umran is diptote, insan is triptote because it carries a single cause, and that once it receives definiteness through the name Adam, it is not declined, because of the pattern. He then adds: "Vicegerency is a garment placed upon him, with its hems. When God wishes, He draws this garment off and clothes another with it" (pp. 199-200).

The Garment of Vicegerency; the Names Adam and Muhammad

Dost says the earth is a place of bowing, that the human being was placed there as a deputy, and that if he had been made vicegerent in the heavens he could not have seen his servanthood within his loftiness; and he cites the hadith: "Grandeur is My cloak, and majesty My waist-wrapper. Whoever contends with Me over either of them, I destroy." The servant is "small within the Real's grandeur"; smallness is the servant's cloak, and lowliness his waist-wrapper (p. 200). Perfect vicegerency is sealed with the declinable name Muhammad; names such as Noah, Seth, Shu'ayb, Salih, Hud, and Lot are fully declinable, while Idris, Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Solomon, and David are not; Moses, Jesus, and John are truncated (maqsur) in meaning, not in outward form, and their being truncated is for their protection (pp. 200-201). As for the deficient names, the servant near the Real feels pain when his deficiency appears; "Therefore the Real, out of grace and generosity, gave him intimacy, naming Himself with deficient names and saying: 'He who created you.'" Dost ends the chapter: "But our perfection, together with our incapacity and poverty, lies in the names having no effect in us in any way" (p. 202).

Unlettered Knowledge: Moses-Khidr and Beyond Qualification

Chapter 289 (Mosaic, the Station of Unlettered Knowledge Preceded by No Prior Knowledge — also the opening of the Twentieth Sifr) defines unletteredness not as ignorance but as the reception of divine bestowal directly, independent of rational/speculative intermediaries; the chief proof is the story of Moses and Khidr. A lengthy quotation from al-Ghazali is cited (pp. 208-209): the scale of the jurist and the theologian was given "not to pass judgment upon God, but to pass judgment for God" — some leave the scale at the door, some break it, some carry it in and out with them. Under the subheading "The Root of Divine Knowledge," Abu Yazid's station beyond all qualification is treated: "There is neither evening nor morning. Evening and morning belong to one bound by qualification. I have no qualification" — "like the blessed olive tree, neither of the east nor of the west" (pp. 211-212).

Unletteredness, Khidr's Knowledge, and Insight

In Chapter 289 Dost says that unletteredness does not conflict with memorizing the Qur'an or the prophetic reports; the unlettered is one who does not use the power of speculative inquiry and the judgment of reason to bring out the meanings and secrets within, who does not use the data of rational proof in divine matters nor the data of juristic proofs and analogies in legal rulings. When the heart is freed of the knowledge of these data, "it is unlettered and becomes, without any delay, swiftly receptive to the divine bestowal" (p. 205). In the story of Moses and Khidr he draws attention to God's use of the plural pronoun for Khidr — "a servant of Ours," "We gave him knowledge from Us," "We taught him": it "brought together on his behalf the knowledge of the outward and the inward, of secret and openness, of ruling and wisdom, of reason and (revealed) positive law, of proofs and doubts" (p. 206). The surplus of this knowledge is insight ("I and those who follow me call to God with insight"). The mujtahid abandons tomorrow a ruling he gave today when he sees another proof, whereas the prophet's ruling can be lifted by abrogation, yet "Where is abrogation and where is error?" (p. 207).

The Scale: Leaving It, Breaking It, and Measuring for God

After the saying attributed to al-Ghazali, Dost describes three conditions of the scale: the scholar who leaves his scale at the door is in a better state than one who enters God's presence with the scale, but if his heart is tied to the scale he left, he is deprived of the sought knowledge; "Better in state than this one is the one who breaks his scale. If the scale is of wood, he burns it. If it is of what melts, he melts it" (p. 209). Reporting that Abu Hamid said he stayed forty days astray, Dost notes that this is not the Sufis' state: "For the believer enters God's presence as a believer." The one to whom the opening is granted comes out with the scales and "measures with them for God, not about God. The prophets did likewise." God made the scales many so that each scale would measure what it was set for; when the theologian wants to weigh with the scale of reason what exceeds the power of reason, or the Shafi'i jurist wants to weigh with his own scale the nabidh that Abu Hanifa deemed lawful, the scale rejects it (p. 209). A saying of Abu Yazid is reported: "When you see one who believes the words of the people of this path […] ask him to pray for you. For such a one is among those whose prayer is accepted" (p. 210).

Circularity: Bodies Returning to Their Root

The close of Chapter 289 carries a teaching in Dost's own words: "You will not see any body God has created remaining in straight motion. Every body God has created — the inanimate, the plant, the animal, the sky, the earth, the mountain, the leaf, the stone — is inclined toward circularity... The more bodies bend, the closer they have drawn to their root, which consists of circularity itself" (p. 213).

Bodies Made of Light, Darkness, and Prime Matter

Dost gives, among the matters this way-station contains, the knowledge of how natural bodies were created and that they are made of light: when a person learns how dense bodies become clear, he makes them transparent "because of the luminosity that is their root"; examples are sand purified of its impurities and turned to glass, polished stones, crystal, and precious metals. The reason is that all existents come from God's name "the Light of the heavens" and "the Light of the earth" (p. 212). Without the luminosity in dense bodies, the one who has unveiling could not see "what is behind the wall, or beneath the earth, or above the heavens," some friends of God could not pass through walls, and the dead could not sit up in the grave (pp. 212-213). He also describes the descent of light: the first to exist is the intellect, the Pen; from it God brought into being the soul, that is, the Preserved Tablet; "things go on thickening until they descend to the pillars and the engendered." Each existent has a face toward God, which is light, and a face toward its cause, which is darkness and density (p. 213). The human body is the last engendered thing and is inclined toward circularity; yet in the human being, unlike animals and plants, there is also straight motion (p. 213).

Dost explains the verse "A sign for them is the night; We draw the day out of it": light is hidden in darkness; God did not say "We draw the light out of it," for if light were drawn out of the night, the existence of darkness would vanish. "If darkness had no essential light, there could be no place for day, and nothing perceived"; though perception is assigned to the eye, it truly belongs to everything: "So everything perceives itself and everything" (pp. 213-214). He says the Prophet could perceive from behind as from before, his bones not veiling him, and that "God has given darkness and density a trust"; he adds: "For the lights they hide within them, there is no more trustworthy trustee than dense dark bodies." He recalls the verse "By this secure city": a city is a place with walls, ramparts, earth, mud, and tiles, and God described it as secure (p. 214). He says the first form to receive the first body is circularity, the first body is the sphere, and prime matter (haba') is the first thing in which darkness appears; "every darkness in the world is from the substance of the haba'." In the reasoners' terminology darkness is attributed to nature; for Dost, darkness is nothing other than the unseen (pp. 214-215).

The Close: The Key-and-House and the Scale Analogies

The example of one who finds the keys to the unseen yet does not know which house they open (p. 213) describes the difference between knowing the "keys" of the unseen and knowing the unseen itself.

The Blessed, the Wretched, and Pleasure According to Temperament

After describing how light spreads over things, Dost divides created beings into the blessed and the wretched: all existents acknowledge the existence of their Creator, yet the Creator, as possessor of the absolute unseen, cannot be known in His essence. One who believes the prophets' report, sets his own opinion behind him, and does the deeds the reports require is one who "lends an ear and bears witness"; one who rejects what the prophet announced, through a corrupt interpretation or by denial, "has become wretched through the dark side within him" (pp. 215-216). Punishment arises from divine justice, while the reward of the blessed arises from beneficence and is "unchanging, not altered, and not removed." The people of unveiling differ over whether the punishment applied to the wretched will last forever; Dost says they agree that the wretched will remain there eternally and not leave. A saying is reported from Abu Madyan: "The blessed enter by God's beneficence, and the people of the Fire enter the Fire by God's justice. In the place they enter, they settle according to their deeds and remain according to their intentions" (p. 216).

Dost reports that punishment inflicts pain for a span equal to the length of life spent in associating partners with God in this world, and that "when the span is completed, God creates a pleasure for them in the Fire": had they entered Paradise, they would have felt pain, since it would not suit the temperament on which God created them. The people of the Fire take pleasure in fire, cold, and snake and scorpion stings, as the people of Paradise do in shade, light, and beautiful houris; "The dung beetle has a temperament that is bothered by the scent of roses and takes pleasure in dung", and an angry person feels pain at the smell of musk. His conclusion: "Pleasures follow aptitude, while pains arise from the lack of aptitude" (p. 216). He also relates the words of one of the people of unveiling: those in Hell will come out, its gates will knock against each other, and crickets' grass will grow in it; God creates for Hell a group of His own temperament and fills it with them — as He created fish in water and the mole in the earth. The fish dies when it leaves the water, and land animals and humans perish when drowned in water; there are also creatures that live on both land and sea, and all of this accords with the temperament on which God created them (p. 217).
