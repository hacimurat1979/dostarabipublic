Sources, part 1/8 ('–B)

This section is not a reading summary. It is an attempt to read Dost's words side by side with the findings of physics that do not fit reason; it is our own reading frame. We do not say “the same” or “confirmed”: we only ask, and we say where they part ways. The text is our own rendering of a Turkish original.

  • 't Hooft 1993 — 't Hooft, Dimensional reduction in quantum gravity. arXiv:gr-qc/9310026
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: how can gravitational collapse, that is, black-hole-type events, be reconciled with the principles of quantum mechanics? The author argues that this reconciliation requires that at the Planck scale our world is not 3+1 dimensional. According to him, the observable degrees of freedom can be described as Boolean variables defined on a two-dimensional lattice evolving in time. He says this conclusion is reached resting on little other than 'unitarity', entropy and counting arguments. The author uses cellular automata as examples and argues that this dimensional reduction imposes more constraints than our freedom in model building. This is a theory paper; not an experiment, and the author's own claim. According to him, this is why a fully consistent mathematical model for quantum black holes has not yet been found. What gives one pause is the idea that the information of a world that looks three-dimensional can be held on a two-dimensional surface.

  • Aad 2012 — Aad, Observation of a new particle in the search for the Standard Model Higgs boson with the ATLAS detector at the LHC. Phys. Lett. B 716, 1 (ATLAS Collaboration)
    In brief, what does it say?

    The ATLAS collaboration reported a new boson around 126 GeV at the LHC.

    The last untested piece of the Standard Model was the Higgs boson. ATLAS’s question is whether there is an excess in proton–proton collisions at the LHC that fits this particle.

  • Adam 2021 — Adam, Measurement of e+e- momentum and angular distributions from linearly polarized photon collisions. Phys. Rev. Lett. 127, 052302
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: when particles of light (photons) collide, can matter and its antimatter be born? This process is known as the Breit-Wheeler process. In the experiment, gold nuclei were collided at very high speed; because the nuclei carry a large charge, there are extraordinarily strong electromagnetic fields around them, and these fields can make photons collide with one another. The team observed 6085 electron-positron pairs coming out of these collisions. A marked modulation and orientation were found in the angular distribution of the pairs; the mass distribution came out smooth, and no trace of known intermediary particles was seen. These features agree with quantum electrodynamics calculations that assume linearly polarized photons colliding. So this is an experimental finding; its agreement with theory is the paper's own determination. What gives one pause here is that a pair of matter being born from light in a vacuum softens the boundary between matter and light. The paper also says the finding has consequences for vacuum birefringence and for mapping magnetic fields.

  • Adlam 2021 — Adlam, Foundations of Quantum Mechanics. Cambridge University Press (Elements in the Philosophy of Physics)
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was: although quantum mechanics has been a very successful theory for more than a hundred years, why is it still not understood what it says? This short book by Emily Adlam (from Cambridge's Elements series) introduces the most surprising questions at the foundations of quantum mechanics and presents the main answers given to them, together with the costs each one requires. Topics such as the measurement problem, entanglement and Bell's theorem are taken up one by one. The book does not declare one interpretation correct; it shows how the interpretations answer which questions and what they leave behind.

  • Aharonov 1959 — Aharonov, Significance of Electromagnetic Potentials in the Quantum Theory. Physical Review 115, 485-491
    In brief, what does it say?

    Aharonov and Bohm showed that even in a region where the field is zero, the vector potential can have a measurable effect on the interference pattern of a charged particle.

    The paper considers the following setup: a beam of electrons is split in two, passes on either side of a long, thin coil (a solenoid), and recombines on a screen. There is a magnetic field inside the coil but none outside it; that is, the electrons feel no magnetic force. Even so, the vector potential outside the coil is not zero.

  • Aharonov 1988 — Aharonov, How the result of a measurement of a component of the spin of a spin-1/2 particle can turn out to be 100. Phys. Rev. Lett. 60, 1351
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: when we measure the spin of a particle, the result is 'plus one half' or 'minus one half.' But what comes out if we make the measurement very gentle and select only particles that have certain states at the beginning and at the end? The authors showed that in such a setup the average result can give an unusual value; they called it a 'weak value.' The example in the paper's title says that a value that looks like 100 can come out for a spin component of a spin-one-half particle. For this reason a weak value is not read like an ordinary measurement result; it is a kind of average that a selected ensemble gives under a gentle measurement. We limited the detail in this summary to what is in the paper's raw abstract and title. It is a theoretical definition and calculation; its interpretation (whether this value is 'real') is disputed. What gives one pause is that a measurement result can fall far outside the possible range.

  • Allen 1938 — Allen, Flow of Liquid Helium II. Nature 141, 75
    In brief, what does it say?

    Allen and Misener measured the flow of helium II through narrow capillary tubes and showed that it differs from the usual viscous flow.

    This short paper, which appeared in the same issue as Kapitza’s note, approaches the same phenomenon with an independent experiment. The question is again this: how does liquid helium below the λ-point behave while passing through a narrow tube?

    This summary was written, because we could not reach the source's own abstract, only from the general knowledge we are sure of; it should be checked against the source.

  • Almheiri 2013 — Almheiri, Black holes: complementarity or firewalls?. J. High Energy Phys. 2013(2), 62
    In brief, what does it say?

    Almheiri, Marolf, Polchinski and Sully argued that three widely held assumptions cannot all be true together, and said that the most 'conservative' way out is a firewall at the horizon.

    The paper sets out three statements and says they cannot all be true together. The first is that Hawking radiation is in a pure quantum state, that is, that information is preserved. The second is that the information carried by the radiation comes from the region near the horizon, and that beyond a microscopic distance from the horizon the low-energy effective field theory holds. The third is that an observer falling in sees nothing out of the ordinary at the horizon; this is the equivalence principle that comes from general relativity.

  • Altaie 2009 — Altaie, Re-creation: a possible interpretation of quantum indeterminism. arXiv:0907.3419
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper reviews the main interpretations that try to explain why quantum measurements give uncertain, unpredictable results, and argues that a corner is left open in all of them. The author puts forward his own proposal: physical properties may be continuously re-created. The rate of re-creation is taken as the measure of how determinate measurements are. According to the author, from this standpoint the existence of uncertainties, the meaning of quantities that cannot be measured together, and the origin of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle are better understood. This is not an experiment; it is an interpretation the author proposes himself, that is, contested ground. Relating quantum uncertainty to an idea of 'continuous re-creation' is what makes it interesting in this source list.

  • Anderson 1933 — Anderson, The positive electron. Phys. Rev. 43, 491
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was: among the tracks opened by cosmic rays, is there a light particle other than the electron? Anderson placed a cloud chamber (Wilson chamber), which makes visible the paths of charged particles passing through it, inside a strong magnetic field and took about 1300 photographs. In 15 of them the track belonged to a positively charged particle whose mass could not be as large as the proton's; from energy loss and ionization the charge was found equal to the proton's. The curvatures and the ionization required the mass to be less than twenty times the electron mass. Anderson named these particles positrons and wrote that the tracks appeared in groups together with other tracks, and that they therefore had to be secondary particles ejected from atomic nuclei. The finding gives one pause because it showed that the electron really has an oppositely charged twin.

  • Anderson 1995 — Anderson, Observation of Bose-Einstein Condensation in a Dilute Atomic Vapor. Science 269, 198-201
    In brief, what does it say?

    Anderson and colleagues observed Bose-Einstein condensation in a dilute vapor of rubidium atoms.

    The condensation Einstein predicted around 1925 could not be observed directly for decades. In a dilute atomic gas this requires reaching a very low temperature: the temperature must fall so low that the average distance between atoms and the atom's quantum wavelength are about the same.

  • Arndt 1999 — Arndt, Wave-particle duality of C60 molecules. Nature 401, 680
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was this: is behaving like a wave peculiar only to small things such as electrons and photons, or can a fairly large molecule do it too? The team passed C60 molecules, shaped like a soccer ball and made of sixty carbon atoms, through a thin grating and looked at where they landed on the screen behind. The molecules struck one at a time, at a point, like particles; but when many strikes accumulated, an interference pattern peculiar to waves, that is, light and dark bands, appeared on the screen. This is an experimental finding and admits no dispute. What gives one pause is this: the pattern comes out only when it is calculated as if each molecule had passed 'through more than one path', not as if it had passed through a single hole of the grating. What this means, that is, what the molecule really does, is a matter of interpretation, and theorists are still divided. The paper shows only the existence of the pattern and its agreement with the expected wave calculation.

  • Arute 2019 — Arute, Quantum supremacy using a programmable superconducting processor. Nature 574, 505–510
    In brief, what does it say?

    The Google paper claiming quantum supremacy by random circuit sampling on the 53-qubit superconducting Sycamore processor.

    The paper is presented as an experimental test of the promise that quantum computers can perform certain tasks exponentially faster than classical computers. The difficulty is building a processor that works with high fidelity and can run quantum algorithms in a very large computational space.

  • Aspect 1982 — Aspect, Experimental test of Bell's inequalities using time-varying analyzers. Phys. Rev. Lett. 49, 1804
    In brief, what does it say?

    Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen expected a 'local' nature: two things in separate places should not act on each other instantly. In 1964 John Bell turned this into a testable inequality. Alain Aspect and colleagues measured the polarizations of two photon pairs; by changing the measurement direction very quickly while the photons were in flight, they tried to close off the possibility of communication at the speed of light between the two measurements. The result agreed with the prediction of quantum theory and exceeded Bell's inequality by about five standard deviations. This is an experiment. What gives one pause is this: the relations of two things measured separately cannot be explained by a local 'hidden plan'. What this points to (a non-local influence, or another description of reality) is a matter of interpretation and is disputed.

  • Bailey 1977 — Bailey, Measurements of relativistic time dilation for positive and negative muons in a circular orbit. Nature 268, 301
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was: does a fast-moving clock really run slow? Bailey and colleagues circulated muons in a circular orbit at a speed very close to the speed of light in a muon storage ring at CERN and measured how long they lived until they decayed. Since the muon is a very short-lived particle, it works as a clock on its own. Moving muons lived markedly longer than stationary muons, and the lengthening agreed with the prediction of time dilation in special relativity. The finding gives one pause because it showed that time dilation is not a method of calculation but a measurable fact. We do not have the paper's own summary; the generally known framework is given here.

  • Bardeen 1957 — Bardeen, Theory of Superconductivity. Phys. Rev. 108, 1175
    In brief, what does it say?

    Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer gave the first microscopic theory of superconductivity, starting from electrons.

    Some metals, below a critical temperature, lose electrical resistance entirely and push magnetic fields out of their interior. Up to 1957 these phenomena had been observed, but how electrons established such an order was not known. The question was: how do electrons that repel one another establish a collective state that can flow without friction?

  • Bekenstein 1973 — Bekenstein, Black Holes and Entropy. Phys. Rev. D 7, 2333
    In brief, what does it say?

    Bekenstein argued that the entropy of a black hole is proportional to the area of the event horizon, and that a generalized second law must include this entropy as well.

    Physicists knew that black holes appear to an outside observer through three quantities (mass, rotation, charge). This means that the scattered, detailed information that enters a black hole cannot be read from outside. Bekenstein asked this question: if a hot body is thrown into a black hole and its entropy disappears, is the second law not violated?

  • Bell 1964 — Bell, On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen paradox. Physics 1, 195
    In brief, what does it say?

    Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen had asked in 1935: might quantum theory be incomplete, that is, is there a depth in which things are definite even before measurement, which the theory does not state? Bell made this question testable. Imagine we measure two particles that are tied to each other in distant places. If there is a 'local' hidden order that determines in advance the result of each particle and is not affected from afar, the relation between the two measurement results cannot exceed a certain limit. Bell wrote this limit as an inequality; the predictions of quantum theory exceed this limit at some measurement settings. That is, a theory that explains quantum results with local and predetermined values cannot be constructed. This is a theoretical result; testing experiments were carried out later and matched the quantum prediction. The interpretive side is open: what the result leaves behind (locality, or predetermination) is still debated.

  • Bennett 1982 — Bennett, The thermodynamics of computation—a review. Int. J. Theor. Phys. 21, 905
    In brief, what does it say?

    Bennett surveyed the thermodynamics of computation and set out clearly the view that resolves the Maxwell's demon puzzle through the cost of erasing information.

    This paper presents neither a new experiment nor a quantum model; it is a review. Its question is: what is the least energy a computation must expend?

  • Bennett 1984 — Bennett, Quantum cryptography: Public key distribution and coin tossing. IEEE Int. Conf. on Computers, Systems and Signal Processing, Bangalore, 1984; reprint: Theoretical Computer Science 560 (2014) 7–11
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper proposing secret key distribution with polarized photons (the BB84 protocol) and quantum coin tossing.

    The paper's starting point is this: when digital information is carried with basic quantum systems such as polarized photons, the uncertainty principle gives rise to cryptographic possibilities that do not exist in conventional media. In particular, because measurement disturbs the system, someone listening in on the transmission channel is noticed with high probability.

  • Bennett 1993 — Bennett, Teleporting an unknown quantum state via dual classical and Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen channels. Phys. Rev. Lett. 70, 1895
    In brief, what does it say?

    Teleportation is the sending not of an object itself but of its information. The proposal of Bennett and colleagues works like this: Alice and Bob share in advance an entangled pair of particles. Alice makes a joint measurement on the particle in the unknown state she wants to teleport and on her own particle; this measurement destroys the original state but gives an ordinary result of two bits. Alice tells Bob this result by an ordinary route such as telephone or letter. When Bob applies a correction to the entangled particle he holds, according to the result, he obtains an exact copy of the original state. This is a theoretical proposal; it was later realized in experiments. Nothing travels faster than light, because ordinary news cannot exceed the speed of light; and no copying occurs, because the original is destroyed. What gives one pause is that the information is carried half by entanglement and half by the ordinary channel.

  • Bennett 1996 — Bennett, Concentrating partial entanglement by local operations. Physical Review A 53, 2046–2052
    In brief, what does it say?

    A paper showing that shared pure, partially entangled pairs can be converted by local operations into maximally entangled pairs, and that the rate is determined by entropy.

    The question is this: if two distant observers hold many copies of the same partially entangled particle pair, can they, by applying only local operations to their own particles and talking to each other classically, concentrate this entanglement into a smaller number of maximally entangled (EPR "singlet") pairs?

  • Bennett 1996 — Bennett, Mixed-state entanglement and quantum error correction. Physical Review A 54, 3824–3851
    In brief, what does it say?

    A paper showing that sending a quantum state through a noisy channel is equivalent to distilling maximal entanglement from mixed entangled states.

    This paper examines what can be done when entanglement is mixed with noise. Two ways of protection are compared: entanglement purification (extracting a small number of maximally entangled pairs from the mixed state held by the two parties) and quantum error-correcting codes (sending an arbitrary quantum state through a noisy channel without corruption).

  • Bernstein 1993 — Bernstein, Quantum complexity theory. Proc. 25th Annual ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing (STOC '93), 11–20; full version: SIAM J. Comput. 26 (1997) 1411–1473
    In brief, what does it say?

    A paper that founded the complexity theory of quantum Turing machines and related the class BQP to classical classes.

    The paper examines quantum computation from the standpoint of complexity theory. The first result is that, in Deutsch's quantum Turing machine model, an efficient universal quantum Turing machine exists. The authors note that this construction is much more complicated than its classical counterpart; they show that even simple principles such as looping, branching and composition are laborious in the quantum setting.

  • Berry 1977 — Berry, Level clustering in the regular spectrum. Proceedings of the Royal Society A 356, 375-394
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper that suggests that level spacings in regular (nonchaotic) systems are randomly (Poisson) distributed; it shows the opposite case.

    Berry and Tabor examine systems in which the classical motion is regular. In such an f-dimensional system, each energy level is labeled by f quantum numbers, and these numbers come from the f constants of the classical motion. Levels with very different quantum numbers can fall at similar energies.

  • Berry 1984 — Berry, Quantal phase factors accompanying adiabatic changes. Proceedings of the Royal Society A 392, 45-57
    In brief, what does it say?

    Berry showed that a slowly rotated quantum system acquires a geometric phase in addition to the usual temporal phase.

    The paper considers a system in which the parameters of the Hamiltonian are changed slowly and carried around a cyclic path back to the start. A system that begins in an eigenstate keeps tracking the same eigenstate as long as the slowness condition holds. It is shown that, in addition to the familiar "dynamic" phase that accumulates with time, the system acquires another phase.
