39. The Information Paradox

Main question: As a black hole evaporates, is the information that fell into it lost, or does it come back out in the radiation?

In quantum mechanics, evolution is reversible; information is not destroyed. Hawking radiation, however, appears to depend only on the temperature of the black hole and not to carry what fell into it. If the black hole evaporates completely, is the information lost? This question is unresolved; different approaches are disputed.

Hawking 1976 — Breakdown of predictability in gravitational collapse

Phys. Rev. D 14, 2460 theory

Hawking argued that in the process of black hole evaporation a pure quantum state would turn into a mixed state and predictability would break down.

The paper questions the consequence of the 1974 radiation calculation. If a black hole forms and evaporates completely, the detailed quantum state used at the start becomes unreadable from the final state.

The argument is built as follows: Hawking radiation arises from pairs created near the horizon; one partner of these pairs is inside the black hole and cannot reach the outside observer. For this reason, the radiation going out, examined by itself, is in a thermal, that is, mixed state. When the black hole disappears, the partner inside will vanish too, so a transition has been made from a pure initial state to a mixed final state.

According to the author, this shows that the ordinary unitary evolution of quantum mechanics breaks down in the presence of gravity: the final state cannot be predicted from the initial state.

This result rests on the semiclassical approximation; the quantum nature of gravity is absent from the model. Whether the result is correct depends on how the whole theory works at the level of quantum gravity, and this is still open.

The paper openly argues that the conclusion drawn from Hawking's earlier radiation calculation touches a basic assumption of quantum theory. This is the beginning of the debate known in the following decades as the 'information paradox.'

This summary was written without looking at the paper's own abstract or text, relying on a known account; it should be checked against the source.

Afterwards: In later years most researchers worked in the direction that information is conserved.

Page 1993 — Information in black hole radiation

Phys. Rev. Lett. 71, 3743 theory

Page estimated how fast and in what way information would appear if it comes back in the black hole radiation.

Page's paper begins with a question: if black hole formation and evaporation can be described by an S-matrix, that is, a unitary mapping from the initial state to the final state, then information must come out together with the radiation. When and to what extent does this exit take place?

The author makes an estimate and shows that information may come out so slowly at first, or be so spread out, that in a perturbative analysis, that is, by computing small corrections through the ratio of the Planck mass to the black hole mass, or through a 1/N expansion in two-dimensional dilatonic black holes, this exit may not be visible at all.

This does not mean that information does not come out: perturbation theory cannot capture the slow exit of information. The aspect of the paper most cited in later years is that the exit of information becomes pronounced after a significant part of the radiation has been given off; this is called the 'Page curve' and the 'Page time.'

The limit of the paper is that it is an estimate; it is not a calculation derived from the dynamics of quantum gravity.

This estimate allows views that assume information is conserved to give a concrete quantitative prediction of when information should be expected in the black hole's radiation. The same work is supported by the simultaneous paper that computes the average entropy of a subsystem in random pure states.

This summary was written without looking at the paper's own abstract or text, relying on a known account; it should be checked against the source.

Afterwards: Together with the paper 'Average entropy of a subsystem' published in the same year, it provided a widely used criterion for when information would appear in the radiation.

40. The Firewall Paradox

Main question: Can it be that nothing happens for an observer at the horizon, that information is preserved, and that local field theory holds, all at the same time?

If we assume information is preserved, the radiation of a black hole must be entangled with the earlier radiation. The same radiation, however, is also entangled with a partner inside the horizon. A quantum state cannot be fully entangled with two partners at once. To resolve this contradiction, a 'firewall' at the horizon was proposed; this is a consequence of a contested proposal.

Almheiri 2013 — Black holes: complementarity or firewalls?

J. High Energy Phys. 2013(2), 62 theory

Almheiri, Marolf, Polchinski and Sully argued that three widely held assumptions cannot all be true together, and said that the most 'conservative' way out is a firewall at the horizon.

The paper sets out three statements and says they cannot all be true together. The first is that Hawking radiation is in a pure quantum state, that is, that information is preserved. The second is that the information carried by the radiation comes from the region near the horizon, and that beyond a microscopic distance from the horizon the low-energy effective field theory holds. The third is that an observer falling in sees nothing out of the ordinary at the horizon; this is the equivalence principle that comes from general relativity.

The cause of the contradiction is the sharing of entanglement. For information to be preserved, late radiation must be entangled with radiation that left earlier. But for an observer at the horizon to see the 'ordinary' vacuum, that same late radiation must also be entangled with its partner on the inner side of the horizon. A state cannot be fully entangled with two different systems at the same time.

The authors say that perhaps the most conservative resolution is that the infalling observer burns up at the horizon. Other resolutions would require a new dynamics in which semiclassical physics breaks down at macroscopic distances from the horizon.

The paper is a 'no-go' argument; it does not say that a firewall must be real. Giving up one of the assumptions the argument rests on, or interpreting the result differently, became the subject of later debates.

The paper's influence lies not in a solution but in a sharp statement of the problem: the question of which of three reasonable assumptions to give up moved to the center of the quantum gravity debate. In their own later writing the authors say 'we have not changed our minds', stating that they broadened and sharpened the argument of the first versions.

Afterwards: The paper quickly prompted many responses; one of them is Maldacena and Susskind's 2013 proposal.

41. ER = EPR

Main question: Could the bond between entangled particles and the wormhole linking two black holes be two faces of the same phenomenon?

An Einstein–Rosen bridge is a wormhole that connects two distant regions in general relativity; an EPR pair is two entangled particles. Maldacena and Susskind proposed that two maximally entangled black holes are connected by an Einstein–Rosen bridge, and that similar links may exist in more general entangled states as well. This is a proposal; it has not been proven.

Maldacena 2013 — Cool horizons for entangled black holes

Fortschr. Phys. 61, 781 theory interpretation

Maldacena and Susskind put forward a proposal that identifies entanglement with a spacetime bridge, and used it as a way out of the firewall paradox.

General relativity contains solutions in which two distant black holes are connected through the interior region by a bridge, that is, an Einstein–Rosen bridge. The authors interpret these solutions as a maximally entangled state of two black holes; that is, a complex EPR pair.

The paper's proposal is that similar bridges may exist in more general entangled states as well. In other words, entanglement and geometric connection are, to some extent, two descriptions of the same phenomenon. The proposal is briefly called 'ER = EPR'.

Within this framework, AMPS-type paradoxes can be set up for entangled black holes, and the authors argue that they can be resolved. The idea is this: the entanglement between the radiation and its partner inside the horizon may also be one and the same as a geometric connection, and this is a way of getting around the obstacle that a state cannot be entangled with two partners at once.

The paper is long and detailed; the results are mostly supported by examples in the AdS/CFT context and by theoretical consistency arguments. This is not an experimental test.

Its limit: the full form of the proposal for general entangled states is not clear; the authors themselves put it as 'we propose' and 'this suggests possible resolutions'.

An important part of the paper is the detailed study of the solution in which two maximally entangled black holes are connected by a shared interior region. Within this framework the authors set up a version of the firewall paradox and try to resolve it; for general entangled states they present the proposal as a possibility.

Afterwards: The proposal became a starting point for later work examining the relation between entanglement and spacetime geometry.

42. Quantum Darwinism

Main question: How can observers come to agree on the state of a quantum system without touching the system?

Quantum theory describes a system by a wave function; until a measurement is made, this state can be a superposition of several possibilities. In everyday life, however, everyone sees the same thing about where a table stands. How does this difference arise? Decoherence (the loss of phase coherence) is the system's interacting with its environment and losing the traces of superposition. Quantum Darwinism adds to this: only some states of the system leave many copies of traces in the environment, and observers who look at these traces reach the same conclusion without touching the system directly. The question is how information that looks 'objective' emerges from a quantum ground.

Ollivier 2004 — Objective properties from subjective quantum states: Environment as a witness

Physical Review Letters 93, 220401 theory

One of the first formulations that treats the environment not as a passive destroyer but as an active witness that multiplies information.

The paper asks how objective properties emerge in open quantum systems. An open system is one that interacts with its environment; photons, air molecules or other particles pass by the system and carry a trace of it. In the classical account of decoherence, the environment was seen as a 'reservoir' that selectively destroys quantum coherence. The authors give the environment a second role: a communication channel that carries and broadcasts information about the system.

The study's main claim is this: only the 'preferred' (pointer) states of the system can leave a superfluous number of traces in the environment, that is, a redundant record. If we divide the environment into small pieces, each of these pieces is enough, independently, to read the same pointer observable of the system. The consequence is that we can learn not everything about the system but only its pointer observable.

The conclusion drawn from this concerns observers. Most of the time we learn the state of a system not directly but by looking at part of its environment (for example, by catching the light scattered from an object). Many observers can do this independently of one another, without touching the system, and they agree on the same pointer observable. In the paper's wording, these preferred pointer states exist 'objectively' in this operational sense. This work was elaborated in a longer paper by the same authors (Phys. Rev. A, 2005). The paper is a theoretical modeling study; it contains no laboratory experiment.

Afterwards: Zurek developed the same idea in the following years under the name 'Quantum Darwinism'; the debate turned to how general the criterion of 'objectivity' is and to the effect of correlations among environment fragments.

Zurek 2009 — Quantum Darwinism

Nat. Phys. 5, 181 theory interpretation

Zurek's review in Nature Physics presenting the idea of Quantum Darwinism as a whole.

Zurek approaches the quantum measurement problem from a new angle: the robustness of the classical world may arise from a fragile quantum state leaving many copies of traces in the environment. According to the paper's abstract, Quantum Darwinism describes the multiplying records in the environment of the selected states of a system. Thanks to these records, the fragility of a single quantum system turns into a classical durability in the many records that carry correlations about that system.

The selection in the idea is the technical counterpart of the 'fitness' analogy in nature: the states that leave the most copies in the environment count as 'the fittest', while superpositions, being impossible to copy, are left behind. This process is called einselection (environment-induced selection); the term also appears in Zurek's 2003 review.

In the paper Zurek brings three things together. First, he explains how the appearance of an effective 'wave packet collapse' arises thanks to the multiplication of records. Second, he shows that many observers can read the same information from different parts of the environment. Third, he offers a framework for deriving the Born rule, which ties the probability of finding a state in a measurement to its amplitude. According to the author, these three advances together are an important step toward solving the quantum measurement problem.

This last claim is contested. Whether the Born rule has been derived, and whether the idea solves the whole measurement problem, are questioned separately. The paper is a theoretical review.

Afterwards: The idea later became the subject of detailed studies on the number of environment fragments and on correlation structures; one of the experimental attempts was published in 2018 (Ciampini et al.).

Ciampini 2018 — Experimental signature of quantum Darwinism in photonic cluster states

Physical Review A 98, 020101 experiment

The experiment that first measured the traces of Quantum Darwinism in a small 'environment' model built with photons.

The previous two works were theory; this paper is an experiment. The team sets up in the laboratory a system in which the 'environment' consists of a few photon pieces. The source they use is a hyperentangled source that entangles the polarization and the path information of photons at the same time; the entangled photons form a 'graph state' (cluster state), that is, a multipartite state connected like a network.

Their aim is to test how correlations among the pieces making up the environment affect the Quantum Darwinism framework. In the simple models of the theory, the environment pieces interact with the system but not with one another. In real environments, however, the environment elements are also connected to each other. The experiment deliberately builds this multipartite entanglement and shows that, as a result, the way the system is learned from the environment changes.

According to the authors' abstract, the work is one of the first experimental attempts to characterize Quantum Darwinism. An important observation is that multipartite entanglement and the possibility of back-action from the environment on the system are meaningfully reflected in the properties of the framework. That is, these details, which are absent from the basic theoretical picture, make a difference.

This experiment is described as a 'signature', not a 'proof'; the word signature in its title stresses this. It is a small, controlled photon model and does not represent a real macroscopic environment.

Afterwards: Attempts on other platforms were made later in the same area; the move from the small model to the real environment is still an open task.

43. Weak Measurement

Main question: What can be learned about a quantum system by barely touching it?

In quantum mechanics, measurement disturbs the system: if we measure position very precisely, momentum becomes uncertain. Weak measurement turns this situation around: a measurement is made that touches the system very lightly, the result of a single trial is very noisy, but the average over thousands of trials carries meaning. This average is called the 'weak value'. The question is this: when the disturbance the measurement causes to the system is small, what kind of information is obtained, and how should this information be interpreted? Weak values can sometimes fall outside the system's usual range of outcomes. This feature is both a useful measurement tool and a topic open to interpretive debate.

Aharonov 1988 — How the result of a measurement of a component of the spin of a spin-1/2 particle can turn out to be 100

Phys. Rev. Lett. 60, 1351 theory

The paper that first defined the weak value concept and predicted an unexpectedly large value for a spin-1/2 particle.

Aharonov, Albert and Vaidman work with the notions of 'preselection' and 'postselection' in quantum measurement. The system is first prepared in a certain initial state (preselection). The measuring device is coupled very weakly to the system. Then, by a separate measurement, the system is selected from among those found in another state (postselection). The average shift the measuring device registers gives the quantity the paper calls the 'weak value'.

The paper's title asks a striking example: can the measurement result of a spin component of a spin-1/2 particle come out as 100? For spin-1/2, the usual measurement results are only two values (plus or minus half a unit). The authors calculate that when the preselected and postselected states are nearly orthogonal to each other, the weak value can come out much larger than these two numbers. This means the average of the device's pointer goes outside the limit.

This result is not a contradiction, because in weak measurement each single measurement is noisy and the result is only the average of a selected subset. The weak value is not the value the system takes in a single measurement, but an average effect under certain conditions.

On interpretation there are two separate tendencies: one regards the weak value as a 'real' property of the system, the other sees it merely as the average response of the measuring device. This is contested.

This summary was written without looking at the paper's own abstract or text, relying on a known account; it should be checked against the source.

Afterwards: The measurement of weak values in the laboratory was shown in 1991; in the following decades applications such as increasing measurement precision and state reconstruction were developed.

Ritchie 1991 — Realization of a measurement of a "weak value"

Physical Review Letters 66, 1107-1110 experiment

The first laboratory measurement of a weak value (with the polarization of a light beam).

This paper is the first experimental demonstration of the weak value concept proposed theoretically in 1988. The experiment is done in an optical setup: the polarization of the light is preselected, a crystal shifts the light beam very slightly depending on polarization, and the beam is then postselected on another polarization. The measured quantity is the shift of the beam.

The theory's prediction is that when the postselected state is in an orthogonal position very close to the preselected state, the shift can go far beyond the usual limit. The experiment shows that this prediction holds under the conditions of the setup. That is, it measures that the shift can come out clearly larger than the crystal alone could give.

It should be stressed that this finding does not contradict quantum mechanics. The same effect can also be described by classical optics explanations that use the interference of waves. For this reason the experiment itself does not settle a case on interpretation; it shows that the weak value is a measurable quantity and that it behaves as expected.

This summary was written without looking at the paper's own abstract or text, relying on a known account; it should be checked against the source.

Afterwards: In the following period the weak value method was used as a measurement technique for amplifying small shifts.

Kocsis 2011 — Observing the Average Trajectories of Single Photons in a Two-Slit Interferometer

Science 332, 1170-1173 experiment

The experiment that reconstructs, by weak measurement, the average paths of photons inside a double-slit interference.

In the double-slit experiment, single photons leave dots on a screen; when enough photons are collected, an interference pattern appears. The question is what path a single photon follows after the slits. In quantum mechanics, measuring the path destroys the pattern. This work, instead of 'measuring' the path, reconstructs the average path by weak measurement.

In the setup, the direction information of the photons is measured weakly by coupling it slightly to polarization; then the final position of the photons (postselection) is recorded. Extracting the average direction for each final position gives an 'average trajectory'. These trajectories do not cross one another and look like the streamlines that form the interference pattern.

The authors do not interpret what these trajectories mean; they are not the real path of a single photon but averages computed from the weak measurements of many photons. Their relation to the trajectories of Bohmian mechanics was discussed later. The claim of a comment written on the paper (Coffey, 2011) is that the average trajectories are consistent with Bohm trajectories; what this means is a matter of interpretation and is contested.

This summary was written without looking at the paper's own abstract or text, relying on a known account; it should be checked against the source.

Afterwards: Interpretive debate: correspondence was held over whether the average trajectories are the same as the Bohm trajectories or merely consistent with them.

Dressel 2014 — Colloquium: Understanding quantum weak values: Basics and applications

Reviews of Modern Physics 86, 307-316 theory

A review that explains what weak values are and what they are good for, through measurable quantities.

The paper is a review written about 25 years after the first definition of the weak value, and it aims to clear up historical confusion. The authors' approach is to introduce the weak value through measurable quantities and through how it is determined in the laboratory.

The review treats three separate areas of use of the weak value through a single experimental setup. The first is, as a large interaction parameter, to lift small signals above technical noise; that is, it is an amplification tool. The second is, as a complex-valued, measurable quantity, to make possible the direct determination of the quantum state and the measurement of the geometric phase. The third is, as the conditional average of the eigenvalues of generalized observables, to provide a measurable window onto the nonclassical features of quantum mechanics.

The paper also carries the interpretive debate as background: the weak value is sometimes read as a 'real property of the system', sometimes only as the conditional average of the measuring device. The review's approach is pragmatic; whichever interpretation is adopted, it explains how the weak value is measured and what it is used for.

This summary is based on the paper's short published abstract (arXiv); I have not summarized the whole review here.

Afterwards: The field subsequently turned both to measurement-precision applications and to debates over the foundations of preselected and postselected measurements.

44. Contextuality

Main question: Does the result of a measurement depend on which other measurements are made at the same time?

In everyday experience, the color of an object does not depend on which other property we look at while measuring it. Quantum mechanics says this is not true: the result of some measurements must depend on which other measurements are made together with it. This is called contextuality. The Kochen–Specker theorem shows that it is not possible to assign predetermined, context-independent values to certain sets of measurements. The question is how this is tested by experiment and what it means.

Kochen 1967 — The Problem of Hidden Variables in Quantum Mechanics

Indiana University Mathematics Journal (J. Math. Mech.) 17, 59-87 theory

The original mathematical paper showing that quantum mechanics contradicts the assumption of noncontextuality.

Kochen and Specker examine mathematically whether 'hidden variables' can be added to quantum mechanics. A hidden variable theory is the idea that the uncertainty of quantum results comes only from our lack of knowledge and that there is a predetermined value for every measurement.

The paper's result is this: if the dimension of the Hilbert space is at least three, it is not possible to assign context-independent, predetermined values to the measurable quantities (observables). Context independence means that the value of a measurement is independent of the other measurements that can be measured together with it. The original proof proceeds through a particular set of directions that tries to satisfy this condition, and shows that every attempt leads to a contradiction.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry summarizes the theorem as follows: under the assumption of noncontextuality, it is impossible to assign values to certain sets of quantum observables, even before statistical distributions are asked about. This is a different prohibition from Bell's theorem: Bell's theorem targets the assumption of locality, the Kochen–Specker theorem the assumption of noncontextuality.

Interpretations of the meaning of the result differ: options such as rejecting value definiteness, accepting contextuality, or there being no values at all are debated. The theorem is an impossibility result; it does not say which of these options is correct.

Afterwards: The proofs were later shortened and the theorem was made experimentally testable; whether this can be tested was also debated.

Kirchmair 2009 — State-independent experimental test of quantum contextuality

Nature 460, 494 experiment

The experimental demonstration, with trapped ions and independent of any particular state, of the contradiction between noncontextuality and quantum mechanics.

The paper begins by recalling that, as Bell showed in 1964, local hidden variable models cannot account for quantum predictions. An intuitive classical property is noncontextuality: the value of a measurement is independent of the other compatible measurements made at the same time. The results of Kochen, Specker and Bell show that this property contradicts quantum mechanics.

It was known that this contradiction comes from the structure of the theory and does not depend on particular special cases. However, whether the Kochen–Specker theorem could be tested by experiment had been debated. Earlier tests had been done with photons and neutrons, required special quantum states, and left loopholes open.

In this work the authors use trapped ions and demonstrate a state-independent contradiction with noncontextuality. According to the authors, the experiment is not subject to the detection loophole, and despite imperfections and possible measurement disturbances the results cannot be explained in noncontextual terms.

This experiment shows that contextuality is not only a mathematical result but an observable phenomenon. The interpretation is still contested: what contextuality points to (for example, that measurement results are not determined before the measurement) is not the subject of the experiment itself.

Afterwards: In the following years the same test was repeated on other systems; for example, a similar experiment was published on the nitrogen-vacancy center in diamond (a qutrit).

Budroni 2022 — Kochen-Specker contextuality

Rev. Mod. Phys. 94, 045007 theory

A review that brings together the proofs, experimental tests and applications of Kochen–Specker contextuality.

The review states the Kochen–Specker theorem briefly as follows: quantum mechanics contradicts classical models that assume the result of a measurement does not depend on the other compatible measurements made together with it. Here, compatible measurements are those that can be made at the same time or, more generally, that are jointly measurable. This contradiction is generally called quantum contextuality.

The paper gives an introduction to the subject and surveys the various proofs of the theorem and the different notions of contextuality. It explains how some of them can be tested experimentally, and discusses the connections of contextuality with nonlocality (Bell nonlocality) and with graph theory. In the last section some applications of contextuality in quantum information processing are reviewed.

A practical benefit when reading this review is to see that the word 'contextuality' does not correspond to a single thing: different forms of the concept rest on different assumptions, and each is tested by a separate experiment.

Afterwards: Current work in the field is moving toward treating contextuality as a resource in quantum computation; this heading is contested and is a separate topic.

45. Quantum Chaos

Main question: Does the quantum counterpart of a classically chaotic system carry a trace of chaos?

In classical mechanics, chaos is the rapid growth of small differences in initial conditions. Quantum mechanics is linear and does not show chaos in the same sense. Even so, the quantum energy levels of a classically chaotic system carry a certain statistical pattern. The question of this topic is how the chaotic or regular character of the classical dynamics shows up in the quantum spectrum. At the core of the answer lies the distribution of level spacings.

Bohigas 1984 — Characterization of Chaotic Quantum Spectra and Universality of Level Fluctuation Laws

Physical Review Letters 52, 1-4 theory

The paper that suggests that the level fluctuations of chaotic quantum spectra are universal.

Bohigas, Giannoni and Schmit examine the statistics of the quantum energy levels of a classically chaotic system. The levels of a quantum system are like points on a ruler; the real question is how the spacings between neighboring levels are distributed.

The paper's result is this: in classically fully chaotic systems (for example, a billiard of the Sinai type), the level spacings follow the distribution predicted by random matrix theory. In this distribution very close levels are rare; the levels behave as if they 'repel' one another (level repulsion). This behavior looks universal rather than dependent on the details of the system.

This observation suggests that random matrix theory (a theory originally developed to describe complex nuclei) can be applied to chaotic quantum systems. The result is today mostly referred to as the Bohigas–Giannoni–Schmit conjecture.

This summary was written without looking at the paper's own abstract or text, relying on a known account; it should be checked against the source.

Afterwards: A semiclassical (periodic orbit) explanation for the conjecture was developed later.

Berry 1977 — Level clustering in the regular spectrum

Proceedings of the Royal Society A 356, 375-394 theory

The paper that suggests that level spacings in regular (nonchaotic) systems are randomly (Poisson) distributed; it shows the opposite case.

Berry and Tabor examine systems in which the classical motion is regular. In such an f-dimensional system, each energy level is labeled by f quantum numbers, and these numbers come from the f constants of the classical motion. Levels with very different quantum numbers can fall at similar energies.

The authors examine the distribution of the spacings between neighboring levels in the classical limit. To remove the effect of the varying local level density, they use a scale transformation. The result: in typical regular systems the distribution has the form P(S) = e^(−S); this is a property of a Poisson process, in which levels are randomly distributed.

This general rule has an exception: in a system of harmonic oscillators the energy contours are flat. If the frequencies are commensurate, P(S) is not defined; if they are incommensurate, it peaks around a nonzero value, that is, there is some order in the level distribution. The authors confirm their results with numerical experiments on simple two-dimensional systems.

Read together with the BGS paper, the picture is set up as follows: in regular systems Poisson is expected, in chaotic systems random-matrix-like level repulsion. This paper gives the theoretical grounds for one of the two ends.

Afterwards: The expectation of a Poisson distribution for regular systems, together with the BGS conjecture for the chaotic side, became a basic framework in the quantum chaos literature.

46. Open Quantum Systems

Main question: How is the change over time of a quantum system that interacts with its environment written down?

A real quantum system is never completely isolated; it interacts with its environment to a greater or lesser degree. The Schrödinger equation describes isolated systems. The state of a system interacting with its environment, however, is described by a density matrix, and this state changes through effects such as energy loss and loss of phase coherence. The question is what the most general form of this change is that is mathematically consistent (in that probabilities are neither negative nor sum to something other than one). The Lindblad equation is the answer to this question when the environment is 'memoryless' (Markov).

Lindblad 1976 — On the generators of quantum dynamical semigroups

Communications in Mathematical Physics 48, 119-130 theory

The paper that gives the most general form of the generators of quantum dynamical semigroups.

Lindblad examines the semigroups that describe the time evolution of a quantum system interacting with its environment. A semigroup is a time evolution in which the state after a certain time step can be obtained by applying two half steps in succession; this corresponds to the assumption that the environment is memoryless (Markov).

The paper finds the most general form of the generators (that is, of the operator giving the time derivative) of 'completely positive' semigroups. Being completely positive means that the state stays physical (free of negative probabilities) even when the system is coupled to another system. The resulting equation gives the density matrix in terms of the system's own Hamiltonian and 'jump operators' that describe the effect of the environment.

Today this equation is known as the Lindblad or GKSL equation; a separate team reached the same result in the same year. According to the GKSL entry on Wikipedia, the equation is one of the general forms of Markovian master equations for density matrices and includes the Schrödinger (von Neumann) equation as a special case.

Its limit: the equation assumes that the environment is memoryless; it is not valid for dynamics with memory (non-Markovian).

Afterwards: This equation became a standard tool in quantum optics, in quantum information processing and in decoherence modeling; non-Markovian dynamics is a separate research area.

Gorini 1976 — Completely positive dynamical semigroups of N-level systems

Journal of Mathematical Physics 17, 821-825 theory

The paper in which a separate team reached the same result for N-level systems.

Gorini, Kossakowski and Sudarshan examine the completely positive dynamical semigroups of N-level (finite-dimensional) quantum systems. The work was published in the same year as Lindblad's paper, independently of it.

The heart of the problem is this: what equation does the density matrix of a finite-dimensional quantum system interacting with its environment obey, so that the state always remains a valid quantum state? The paper writes this equation out explicitly in finite dimension; the generator contains the system Hamiltonian and a positive matrix representing the effect of the environment.

The combination of the two papers is today referred to as the GKSL (Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad) equation. According to the Wikipedia entry, this equation is one of the general forms of Markovian master equations for open quantum systems, and for every initial condition the solutions give trace-preserving, completely positive maps.

For the detailed mathematical conditions one needs to look at the paper itself.

Afterwards: The GKSL equation is today the standard modeling of systems that involve energy loss and decoherence; its limit is the Markov assumption.

47. Resource Theory of Entanglement

Main question: Can entanglement be measured, converted and spent like a 'resource'?

In quantum information processing, entanglement is seen as a resource that is consumed in tasks such as teleportation and superdense coding. Resource theory asks this question: what can we do with local operations and classical communication (LOCC) alone, and what is it that cannot be obtained with them? Entanglement is what cannot be produced by these operations. The question is how we tell whether a state is entangled, how we measure how entangled it is, and whether we can make entanglement usable by purifying it.

Horodecki 1996 — Separability of mixed states: necessary and sufficient conditions

Physics Letters A 223, 1-8 theory

The paper that gives the necessary and sufficient conditions for telling whether mixed states are entangled (separability).

The paper asks whether mixed quantum states are separable. A separable state is one that carries only classical correlation between the two parties. Non-entangled states are exactly these.

The authors find necessary and sufficient conditions for separability. One of the results is that, in 2×2 and 2×3 dimensional systems, positivity of the partial transposition is necessary and sufficient for separability. That is, in these dimensions, if the partial transpose of a state contains a negative eigenvalue the state is entangled; if it does not, it is separable.

This is not true in general. In larger dimensions a state can be entangled even though its partial transpose is positive. The authors also take up some mixture examples that show how the criterion works.

The paper is a basic step that carries entanglement from pure states to mixed states; for real laboratory states are mostly mixed.

Afterwards: This criterion was later referred to as the Peres–Horodecki criterion and became one of the standard tools of entanglement detection.

Horodecki 1998 — Mixed-State Entanglement and Distillation: Is there a "Bound" Entanglement in Nature?

Physical Review Letters 80, 5239-5242 theory

The paper that shows there are two separate kinds of entanglement: distillable (usable) and nondistillable ('bound') entanglement.

The paper asks this question: can every entangled mixed state be converted into a pure pair (a singlet) that can be used in tasks such as teleportation? This conversion is called distillation; it is done with local operations and classical communication alone.

The authors show that if a mixed state can be distilled to the singlet form, it must violate Peres's partial transpose criterion. The consequence is this: there are two qualitatively different kinds of entanglement. One is 'free' entanglement and can be distilled. The other is 'bound' entanglement and cannot be brought to the singlet form that is useful for quantum communication.

That is, a state may be entangled and yet its entanglement may not be usable. The paper also discusses the possible physical meaning of this result.

This shows that entanglement cannot be described by a single 'amount'; for this reason resource theory uses several measures together.

Afterwards: The existence of bound entanglement became an important research topic of entanglement theory in the following years; which states are bound is a separate question.

Horodecki 2009 — Quantum entanglement

Reviews of Modern Physics 81, 865-942 theory

The Reviews of Modern Physics review that brings together the characterization, detection, distillation and quantification of entanglement.

The review gives a compact account of the basic aspects of entanglement: characterization, detection, distillation and quantification. The authors discuss the various faces of entanglement through Bell inequalities, entropic inequalities, entanglement witnesses and quantum cryptography, and show the connections among them.

The review's framework is the 'distant laboratories' paradigm: the two parties can operate only in their own laboratories and can communicate classically. Within this framework the basic role of entanglement is stressed.

The paper also takes up the irreversibility of entanglement operations. The phenomenon of bound entanglement is highlighted as an extreme example. According to the review, entanglement witnesses play a basic role in detection.

Afterwards: This review was used as a standard reference in later work on entanglement.

48. Quantum Metrology

Main question: Can quantum properties make measurements more precise than the classical limit?

If we measure a quantity (a length, a phase) many times and take the average, the error falls with the square root of the number of measurements. This is called the standard quantum limit (or the shot noise limit). Quantum metrology asks whether this limit can be exceeded with quantum resources such as entanglement or squeezed states. If it can, the error falls faster with the number of measurements.

Giovannetti 2004 — Quantum-Enhanced Measurements: Beating the Standard Quantum Limit

Science 306, 1330-1336 theory

A Science review that explains how quantum tricks (squeezing, entanglement) get past the standard quantum limit.

The paper begins by noting that quantum mechanics places a limit on measurement precision through the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. Conventional measurement techniques often cannot reach these limits.

The authors note that known limits such as the shot noise limit or the standard quantum limit are not as fundamental as the Heisenberg limits and can be surpassed by strategies that use 'quantum tricks'. Squeezing and entanglement are mentioned among these tricks.

The review's main message is the order in which quantum strategies can improve precision. The abstract of the same authors' 2011 review 'Advances in Quantum Metrology' (Nature Photonics 5, 222) puts this difference as follows: in classical estimation theory, because of the central limit theorem, the error falls with n measurements like n^(−1/2); with quantum effects it is often possible to reach 1/n.

Afterwards: To what extent noise and experimental imperfections reduce this gain became one of the main topics of the field in the following years.

Giovannetti 2006 — Quantum Metrology

Physical Review Letters 96, 010401 theory

The paper that sets up quantum metrology in a general framework and proves that the square-root gain is the best possible.

The authors present a general framework that covers most cases in which quantum effects increase precision in the estimation of a parameter. Quantum metrology is the subject of this framework.

According to the paper's abstract, the typical quantum gain in precision is proportional to the square root of the number of samples taken from the system. That is, while a classical strategy gives a scaling like n^(−1/2) for the error when sampling n times, a quantum strategy can approach 1/n; this means an improvement of square-root size. The authors prove that this gain is the best possible.

The paper also describes the different strategies (classical and quantum) that make it possible to reach this limit. In this way, rather than particular experiments, a general framework is obtained that says which kinds of resources give which scaling.

For the detailed proofs one needs to consult the paper itself.

Afterwards: In the following years the framework entered reviews with extensions that take noise and imperfections into account.

49. Quantum Sensing

Main question: How are quantum systems used as sensors that measure small physical quantities?

Quantum sensing is the use of the quantum properties of a quantum system to measure a physical quantity. One of quantities such as a magnetic field, an electric field, temperature or time slightly changes the energy levels of the quantum system, and this change is read out. The question is which systems are good sensors, by which methods they are read out, and what limits the precision.

Degen 2017 — Quantum sensing

Reviews of Modern Physics 89, 035002 theory experiment

A Reviews of Modern Physics review that introduces the basic principles, methods and concepts of quantum sensing from an experimentalist's point of view.

The review defines quantum sensing as the use of a quantum system, of quantum properties or of quantum phenomena to measure a physical quantity. Historical examples mentioned include superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs), magnetometers based on atomic vapors, and atomic clocks.

According to the authors, in recent years quantum sensing has become a separate and fast-growing branch of research within quantum science and technology. The most common platforms are spin qubits, trapped ions and flux qubits.

For an interested experimentalist the paper introduces the basic principles, methods and concepts. The field is expected to open up new possibilities in applied physics and in other areas, especially in matters of high sensitivity and precision.

Quantum metrology asks theoretically about the limits of precision; sensing asks with which hardware these limits are approached in practice.

Afterwards: Platforms such as the nitrogen-vacancy centers of diamond were used widely in magnetic field sensing in later work.

50. Quantum Networks and the Quantum Internet

Main question: Is it possible to connect distant quantum processors and carry quantum information over a network?

The classical internet carries bits. A quantum network, however, wants to share quantum states and entanglement among nodes. Because quantum states cannot be copied (see No-Cloning) and because there is loss and noise over long distances, this cannot be a copy of the classical network. The pieces of the solution are entanglement distribution, teleportation and quantum repeaters. The question is what becomes possible when these come together.

Kimble 2008 — The quantum internet

Nature 453, 1023-1030 theory experiment

A short vision paper in Nature that describes the opportunities and challenges of quantum networks.

Kimble says that quantum networks present a common set of opportunities and challenges for quantum computation, communication and metrology. For a network made up of many nodes and channels to be realized, new scientific capabilities are needed in producing and characterizing quantum coherence and entanglement.

At the center of the paper are 'quantum interconnects': tools that reversibly transform the quantum states of one physical system into those of another. For example, transferring the state of an atom to the state of a photon and sending that photon onward.

According to Kimble, this connection can be provided by optical interactions between single photons and atoms. In this way entanglement distribution and quantum teleportation between nodes become possible.

The paper is a vision and review; it is not a definite technical design or the measurements of a particular network.

Afterwards: In the following decade experimental work on quantum repeaters and memories multiplied; a full-scale quantum internet has still not been realized.

Wehner 2018 — Quantum internet: A vision for the road ahead

Science 362, eaam9288 theory

A Science vision paper that describes the gradual stages of development of the quantum internet and its applications.

This paper is written as a 'roadmap'. The idea of the quantum internet is to support long-distance communication in the classical internet with quantum communication; that is, for any two points on Earth to be able to exchange quantum information.

In the paper's framework the quantum internet is treated in several stages of development. Each stage defines the tasks the network can perform and the hardware capabilities required; as the stages increase, the network can perform more tasks.

The reader needs to look at the paper itself.

This summary was written without looking at the paper's own abstract or text, relying on a known account; it should be checked against the source.

Afterwards: Roadmaps of this kind were used in the following years as targets in experimental network work.
