The Point and the Circle: Dost's Own Drawn Diagram

pp. 264–357

Site reading summary

Four chapters move from the hidden glorification of animals and inanimate beings, through Dost's "three darknesses" vision from his earliest days in Sufism, and the perfect human's gathering of the triad of likeness-opposition-contrariety, to a point-and-circumference diagram Dost draws with his own hand, stating that "the point is the Real... the lines and possibles that emerge begin from God and return to Him."

Read the part

The Hidden Glorification of Animals and Inanimate Beings

Chapter 357 argues that animals, plants, and inanimate beings are in reality "rational" (perceiving, speaking), something only the people of unveiling and faith can witness. Dost supports this with a series of examples from the Qur'an and hadith: the verse "everything glorifies Him with praise," the ants speaking with Solomon, the hoopoe saying "I know what you do not know," and the sound report that the palm trunk the Prophet leaned against later wept (pp. 264-266). The chapter title mentions "two Mosaic secrets" (p. 265).

The chapter opens with the way by which the possibles are known as they are in themselves. Dost says this knowledge can be had only if God shows it from His own essence, by unveiling: "The only way for this knowledge to be realized is eternal solicitude. This solicitude grants you a complete disposition that enables you to receive it." He lists the means of gaining that disposition: soul-disciplines, bodily striving, taking on the character of the divine names, realizing oneself with pure angelic spirits, purification by a lawful way (not a rational one), turning away from attachment to existents, and emptying the heart of everything other than the Real (p. 264).

He explains that the possibles know and speak even in their states of non-existence: they are heard and seen through a hearing and seeing of fixed standing (thubuti) that has no outward existence, and when God's command "Be" is made audible to them they hasten to the command. "In their own eternal non-existence the possibles know the Necessary Being by their very nature and glorify Him. This is an eternal glorification and an everlasting, essential praise." (p. 265) Every existent praises God in its own tongue; this tongue is not understood, the people of unveiling hear it by witnessing, and the believer accepts it by faith. Dost ties the mention of the names al-Halim and al-Ghafur in the verse to the fact that some servants are deprived of unveiling and faith: rationalists enslaved to their own thought and those content with analogical consideration. The sound-speaking believers among the friends of God, by contrast, "pass to the inward together with the outward, and to the meaning together with the letter; they do not turn away from the letter." (p. 265)

Dost says God has veiled the eyes of some servants from the life, speech and perception that spread through the possibles and all existents. What shows its life is called "living"; what keeps its life hidden is called "plant" or "inert": "Thus beings are divided according to the veiled, while according to the people of unveiling and faith they are not divided." The people of witnessing say "we heard and saw," the veiled say "we neither saw nor heard," and the people of faith say "we believed and affirmed" (p. 266). He lists the verses on the matter: the prostration of everything in the heavens and on the earth, the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains and the living creatures; the glorification of thunder; the prostration of shadows; the ant saying "enter your dwellings"; the teaching of the speech of birds. From hadith he cites the testimony of everything dry and moist for the muezzin as far as his voice reaches, Uhud as "a mountain that loves us and that we love," the stone in Mecca that greeted the Prophet, the glorification of the stones in his palm, and the poisoned leg of lamb that spoke to him (pp. 266-267).

He relates the hadith on the killing of the Jews at the end of time: when a Jew hides behind a tree, the tree will say "O Muslim, there is a Jew behind me, kill him," while the Gharqad tree will not say it. Dost says there is a "strange divine secret" here: among trees too there are those who hope for God's mercy and keep the right of the neighbor; the right of the neighbor is a quality praised in all societies and religions, and the Prophet said to his cousin Umm Hani': "Whom you have taken as a neighbor, we too have taken as a neighbor." (p. 267) He says the particle "man" ("whoever") in the verse "God singles out for His mercy whomever He wills" is indefinite and takes in everything, because everything is living and speaking; each thing learns from God with what to praise itself (p. 267). The inseparable mark of one who knows that everything speaks, he gives as shame before everything, even before himself and his neighbors (p. 268).

The Name of Animals and the Acts That Issue from Them

Dost says animals were named "bahima" from "ibham" (vagueness) because God made their condition vague to us; this vagueness comes from our not knowing the truth of them or from our bewilderment before them (p. 269). He gives examples of acts that issue from animals by their innate nature, without thought or forethought: spiders spinning webs to catch flies, some animals storing food by a measure, ants eating only half of what they have stored for fear of going hungry. "Where, then, is the senselessness ascribed to them, and where is this conduct?" (p. 269) He notes that God made the condition of animals vague for some people, and that this leads them to interpret away the speech of animals in the Book and the Sunna (p. 269).

Dost says that a rational being wishing to convey the thought within does not content himself with arranging letters: at times he uses a spoken expression, at times a gesture of hand or head, at times writing or numerals, at times any means that makes understanding possible; all of these are called "speech" (p. 270). He describes the creature of the verse "We shall bring forth for them from the earth a creature that speaks to them": it comes out of Ajyad, its front cannot be told from its back, and it is called the "jassasa." It breathes out once, and by this blowing the faces of people become distinct; on each person's forehead is drawn the state he has in God's knowledge, faith or unbelief. The speech ascribed to the animal is the movement that makes this line distinct; if it is to speak, it speaks in its own tongue and the one addressed understands. He ties this to the sound report in Muslim's hadith of the Antichrist, in which Tamim al-Dari meets the Antichrist (p. 270).

Higher and Lower Forms, the Preserved Tablet, and Teaching

Dost says every form in the lower world has a counterpart in the higher world: "The forms of the higher world preserve the existence of the forms of the lower world." Between the two worlds run unbroken ties from each form to its counterpart in the other; ascent and descent take place along these ties, so they may be counted as "ladders and steps" and are sometimes called "affinity" (pp. 270-271). There are also ties between the celestial forms and what the Prophet, relaying from God, called the "Preserved Tablet." The Tablet has this name because God preserves in it what He has written; since everything is in it, the Qur'an calls it "everything": "God wrote His books in it, and from it sent down the pages He revealed to His prophets and messengers." The books detail what is summarized in it (p. 271).

He defines teaching: "Teaching is the removal of the veils that prevent the dispositions of the lower forms from receiving the outpouring (directed to them from the higher forms)." He gives the example of an obstacle that blocks the flow of water: once it is removed, the water flows from where it had been held back; in truth the one who removes the obstacle is not the one who makes the water flow. The one who teaches another of the lower forms has likewise only removed the veil of ignorance and doubt, though people suppose that the one who teaches them knowledge is the one who removes the veil of ignorance (p. 272). He notes that each possible has its own particular face, which no one but the one brought into existence knows, and that by this face everything is in need of God, not of its cosmic cause. The particular knowledges God gives each form are tasted knowledges, which cannot be conveyed and are known only to the one who tastes; from this particularization the "gardens of particular favor" came to be (p. 272).

He says this station is the one station that embraces the whole world and the human being, and that it holds the general mercy in the world. By its nature the world rests on four elements; by its spirit it arises from father, mother, the breathing-in and the divine turning, so mercy reaches it from four sides. The mercy that keeps moisture and saves it from the effect of dryness differs from the mercy that keeps dryness and prevents moisture from destroying it, and the mercy that holds a thing cold differs from the one that holds it hot: "These elements hold one another back, and as long as this holding back and balancing continues, the form of the body is preserved." Whoever learns these four mercies from himself will learn where he is headed; whoever does not know them in himself is ignorant of his own state (pp. 272-273).

He explains that God veils some people from witnessing these mercies in order to prevent their being put into words, as occurs in the conversations of Umar and Mu'adh. Knowledge reaches its people in two ways: delivered to them directly from God, or taught by trustworthy ones who know another's trust (p. 273). He says the angel who brings the knowledge is one of the winged angels, and the highest of them is the one with the fewest wings, that is, with two (p. 273). He also relates that in one of the cities of the Maghrib he saw a creature resembling a parrot, which came out of a man's chest and ran very swiftly as the man moved it as one moves wings (pp. 273-274). Among angels there are those with from two to six hundred wings and more: "The wings of angels are for descending, not for ascending. The wings of elemental bodies are not for descending but for ascending." (p. 274)

He gives a story as an example of animals knowing what some people do not: a man was beating the head of the donkey he rode with a stick, and when another man tried to stop him, the donkey said "leave him, he is hitting his own head." Dost explains the saying through readings such as the donkey counting the one who strikes it as a donkey, knowing the good it earns with God through the beating, or reckoning that it is fulfilling the right of service for which it was created (p. 274). When the Prophet migrated to Medina and let his camel go, the Companions wished to stop it, and he said: "Leave her, for she is moving by command." Another hadith says "The elephant held her back," which refers to Mecca, and the incident of the elephant is well known. Beings other than humans and jinn, whether animal, plant, inert, angel or spirit, all have a clear proof from their Lord about their own states (pp. 274-275).

Saying "this station contains various knowledges," Dost lists what is learned from it: the knowledge of numbers and letters (the knowledge of the friends of God, as Muhammad b. Ali al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi said), the summary mercies and those particular to man, the knowledge of exposition, the knowledge of good tidings, the ranks of faith, the changing of states and the changing of hearts as they receive divine gifts. Among the questions raised: what the signs and proofs indicate, why they differ though what they point to is one, why the heart is veiled from knowing a thing; whether man joins the animals or is a special kind, and by what trait man is distinguished from the animal when all living things are speaking. He adds that he wrote a book called al-Tadbirat al-ilahiyya on the manners of kings (p. 275). The following lines list the declaration of oneness particular to animals, the king's taking of spies, consultation, the saying "Give gifts to one another and you will love one another," the effect of beneficence on souls, the answering of the prayer of the unbeliever and the polytheist, the gathering, and the naqur (forms) (pp. 275-277).

The Speaking Soul and the Animal Soul: Rider and Mount

Chapter 358 opens under the title "Knowledge of Three Secrets Differing in Their Lights, Abodes and Full Moons" (p. 277). Dost first says the speaking soul is happy in this world and the next and has no share in wretchedness, and he separates the two souls as rider and mount: "The speaking soul is a rider on a mount, and it uses the mount to walk the straight path the Real has set for it." If the animal soul answers this will it becomes a submissive mount, and if it resists it becomes a rebellious one; yet its aim is not to oppose, it moves by its nature, because it does not know the Law (p. 278). He explains that the seat of the punishments for theft, adultery, fighting and slander is the animal soul, which feels the pain, so that there is no difference between the seat of torment in a human being and that of all animals. He relates that the Prophet stood up for the funeral of a Jew and, when told he was a Jew, said "Was he not a soul?" (p. 278).

Dost says that in the states of non-existence God constantly looks at the realities of the possible things, and that divine generosity continues its bestowal upon them. He notes that God addresses the fixed reality (the fixed entity) in the state of non-existence and that it hastens to the command when the address turns to it; the indefiniteness of "each thing" is so as not to separate the fixed from the existent. "God the Exalted is the truthful One who makes known the realities of things as they are in themselves." (p. 279) He explains that the states of the possibles in non-existence arise from themselves, that there is no alteration in non-existence, and that alteration belongs to existence: "So in the fixed entities the matter lies, in particular states, between non-existence and existence." Whoever grasps this, he adds, has learned creation and the attributes by which the Creator must be described, for "There is nothing like Him" and "Every day He is engaged in some affair" hold true (p. 280).

He takes up renunciation: "Renunciation of things may arise from the ignorance in the one who holds it [...] and from the veil covering his eye." He asks how one who witnesses that the whole world praises and knows its Creator can behave as a renunciant toward it, since the renunciant himself is a part of the world. He calls the renunciant's claim to depart from everything other than God "mere ignorance" and says his renunciation is only a shift of place among states (p. 280). He notes that the verse "Flee to God" is followed at once by "Do not set up another god beside God," so that the fleeing is a fleeing from ignorance to knowledge (p. 281). He recounts Moses killing the Copt and fleeing in fear, Shu'ayb's coming before him, the granting of prophethood and the sending to Pharaoh, and states the benefit of knowledge: "For if knowledge does not give its possessor the result his reality requires, there is neither knowledge nor a knower." (pp. 281-283)

He says God did not create man all-knowing, and that He commanded even His Prophet to say "My Lord, increase me in knowledge"; the knowledge in the innate nature of the world and of man is only the knowledge of God's existence and of the created thing's need. Being independent through God is impossible, for being independent of God is impossible (p. 283). He ties the Prophet's withdrawal to the cave of Hira to this: "Whoever seeks his Lord must be alone with his Lord in his inmost secret." God created for man an outward and an inward so that he might be alone with God in his inward; if man takes his knowledge from God, his inward is a place of retreat (p. 284).

Three Darknesses: Dost's Own Vision

Chapter 358 relates a personal vision from Dost's earliest days in Sufism: he saw his own body like a "black rug," then encountered three darknesses piled upon one another. "The first darkness is the darkness of nature visible to you... Above this darkness... is the soul... Above this second darkness there is yet a third darkness. It is the first intellect, the cause from which the soul itself exists." (pp. 284-285) Dost likens this to the three darknesses of the womb (estimation, uterus, belly): "When born, one remains within its darkness, so that while outwardly light, one is inwardly dark. Within this inner darkness, a person can always walk by the lamp of knowledge." (p. 285)

Continuing the vision, Dost recounts that when he saw his body and its darkness he understood he could not look at himself unless it had a certain light, and that when he asked where the light came from he was told, "It is the light of existence, and by it you see yourself." When he saw his shadow spreading over him and was amazed, it was said: "The darknesses of possibility are always in you, for that darkness is an essential attribute of you." To the question "How can I obtain a light in which there is no darkness?" the answer was "You will never find it" (pp. 285-286). He says God is known along this path only by unveiling and witnessing: "By speculative proofs it is His ruling, not Himself, that can be known." The intellect can know only the attribute of the Necessary Being such as the possible's need of Him (p. 286).

He recounts that after God showed him his essence and his body, He showed him the world's relation to him, and that on realizing he had come into being from the world he felt a pang within; the creation of the heavens and the earth, that is, the higher and lower causes of his existence, were above him as agents. He was told: "Even if these causes are above you in respect of the rank in which they appear, the aim is existence, and that is you; these causes exist because of you and so that you may appear." Since God wished to be known, only one in His own form can know Him, and God created the perfect human, not the animal human, in His form (pp. 286-287). Dost says this announcement made up for his brokenness, that he understood he was among the perfect and not an animal, and he gave thanks (p. 287).

The Perfect Human, the World and the Divine Names

He says that when God showed him the world's relation to him and his to the world, he distinguished two ranks and concluded "if I had not been, the whole world would not have been." The originated knowledge of God, he says, is in the form of the eternal knowledge, and that is none other than the perfect human: "For he is the spirit of the world, and the world, in its higher and lower parts, is made ready for him." He likens the resemblance between the animal human and the perfect human to the monkey's resemblance to man in its outward limbs (p. 287). He notes that all the divine names given to God are given to him too, and none is left outside. A thing can be known only by itself or by its like, he says, so the originated being must be in the form of the One who brought it into being (pp. 287-288).

Dost says the perfect human, though many as individuals, is a single reality: God says of him "There is nothing like the like of Him," so He made him a likeness and denied him likeness. The distinction among the names is this: the name that belongs to the Real and not to man is "Allah"; the name that belongs to man and not to the Real is "vicegerent," that is, the perfect human. He notes that the other names are composed of spirit and form, and by their form they point to man, by their meaning to God (p. 288). He cites the hadith "I was a treasure and was not known; I wished to be known, so I created creation, made Myself known to them, and they knew Me," and says: "The Real hides Himself in the form of the perfect human, that is, in his thingness of fixity (the fixed entity)." When the garment of the thingness of existence is put on him, the treasure comes out with man's manifestation (p. 289).

He says that in the verse "He taught Adam all the names" the word "all" signifies comprehensiveness and generality. Taking the phrase "nafs-ka" in the Prophet's supplication "O God, I ask You by all the names with which You have named Yourself" as a thing's annexation to itself, he gives two readings: the annexation is between two forms, or the soul is the soul of the perfect human, who receives all the divine and cosmic names (p. 289). As an example of this reading he gives Jesus' words "You know what is in my soul, and I do not know what is in Your soul"; he notes that Jesus knew he was a vicegerent and a perfect human to whom the divine names belong, and therefore did not say "I did not say I am a god" (p. 290). He relates the prayer's phrase "the names You reserved for Yourself in the knowledge of Your unseen" to the perfect human's being the Real's unseen (p. 290).

He says that in relation to the world man resembles a specialist expert in each branch of knowledge, and that by his comprehensiveness he is superior to the other. He notes that the angel surpasses man in obedience because it is free of the garment of humanness, whereas man, being made of elements, has the trait of heedlessness and forgetfulness (p. 291). He gives the reason for Adam's superiority in knowing the names: "For the vicegerent, unless he appears with the names that are the right of the one who made him vicegerent, by which he is disobeyed and obeyed, is not a vicegerent." Man is complete and superior in comprehensiveness, while the angel is superior in one respect or two (p. 291).

At the end of the chapter comes the usual "this is learned from this station" list: effaced traces and their ranks; the state of one who, having rejected a command, comes to the point of killing himself, and its relation to perfection; the knowledge of calming; "Everyone who commits a crime commits it against himself"; temperaments, and how what harms Zayd benefits 'Amr; why the disputant goes on disputing though he knows his opponent is right; being content with the decree; the effect of one existent upon another; the principle of prophethood and whether anyone in the world stands outside obligation; the change of verses with the change of times and states; the differences between femaleness and maleness; the ranks of brotherhood; the divine scales. He gives the verse "If this is the truth from You, rain stones on us" as an example of a person who knows the possible; the difference between verse and prose and whence the spirits of fire descend are also listed (pp. 292-294).

The Perfect Human: Likeness, Opposition, Contrariety

Chapter 359 defines three kinds of relation — likeness, opposition (friendly contrary), and contrariety (hostile contrary) — and grounds them in examples: "the likeness of two likenesses is two whites, the likeness of two contraries is white and black, the likeness of two opposites is scent and color." (p. 296) It then states: "the perfect human has gathered all of these in himself by his very nature. This trait is found in no one else. He is likeness, opposition, and contrariety before the Real." (pp. 296-297) The perfect human is described as the only being capable of bearing all three relational forms at once.

Chapter 359 bears the title "I tell it to you, my daughter; hear it, my daughter-in-law" (p. 295). Dost says God created for each thing a contrary, a likeness and an opposite, and placed agreement in the opposite, repulsion in the contrary and affinity in the likeness. In the likeness's relation to its likeness, mutual affinity joins them in love; as for the contrary and its contrary, "their repelling one another (repulsion) is essential" and each wants the other to vanish from existence. Two opposites are not like this: the love between them does not allow one to desire the other's annihilation, yet by their nature they never come together (pp. 295-296). He notes that the aim in setting out these distinctions is to explain to which of them the servant's place before God is related (p. 296).

Dost explains the perfect human's standing before the Real through three relations: he is a likeness in being created in the divine form, a contrary in being a servant who cannot be the lord of the one whose servant he is, and an opposite in that the Real is his hearing, his sight and his powers. He notes that the saying "Whoever knows himself knows his Lord" means knowing through the knowledge of likeness, contrariety and opposition (pp. 296-297). He explains the verse "Do not take My enemy and your enemy as friends" as treating the enemy with the stance of contrariety and not of the love of likeness, as though he were your likeness; he says that to tell the essential enemy from the accidental one a scale and sound unveiling are needed (p. 297).

The Heir, Ijtihad, Friendship and Love

He says people differ in degrees of perfection, the lowest of the station being the animal human and the highest "God's shadow": "He is the perfect human and the Real's deputy. The Real is his tongue and his powers." In the time of the messengers the perfect human was the messenger, and after him the perfect one is the heir; non-prophets too can receive the divine descent in their hearts, and such people are called "heirs," not "messengers." Ijtihad, in Dost's words, is "expending every effort to bring about the disposition in the inward"; with that disposition man receives the divine descent (inspiration) that comes to his heart (p. 298). He notes that the true mujtahid is not one who issues a ruling but one who transmits the truth that comes to him in his self-disclosure, and that his sign, because of the oneness of messengership in this age, is that he does not fall into disagreement in his ruling (pp. 298-299).

He says the divine names too contain likeness, opposition and contrariety: al-Rahman and al-Rahim are likenesses, al-Rahman and al-Sabur are opposites, al-Darr and al-Nafi' are contraries. After citing the Prophet's saying "Were I to take a friend, I would take Abu Bakr as a friend, but your companion is the friend of God" and the verse "God took Abraham as a friend," he says that one who learns that God's taking His servants as friends is not impossible should strive to be His friend; the way is to follow the Real's Law, and whoever makes this a characteristic can reach love, a state more particular than friendship: "The friend is a friend with you for your sake, while the lover is with you for his own sake. How great the difference between friendship and love!" (p. 299) Then he gives the verses that begin "I swear, we have eaten bread and salt together" (p. 300).

He describes the right of bread and salt as a habit God created in people: they make sacrifices for a companion with whom they have shared bread and salt. He says the addition "and your enemy" after "Do not take My enemy as friends" comes to make God's enemies unlovable, and that in the phrase "They drive out the Messenger" the Messenger's expulsion from his homeland is mentioned because leaving one's homeland is among the harshest things that can befall a person (p. 301). He relates that Abraham showed his father Azar tenderness and counsel, and when he learned through revelation that his father was an enemy of God he turned from him, as the verse recalls: "When it became clear to him that he was an enemy of God, he disowned him." (p. 301) He adds that for one in whom this station has not been realized by taste, there is an affinity between him and the enemy of God, and that before the enmity became clear he was excused (p. 302).

Among the knowledges of the station he lists: the increase of good, the trait that separates the Real from the false and the limits (definitions that separate things), servants of the names and servants of the pronouns, the science of transcendence, and the cause in the world that calls for taking partners. Taking the hadith that every child is born in the innate nature and his parents make him a Jew, a Christian, a polytheist or a Magian, he asks whether the intellect plays the parents' role when a person leaves the innate nature for polytheism (pp. 302-303). On the shaykh and the disciple, he explains that the shaykh is obliged to hide from the disciple that the disciple possesses the meaning that calls for the form: if he tells him, the disciple will appear with the form without the meaning, and errors and confusions arise (pp. 303-304). He gives the example of the shadow in the mirage: when you come near you do not find it as you saw it, yet you do not doubt what you saw (p. 305). He says that when the Batinis are told "God exists" they flee to saying "He is not non-existent," which creates a partnership with originated existence (p. 305).

Light, Darkness and the World as Isthmus

The title of Chapter 360 is "Knowledge of the Station of Praised Darknesses and Visible Lights" (p. 308). Dost says light makes perception possible but cannot itself be perceived, while darkness does not make perception possible but is itself perceived. When the Prophet was asked "Did you see your Lord?" he said "He was light; how could I see?" (p. 308). The Real is pure light, and the impossible is pure darkness: "The world is the isthmus between light and darkness. As it is not described by darkness in its essence, so it is not described by light." (p. 309)

He relates that God created two eyes in man; with the first man receives light, with the other darkness, while in himself he is neither darkness nor light. The possible is described as bewildered between non-existence and existence, since it cannot cross completely to either extreme (pp. 309-310). He separates the customary senses of light and darkness: in the sensory realm, the light of lightning, stars and lamps; in the inward, the light of the intellect, of faith and of knowledge, and the darkness of ignorance, of associating partners and of folly; what is neither light nor darkness is doubt, conjecture, bewilderment and speculative inquiry (p. 310). He says knowledge about the possible is "a vast sea of knowledge with great waves," a sea with no shore other than its two extremes (p. 310).

The Point and the Circle: Dost's Own Drawn Diagram

In Chapter 360, Dost explains the relation between the Real, the possible, and non-existence through a point-and-circumference diagram he draws himself. "The point is the Real; the space outside the circumference is non-existence or darkness. Between the point and the space outside the circumference lies the possible... We mentioned the point because the point is the origin of the circle's circumference. Just as the circle emerges through the point, the possible emerges through the Real." (pp. 313-314) It continues: "The point from which the line extends toward the circumference is the line's beginning. It is thus the first and the last... The lines and possibles that emerge begin from God and return to Him again. 'All matters return to Him.'" (p. 314).

Dost says the lines extending from the point to the circumference end at a point, and that the circumference as a whole stands to the point in this position; he cites the verses "God encompasses them from behind" and "He encompasses all things." Of the beginning and end of the line he says "it is of the line and not of the line; say it as you wish," likening it to the case of the attributes of which the Ash'arites say "neither He nor other than He." He links the saying "Whoever knows himself knows his Lord" to the verse "We shall show them Our signs in the horizons and in themselves": "For the whole world outside you is the horizons, and they are your circumference." (p. 311)

He explains that the line is composed of points, the surface of lines and the body of surfaces: the name "line" arises from two or more points, a surface from four points, and a body in actuality from eight. He compares this with what we know of God, the essence and seven attributes: "As the body is nothing other than points, so the points are nothing other than the body. Yet at the same time the body is not the points, and the points are not the body." (pp. 311-312) He says the body's length comes from the line, its breadth from the surface and its depth from the joining of two surfaces, and that the Real brought the world into being through three realities, namely His identity, His turning and His word ("Be"). The world is "light upon light, darkness upon darkness," for to every light there corresponds a darkness, and to every existence a non-existence (p. 312).

He says existence is a single reality which the outward existences of the possibles multiply: "Existence is one-many... Just as we exist dependently. Without the Real we would not be." He recalls the verse "God is independent of the worlds" together with the lord-and-ruled relation, in respect of the Real's being God (pp. 312-313). He notes that the possible can look only at the veil, and that if the veils were lifted possibility would vanish. Taking up the hadith "Were the veils lifted, the glories of His face would burn everything His sight reaches," he says the veils remain drawn; had people understood this hadith they would have known themselves, and so the Real. He relates that al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi knew the Real in this way and named this unveiling "the kingdom of the kingdom" (p. 313).

Without Light There Would Be No Perception, and the Spiritual Darknesses

In the vasl titled "Without Light Nothing Would Be Perceived," Dost says the names given to light change according to the faculties: when what is heard is perceived, this light is called "hearing"; when what is seen is perceived, "sight"; when what is touched is perceived, "touch." "So whatever makes perception possible is this light." (p. 314) Everything perceived has a relation to light; he distinguishes the rulings of the necessary and the impossible, saying that the possible falls under the rulings of the necessary and the impossible while the necessary and the impossible do not fall under the ruling of possibility (p. 315). He ties the imperceptibility in the Prophet's saying "He was light; how could I perceive?" to the light of perception being contained in him; a part cannot perceive the whole, so while the Real knows the parts, the parts cannot know Him (p. 316).

He lists the spiritual darknesses: the darkness of knowledge, the darkness of allusion, the darkness of equating two states, the darkness of denial and the darkness of doubt. Where ignorance is taken as the negation of knowledge by a certain state, he says everyone other than God is ignorant, which is why God commanded His Prophet to say "My Lord, increase me in knowledge" (p. 317). He takes up the teaching of the names to Adam and the angels' words "Will You place in it one who will spread corruption?"; he notes that the question put to Jesus, "Did you say to people...," was asked to set out the proof against those who worshipped him and to rebuke them (pp. 317-318). He relates that for the Sufis allusion is "a call made from afar" (p. 319). He calls the darkness that appears when God forgives a person who died without repenting while believing the threat would be carried out the darkness of doubt (pp. 319-320).

He says God brought man out of the darkness of the unseen in which he was, that all the possibles are in the darkness of the unseen, and that each possible has a key which only God knows: "The first key with which He opens an unseen is the perfect human, who is God's shadow among existents other than Himself." God manifested him from the Breath of the All-Merciful; the sura Ya Sin, the heart of the Qur'an, is he, and "Ya Sin" is a call meaning "O master." The outward of man is the extended shadow, his inward the part that stays in the unseen: "For this reason the inward of man can never be known." (pp. 321-322)

Deputyship to the Real: Ten Ranks

In the vasl titled "Explanation of the Ranks of the Ten Categories," Dost says God gave this perfection to the perfect human, who exists according to the divine form, so that he might stand in the Real's place, and that is why he is called "vicegerent" (p. 323). He then lists ten deputyships. In the first, those who come after him among his kind are the vicegerent's vicegerents; the ten categories hold true in his name, and this number admits no addition (pp. 323-324). The second is man's deputizing, by his essence and spirituality, for half of the form: the angel, animal, mineral and plant outside man have no will attached to anything, "they move according to their innate nature, and the requirement of this nature is prostration to God and praising Him." (p. 324)

The third deputyship is the realization of the matter that lies in the possible and brings it from non-existence to existence. He explains that the possibles in their states of non-existence are before the Real, that the Real looks at them with names such as al-Alim and al-Hafiz, and that while no order obtains among the fixed entities, entry into the thingness of existence follows an order (pp. 324-325). He says resolve stands in the place of the word "Be," and that the deputy is the one into whose heart the Real puts the knowledge of the possibles He wills to bring into being; this knowledge comes either by thought, understanding from the names al-Mudabbir and al-Mufassil, or as a notion inspired into the inward (pp. 325-326). In the fourth deputyship, which concerns the Real's wish to be known through the proof He has placed, the verse is "We shall show them Our signs in the horizons," followed by "and in themselves," and the perfect human becomes, by unveiling and witnessing, a proof nearer than the world (p. 327).

In the fourth deputyship Dost says the shadow is in the form of the one from whom it issues and that the creation of the perfect human is a mercy. He reads Abu Yazid al-Bistami's saying "My punishing is harsher than your punishing" as punishing by way of his animal soul: the Real punishes with what is created, mercy is immanent in His punishing, and the punishments set in this world aim at purification (p. 328). He relates that only the perfect human can know the Real: "The intellect of the angel or the animal human has not reached this knowledge of God, for God has veiled all of them from Himself and has appeared to the perfect human." (p. 329) He adds that denial of God arises from the intellect's having delimited the Real, and that had the perfect human not been set in the world no one could deny Him (pp. 329-330).

The fifth deputyship is man's deputizing in the world for the name Raf'i al-darajat: "As no one but God can know him, so no one but the perfect human can know God." Since a part cannot know the whole, no created being can know the perfect human; a whole can know its like and the parts it contains (pp. 330-331). Dost says that if the oneness of the perfect human is multiplied by the oneness of the Real, one oneness would come out, and he asks: "Which oneness is yours? [...] The deputy's oneness, or the oneness of the one who appointed him vicegerent?" (p. 331) In the sixth deputyship he relates that God describes Himself as having many words, and that in distinguishing among words the perfect human takes the Real's place; in the verse of "Be," two of the three letters appear and one is hidden (p. 332). He also notes that Jesus is called "words" because he is many in his outward and inward make-up (pp. 332-333).

He relates that precedence, delay and order in speech are found also in the existents that are the loci of the divine words, that al-Dahr brings out this ordering, and that the Prophet said "Do not curse time, for time is God." He cites a verse: "Speech is in the heart, but the tongue became its proof." He says the listener's share in the speaker's words, apart from those who understand from God, is often only hearing the order of the letters, and that understanding is a state added to the hearing of the word (pp. 333-334). He notes that man is one in his general definition, yet we know that Zayd is not 'Umar, and that only the word "Be" runs through everything given existence; for this reason God is the continual creator and continual preserver (pp. 334-335).

The seventh deputyship is deputyship in the outward and inward acts within man's own being; its basis is the verse "that We may know." Dost describes a prayer that fills the heart entirely and is answered at once as a state that tells, by taste and experience, the truthfulness of the servant's turning to the Real, and counts the testing of one who makes a claim as deputyship to God (pp. 335-336). He says that by answering "Yes" to "Am I not your Lord?" we acknowledged servanthood, that for a rational person who is not a believer too the intellect stands in the place of a messenger, declaring the Creator transcendent and one (pp. 336-337). He notes that in the hadith "Whoever dies knowing that there is no god but God enters Paradise" the Prophet mentions "knowledge," and says that those who remain in Hell for ever are only those who associate partners with God and the imitating polytheists who neglected inquiry, and the divestors (pp. 337-338).

He presents the Prophet's prayer "My Lord, forgive and have mercy, for You are the best of those who show mercy" as intercession for those who, unknowing, stopped at the limit of speculative inquiry, and counts it as a prayer covering all from Adam to the swoon-blast Israfil will blow (p. 338). Dost asks the reader not to forget his share of this intercession and not to be among those who claim that God's mercy reaches only believers (p. 338). One aspect of prophethood has ceased and another continues: "Prophethood in its outward form is a prophethood whose appearance has ceased, while inward prophethood is continuous in this world and the next." As evidence he cites "Your Lord inspired the bee," the ant's saying "O ants," and the hoopoe's words (p. 339).

The eighth deputyship is that which makes the Real's oneness a pair: the Real sees Himself in His perfection in the mirror of the perfect human, and he sees himself in the Real in perfection; the Real and man sit enthroned facing each other on their thrones, like the people of Paradise "on couches facing one another" (p. 339). He says the fixed entity is like a seed in non-existence and a tree in existence, in these verses: "The fixed entity is always a servant to God / The Real is always its object of worship." (p. 340) In the ninth deputyship he says the intelligible isthmus between two likenesses is like the shadow between a person and his extended shadow, and that creation appears through the Real while the Real does not appear through creation (p. 341). Because heedlessness and forgetfulness are states that come with man's creation, he says the transfer of agency is needed, and that the verse "Lord of the east and the west; there is no god but Him, so take Him as your trustee" points to disposal in the outward and the inward (p. 342). On this basis he says "you are the vicegerent of the vicegerent." (p. 343)

The tenth deputyship is the deputyship of the dead in the oneness of God: with death the coverings are opened and the truth shows itself to everyone. He explains the saying of one of the Companions, "Were the veil lifted, my certainty would not increase," as meaning that on seeing what he knew there would be no increase in certainty (pp. 343-344). Taking up Moses seeing the magicians' ropes running, he distinguishes between the magician, who changes the look, and the deputy who holds the agency, who changes the thing looked at; the knowledge on which the magician relies he calls "alchemy" (pp. 344-345). As to miracles, for the verifiers these are not extraordinary but a bringing about of events: "There is no repetition in existence that would make something a habit!" (p. 345) He divides death into necessary death and voluntary death; the greater struggle is the struggle with the soul, and God provides for one who kills his soul by the ruling of martyrdom (pp. 346-347).

The Light of the Oneness of the Essence, the Oneness of Multiplicity and the Oneness of Annexation

Describing "the light of the oneness of the essence," Dost says the "one" contains in potential the oneness of everything that appears in the world, and that number appears with one and vanishes when one vanishes: "Were it not for God, the world would not exist outwardly." He relates that God gave Himself both the name of essence and the name of soul (nafs), that in the verse "Our word to a thing" the "word" is masculine and the will feminine, and that the world came to be from word and will (p. 347). He explains the verse "He grants females to whom He wills, males to whom He wills, or gives them as pairs" as attention to the locus, to the one who inseminates, and to both (p. 348). Though God is independent of the worlds, since He is described by generosity and mercy the object of mercy must exist; God brought the world into being from His generosity (pp. 348-349). "The lights of the essence are nothing other than the existents. They are none other than the self-disclosures of the essence." (p. 349)

On the oneness of annexation, he explains that the possible joins the necessary, that the necessary makes the relations and names it has in itself necessary for the possible, that imagination brings into being everything other than God, and that the perfect human too can bring into being within himself the objects of knowledge other than himself (pp. 350-351). He notes that this oneness can also be called the oneness of union and connection, and cites these verses: "The glass grew thin, the wine grew thin / They resembled each other and the matter became confused." (p. 351) He says the particle "ka" in the verse "There is nothing like Him (ka-mithlihi)" signifies a quality and is not an added letter, though some people, fearing falling into likening, have counted it as superfluous (pp. 351-352).

The Knowledges Listed at the End of the Chapter

At the end of the chapter he lists, as usual, what is learned from the station. He covers the intellect's making itself a sign first and being the first proof; the limits, in which there is a limit of the outward, of the inward, of the rising-place and a limit of the limit; the prohibition on giving examples of God ("for God knows and you do not"), and that those who may give examples are only the prophets and the friends of God (pp. 352-353). He takes up why four witnesses are sometimes required, why proof is asked of one who makes a claim, that confessing something that calls for a hadd penalty is a claim, and that the one who confesses may sometimes have lied, supposing he would be spared a greater pain (pp. 353-354). On the shaykh's attitude to a disciple without aptitude he relates three views; the one he himself prefers is that the shaykh spend every effort and fulfil his duty ("I do more than seventy") (p. 355). He recounts that Abu al-Abbas al-Sabti of Marrakesh advised a man who complained of poverty to marry four times, and that the man finally became rich (p. 356).

Black and White United in Color

In Chapter 359 these lines appear: "the gradation between two opposites is known from this station. As an example we may cite the gradation between black and white. This gradation arises because color unites them." (p. 305).

Being with the Real Without Seeking Recompense

Chapters 358 and 359 contain two passages on the question of wage/recompense. In 358, a question: "Who is superior in rank is known from this station: is it the one who stays far from seeking recompense, or the one who demands it?" (p. 279) And in 359's verse section, a direct counsel: "Be with the Real, and do not seek recompense from Him." (p. 302).
