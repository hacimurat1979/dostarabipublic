The Arrow's Return to the Archer: The Opening of Volume XII

pp. 13–69

Site reading summary

The opening four chapters of the Twenty-Third Sifr bring the Mosaic and Muhammadan rank-labels back into the chapter titles; they read existence as a "wage" relationship binding the possible and the Real in mutual claim; they offer an archer's arrow, reaching its target and returning to the one who shot it, as an image of "deeds returning to their doer"; and they arrive, through a personal experience with Surah al-Ikhlas, at the statement that "the matter ended as it began, its end the same as its beginning."

Read the part

Two Poles of Revelation: Mosaic Opening, Muhammadan Close

The last two chapters of Volume XI carried no Muhammadan/Mosaic rank label in their titles. At the opening of Volume XII, however, Chapter 342's title opens directly with "On the Knowledge, from the Mosaic Rank, of Two Secrets Distinct from Three Secrets United by One of the Ranks of Revelation" (p. 15), and Chapter 344's with "On the Station, from the Muhammadan Rank, of Two Secrets Among the Secrets of Forgiveness" (p. 39).

Chapter 342 opens with the verse "There is nothing like Him" and the question of vicegerency. Recalling the ruling that when two caliphs are given allegiance the other is to be killed, Dost says: "You too must be removed so that He may remain! For you are the 'other' (mentioned in the hadith on vicegerency)!" (p. 13). On revelation: "Divine revelation descends from the loftiest, protected station of might, and for this reason revelation does not depend on acquisition and earning." (p. 15). Were it "sent down upon a mountain, you would see it crumble in fear"; yet the prophets and the people of God are not shaken even when they hear a child ascribed to the Merciful, because "nothing is stronger than knowledge" (p. 15).

Dost mentions Noah's prayer, "for whoever enters my house as a believer" (p. 16), alongside the angels' prayer, "Forgive those who repent and follow Your way"; he says this group of angels was "in the station of divine courtesy" and preferred the divine rank to the creatures (p. 16). On disagreement: "One aspect of God's mercy toward His servants appears in their leaving the ruling on the matter they disagree about to God." (p. 17). In the knowledge of reality, however, "the divine names, especially names of opposite meaning, are the cause of disagreement" (p. 17).

Existence's Wage: The Possible Labors, the Real Claims

Chapter 342 shifts, under a "VASIL" heading, from revelation to a "wage" theory. While still in non-existence, the possible says to the Necessarily Existent, "I wish to employ you so that I may come to exist outwardly"; the Real answers: "If I do as you ask and manifest you outwardly, I will have a claim upon you." (pp. 20-21) That claim is the possible's worship of the Real, its praise and gratitude toward Him — "the possible accepted this wage, and God brought it into existence." (p. 21).

Dost applies the wage theory to the prophets: "The fulfilment of prophetic duties is the work of the one who performs it, for the sender has employed the prophet to deliver his message. So the prophet's (messenger's) wage must be paid by the one who sent him." (p. 20). Hence the prophets said, "Say: I ask you for no wage; my wage will be paid by God" (p. 21). The Prophet, however, was made to say, "I ask only for love toward those who are near" (p. 21). Dost stresses the distinction between "qurba" and "qaraba": "If those related by lineage do not have closeness in faith, they have no right to the spoils, just as they have no share in inheritance." (p. 22).

Dost lists the sciences of the station. Among them: "The quality that sets the world of the elements apart from the world of the spheres" (p. 23); the gradation within the Qur'an: "While giving one sura a quality equal to the Qur'an ten times over, another sura equals half the Qur'an in ruling, another a third, a quarter; one verse is made master over the other verses, one verse takes the place in the Qur'an that the heart has in the human body." (p. 25); "The brotherhood among the suras of the Qur'an", with the Prophet's saying, "Hud and its brothers have aged me" (p. 25); "The word kalima is derived from kalm, meaning wound" (p. 26).

The Scale: Mercy or Wrath

Chapters 343-344 work through the simple and compound division of mercy via the names al-Mudabbir and al-Mufassil, and the numbered degrees of Paradise and Hell; the scale between mercy and wrath is consistently set to tip toward mercy. On the same subject, Chapter 345 contains this: "God forgives all sins... on what basis would punishment be eternal for sinners?" (p. 61). Chapter 343 also gives space to the question of the possible salvation of the people of the Trinity (Christians) (pp. 32-33).

Chapter 343 gives al-Mudabbir and al-Mufassil as the two heads of the station (p. 28). On mercy: "God has ordained two mercies, which are the first things He created: the first is simple, the other compound." (p. 28). Three stations are ordained for compound mercy: with the first the parts of bodies are joined, with the second meanings, attributes and dispositions are composed, with the third rational souls are joined to bodies to govern them (pp. 28-29). One of the hundred mercies is in this world: "This mercy is the source of every animal in the world showing compassion to its young, and it is through this mercy that people show compassion and affection to one another." (p. 30). On Hell he recalls the verse "We made Hell a prison (hasir) for the unbelievers" and says "the mahsur is one whose action has been barred and who is held back" (p. 30).

On the blessing given to those in Hell: "God gives those in Hell the blessing of the one who delights in fire [...] and of the one who delights in cold; the first's blessing depends on the existence of fire, the other's on the existence of zamharir." (p. 31). On the people of the Trinity: "As for the people of the Trinity, salvation is hoped for on their behalf! The reason is the oneness within the Trinity (fardiyya), for fardiyya is one of the attributes of the One." (p. 32). Dost also relates his own unveiling: "We were able to tell the people of the Trinity and the people of tawhid apart at the rank of singularity." (p. 32). On the Fatiha: "God did not say, 'I divided the Fatiha,' but 'I divided the prayer'"; Dost counts this as "the strongest evidence that reciting the Fatiha in prayer is obligatory" (p. 34).

The Arrow's Return to the Archer: Deeds Returning to Their Doer

Within Chapter 344's long list of "this is known from this station" clauses, Dost shares a personal memory: "That the arrow returns to the one who shot it is known from this station. I learned this from someone skilled in archery in Tlemcen. I had watched him shoot his arrow. When the arrow reached its target, it returned to the one who shot it. This was, for me, an instructive example concerning deeds returning to those who do them." (p. 54).

Chapter 344 relates, with its chain of transmitters, the Prophet's hadith: "My community is a community that has received mercy. There is no punishment for them in the hereafter. Their punishments in this world are earthquakes, death and affliction." (p. 39). He recalls the hadith on the people of Hell: "They will neither die there nor remain alive. But the fire strikes people in measure of their sins." (p. 40). On the scale: "When God weighed mercy and wrath, mercy outweighed and was preferred, and divine wrath was lifted." (p. 41). Dost compares the two paths along which the soul journeys: the soul that proceeds by thought says, "This is the goal, and these alone exist!" (p. 43); the one that follows revealed law imitates the Lawgiver, and "there is no goal to be reached beyond God!" (p. 44). He concludes: "The people of thought remain at the rank of nature below the soul, and nothing is reflected into their souls from above this rank. But into the souls of those who journey by the lawful path, it is reflected from here." (p. 44).

Dost distinguishes between "God" and "deity": "The polytheists did not deny God. Had they denied Him, they would not have been polytheists to begin with. [...] So they associated partners not with God but with the Deity." (p. 47). He cites as examples the Samiri's words, "This is your god and Moses' god," and Pharaoh's, "Perhaps I may gain some knowledge about Moses' god" (p. 47). On the end of the ranks: "The ranks of the Real have no end, for just as the world's subsistence has no end, there is no limit to disclosure in form." (p. 48). The two ranks of forgiveness: "The first is sinners being covered from the punishment of sin reaching them; the other rank is covering them from sin striking them." (p. 49).

The Secret of Surah al-Ikhlas: "That Is Me"

Chapter 345 opens with a personal experience Dost had in Aleppo: when Surah al-Ikhlas was disclosed to him, "I saw that the sura was my very essence and my own reality — it was none other than me! ... At this I said: 'That is me.'" (p. 56). From here he traces the root of the word ikhlas (purification) and works through the sura's two names — Ahad (absolute oneness, sharing nothing with anything) and Samad (the one turned to, on whom all causes depend): "God is Samad. He is the one turned to in all matters... because all causes depend on the first Samad to deliver their effects." (pp. 57-58)

Dost explains the name "Ahad": "He described God by ahadiyya, that is, the quality of absolute oneness. Yet every part of the world has an ahadiyya of its own, that is, a oneness, and in it shares nothing with anything." (p. 56). On "Nothing is equal to Him": "Here he means the spouse [...]. Equality is likeness. A woman is never equal to a man." (p. 57); "The receptive is not equal to the agent. The world is God's receptive, and therefore not equal to God." (p. 57). On the meaning of the sura's name: "It purified the Real from the declaration of transcendence (tanzih), which is reason's ruling about God for the sake of the world, and then, in the same reality, separated Him from the sum of the world's attributes." (p. 57).

Dost explains the secret of ikhlas as the secret of destiny: "The secret of ikhlas is the secret of destiny, in which God has hidden His knowledge from the world, or rather from most of those in it." (p. 57). On the eternal and the temporal: "The eternal is the known-unknown. Only He, exalted be His majesty, is unknown just as He is known." (p. 57) and "To know Him is not to know Him." (p. 58). On the meaning of sincerity in religion: "Sincerity in religion means 'a fitting recompense.'" (p. 58); the recompense in Paradise for a deed that issues unintended from its doer may be "a blessing that no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and that has not occurred to any created being's mind" (p. 58).

The Matter Ended as It Began: The Circle Closes

Chapter 345, having defined "sincerity in religion" as "a fitting recompense" (a penalty or reward exactly matched to the deed), goes on to say that the soul is good by nature and accepts evil only under some outside pressure, closing with this sentence: "The soul is created in this nature, and for this reason returns in the end to its origin... Thus mercy reaches the soul in the end. Indeed, its very existence was mercy itself. Thus the matter ended as it began, its end the same as its beginning." (pp. 59-60).

Dost explains the word din as "'ada (to return, to go back to its owner)" and relates the Prophet's saying, "Goodness is habit, evil is persistence." (p. 58). On the soul: "The soul is good in itself, because its father is the pure and holy spirit." (p. 59). On forgiveness: "If the One who is Forgiving and Compassionate in Himself has forgiven a sin, He has forgiven it continually, and the ruling of the sin never returns to the servant." (p. 61); "This subject is the essence of gnosis." (p. 61). On the hadith "I seek refuge in You from You": "Seeing his soul in the form of the Real, he said 'from You,' and sought refuge in God from his own soul." (p. 61).

Dost relates that on the Day of Resurrection the Real asks the prophets, "How were you answered?" and they reply, "We have no knowledge; You know best the unseen" (pp. 62-63). He draws from this: "It is not necessary for unveiling to be general enough to extend to everything." (p. 63). On how to treat the people of unveiling: "We do not raise the person of unveiling above his rank, and we do not demand of him what is not the due of his state." (p. 64).
