  • Slusher 1985 — Slusher, Observation of squeezed states generated by four-wave mixing in an optical cavity. Phys. Rev. Lett. 55, 2409
    In brief, what does it say?

    Even in empty space the field of light is not perfectly calm; according to quantum theory there is always a small fluctuation, 'vacuum noise'. The question was this: can a state of light be made that goes below this noise? Slusher and his colleagues used, inside an optical cavity with sodium atoms, a process called 'four-wave mixing'. In this process, while one direction of the light field is amplified, the perpendicular direction is suppressed along with it: like squeezing one side of a balloon and the other side swelling (an aid to description). The measurement was made with a setup reading the two directions separately, and the noise in the suppressed direction fell below the usual noise of the vacuum. This state is called a 'squeezed state'. This is an experimental finding; it requires no interpretation but shows the limit of the principle: the product of the noises of the two directions cannot fall below a lower bound, but one of them can.

  • Spekkens 2007 — Spekkens, Evidence for the epistemic view of quantum states: A toy theory. Phys. Rev. A 75, 032110
    In brief, what does it say?

    Spekkens built a toy theory: a model based on a simple principle, which is not quantum mechanics but shows behaviors similar to it. The principle is this: even if we have the most knowledge possible about a system, half of the questions that could be asked remain unanswered. With this single rule, many peculiarities of the quantum reappear in the model: the dependence of measurements on order, interference, the impossibility of copying an unknown state, teleportation, and others. According to the author, so many similarities can be taken as evidence for the view that quantum states are states not of the world but of incomplete knowledge. But there are also things the model cannot reproduce: the violation of Bell's inequality and the Kochen-Specker theorem. So this is a theoretical study; it shows similarity, it does not give a definitive result. What gives one pause is how much quantum appearance 'not knowing' can produce once it is structured by a certain rule.

  • Steane 1996 — Steane, Multiple-particle interference and quantum error correction. Proceedings of the Royal Society A 452, 2551–2577
    In brief, what does it say?

    A paper presenting quantum error-correcting codes and their limits by treating multi-particle interference in the light of classical coding theory.

    The paper brings two things together: the mathematics of classical error-correcting codes and many-particle quantum interference. Using this connection, the author explains how error correction can be done in quantum communication channels and quantum computers, and evaluates its limits.

  • Susskind 1995 — Susskind, The world as a hologram. J. Math. Phys. 36, 6377
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: if we bring quantum theory and gravity together, how much information can be stored in a region? According to 't Hooft's proposal the three-dimensional world can be described completely by information written on a boundary surface, that is, on a two-dimensional projection; each very small piece of the surface carries only one discrete degree of freedom. In the paper Susskind summarizes this idea and draws a first, informal picture of how it could be applied. One consequence of this picture is that particles must grow in length as their energies become very high. The author also discusses string theory as a possible candidate for the realization of this idea. This is a theory paper; there is no experiment and the idea was later developed in different forms. What gives one pause is the thought that the information contained in what we call 'volume' cannot exceed that of the surface. How this holds in the real universe is still open.

  • Tegmark 2003 — Tegmark, Parallel universes. Sci. Am. 288(5), 40 (arXiv:astro-ph/0302131)
    In brief, what does it say?

    Tegmark lines up the idea of 'other universes' not as a single speculation but as the natural consequence of four separate theories in physics. At the first level, if space is infinitely large, there are infinitely many regions of the same size as the one we can see, and among them there may be copies very similar to ours, even identical. At the second level, in some forms of inflation theory, other regions of space may have formed with different constants of nature and different sets of particles. The third level is the 'many worlds' reading of quantum mechanics; the paper says this reading adds nothing new and merely accepts the consequences of the same equations. At the fourth level it is thought that different mathematical structures give different physical equations. The author discusses how testable these are, and openly admits that as long as we do not know how to count probabilities (the measure problem), no testable prediction comes from the higher levels. What gives one pause is that the phrase 'other universe' is taken seriously here not as science fiction but as a possibility arising from equations; yet the distance between possibility and evidence is not hidden in the paper either.

  • Thomas 1926 — Thomas, The Motion of the Spinning Electron. Nature 117, 514
  • Tonomura 1989 — Tonomura, Demonstration of single-electron buildup of an interference pattern. Am. J. Phys. 57, 117
    In brief, what does it say?

    Does an electron behave like a wave or like a particle? This paper describes the famous double-slit experiment as it was actually carried out with electrons. The researchers placed in an electron microscope a thin wire that joins the two paths (a biprism) and a detector that counts the arriving electrons one by one. The electrons were sent so sparsely that at most one electron was in the apparatus at any moment. On the screen, at first, single dots appear scattered at random; each dot lands in one place, like a particle. But as thousands of dots accumulate, light and dark stripes emerge among them: the interference pattern peculiar to waves. This experiment is not an interpretation of the theory but a directly observed result; what gives one pause is that even a lone electron leaves a trace as if it had 'passed through both paths at once'. What this means is a matter of interpretation; the experiment shows only the pattern.

  • Tsui 1982 — Tsui, Two-Dimensional Magnetotransport in the Extreme Quantum Limit. Physical Review Letters 48, 1559-1562
    In brief, what does it say?

    Tsui, Störmer and Gossard observed that the Hall resistance forms fractional steps at a very clean semiconductor interface.

    The experiment was carried out on a two-dimensional electron gas accumulated at the interface between the layers of a structure made of gallium arsenide and aluminium gallium arsenide. The sample was cooled to a very low temperature (below 1 kelvin) and placed in a very strong magnetic field; the "extreme quantum limit" mentioned in the title describes this regime: all the electrons fit into the lowest-energy magnetic level.

  • von Neumann 1932 — von Neumann, Mathematische Grundlagen der Quantenmechanik. Springer, Berlin (1932; reprint 1996)
    In brief, what does it say?

    Von Neumann placed quantum mechanics on a sound mathematical foundation over Hilbert space and for the first time analyzed the measurement process in detail within the theory (the book Mathematische Grundlagen der Quantenmechanik).

    This work is not a paper but a book. Its aim is to gather quantum mechanics, independently of the various calculation methods used until then (matrix, wave), into a single mathematical framework. The framework is a vector space called Hilbert space: states are vectors in this space, and measurable quantities are certain operators acting on the space.

  • Weinberg 1989 — Weinberg, The cosmological constant problem. Rev. Mod. Phys. 61, 1
    In brief, what does it say?

    Even empty space may have an energy; this is called the cosmological constant, and it affects the expansion rate of the universe. Astronomical observations show that this value is very small. Yet when the theories of particle physics calculate the energy expected from vibrations in the vacuum, they obtain a number far larger than what is observed; the difference is so great that it takes many digits to write. In this paper Weinberg briefly recounts the history of the problem and reviews five approaches put forward to solve it; none was considered a full solution at the time. Observation and calculation are firm; an explanation is either absent or disputed. What gives one pause is that one of the greatest numerical mismatches in physics arises at the place where two well-established theories (quantum fields and general relativity) meet.

  • Wootters 1982 — Wootters, A single quantum cannot be cloned. Nature 299, 802
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was: can a machine be built that duplicates an unknown quantum state exactly? Wootters and Zurek showed, starting from the linearity of the equations of quantum theory, that it cannot. That is, a perfect second copy of a state we do not know contradicts the theory. This no-cloning theorem is a theoretical result; it is a consequence not of a particular experiment but of the structure of the theory. Re-preparing a known state, however, is possible; the prohibition concerns the copy of an unknown state. We do not have the paper's own summary; the generally known framework is given here.

  • Zurek 2003 — Zurek, Decoherence, einselection, and the quantum origins of the classical. Rev. Mod. Phys. 75, 715
    In brief, what does it say?

    Quantum theory describes the world of atoms, yet the tables and cats around us appear in 'either this or that' states, not both at once. How does this transition happen? Wojciech Zurek compiles studies arguing that it comes largely from interaction with the environment. The environment constantly 'watches' the system; because of this, the delicate coherence of states in which possibilities enter the calculation together (superposition) is disturbed, which is called decoherence. A few stable states that the environment does not disturb remain, and these appear like the states we see in the classical world; Zurek calls this 'einselection' (selection made by the environment). The theory and calculation here are undisputed; the real dispute is whether this explains why a single result emerges in measurement. That is, the article shows how the classical appearance arises, but does not close the 'single result' question for everyone.

  • Zurek 2009 — Zurek, Quantum Darwinism. Nat. Phys. 5, 181
    In brief, what does it say?

    Zurek's review in Nature Physics presenting the idea of Quantum Darwinism as a whole.

    Zurek approaches the quantum measurement problem from a new angle: the robustness of the classical world may arise from a fragile quantum state leaving many copies of traces in the environment. According to the paper's abstract, Quantum Darwinism describes the multiplying records in the environment of the selected states of a system. Thanks to these records, the fragility of a single quantum system turns into a classical durability in the many records that carry correlations about that system.

  • SEP: Uncertainty Principle — SEP, Uncertainty Principle. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    This is the entry on the uncertainty principle in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy; it is not an experiment but a well-organized introduction to the subject. Classical physics assumes that a definite value can be given to every property of a body at the same time. Quantum mechanics rejects this: the more precisely the position of a particle is stated, the more uncertain its velocity (momentum) remains, and the reverse also holds. The entry tells of the first and simple statement of this principle, the more rigorous forms that came later and the role it plays in philosophical debates. The following distinction stands out in particular: is the principle the disturbing effect of a measurement, or a property of the state itself? The entry shows that these two readings rest on different relations and that their consequences are separate. This is a review source; we report its verdict not in our own words but as far as it is told in the entry.

  • SEP: Bell's Theorem — SEP, Bell's Theorem. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    Bell's theorem is not a single result but a family of results. All proceed like this: from the idea of 'local causality' (a thing is affected only by effects near it) and a few auxiliary assumptions, a limit is derived for the relations between the results of measurements made far apart. These limits are called Bell inequalities. Quantum theory predicts relations that exceed this limit in some cases, and experiments show that it is exceeded. Therefore no theory that rests on the idea of local causality and also accepts the auxiliary assumptions can produce the quantum predictions. This encyclopedia entry is a summary of theory and philosophy, not an experiment; which of the assumptions is to be given up (locality, freedom of measurement choice, or something else) is disputed. What gives one pause is that the result raises not the question 'is quantum theory right' but 'which of our intuitions shall we give up.'

  • SEP: Decoherence — SEP, Decoherence. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    One of the best-known traces of quantum physics is interference: as in the double slit, probabilities strengthen or cancel one another. But in everyday life we see nothing like this. Decoherence theory examines how, when a system interacts with its environment, for example air, light or heat, the traces of interference are suppressed very quickly. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry tells this from two sides: first the mathematics of this suppression, second its relation to the measurement problem, the direction of time and the question of 'how the classical world emerges.' What is firm: that interaction with the environment practically destroys interference has also been shown by experiments. What is disputed: whether this is a solution to the measurement problem. Most authors say it is not enough by itself, because the result is still an 'all at once' state. The entry works through these distinctions together with the interpretive options.

  • SEP: Copenhagen Interpretation — SEP, Copenhagen Interpretation. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    This is the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the Copenhagen interpretation; the text provided consists only of the introduction. The introduction counts quantum mechanics as one of the most successful theories in history: it serves to calculate experimental results and to build advanced technology. But it is also a theory that strains our imagination; it seems to violate some principles of classical physics that have been part of common sense since the Renaissance. The Copenhagen interpretation is the first general attempt to understand the atomic world; its founder is mainly Niels Bohr, but Heisenberg and Born also contributed. The entry notes that Bohr and Heisenberg never fully agreed on how to understand the mathematical formalism, and that neither used the term 'Copenhagen interpretation' as a common name. The term is a label put on it later by those who opposed Bohr's idea of complementarity. The rest of the introduction is cut off in the text we have; we do not write the remainder.

  • SEP: Many-Worlds Interpretation — SEP, Many-Worlds Interpretation. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    One of the answers to the question of what happens in measurement in quantum theory is the 'many-worlds interpretation'. According to this interpretation, as a result of measurement one of the possibilities is not selected while the others are erased; every possibility is realized in its own world and these worlds continue independently of one another. The interpretation does not put a 'collapse' rule added to the equations of the theory, and promises to remove randomness in measurement and action at a distance. This is not an experiment but an interpretation; it is disputed because it cannot be distinguished experimentally from the other interpretations. The entry describes the objections to the interpretation and its defenses, and hard questions such as what probability means in this framework. (This is an encyclopedia entry; for detail the source should be consulted.)

  • SEP: Bohmian Mechanics — SEP, Bohmian Mechanics. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    Quantum theory tells only with probability where a particle will be. Bohmian mechanics (the de Broglie-Bohm theory, the 'pilot wave') proposes another way: the particle has a real position at every moment, and the wave function guides these particles. The rule is this: the wave evolves according to the usual quantum equation, and the velocity of the particles is determined by the wave. In this case, in the double-slit experiment the particle passes through only one of the slits, and where it will land is determined by its initial position and the wave; probability comes from our not knowing the initial positions. This is an interpretation (a variety of theory) and gives the same results as the usual quantum predictions; that is, it cannot be told apart by experiment. Its price: instantaneous effects between positions, that is, a nonlocal structure. This entry is an introduction from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.

  • SEP: Relational Quantum Mechanics — SEP, Relational Quantum Mechanics. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
    In brief, what does it say?

    This is the entry on relational quantum mechanics in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy; it is not an experiment but an introduction to an interpretation. The interpretation was put forward in 1996 and has drawn interest in recent years. Its starting point is this: the confusion in quantum theory is not resolved by adding a new equation, a new fact or an inaccessible reality; it is said that the theory proposes to us a radical renewal of our framework of thought. According to the entry, the role of the 'observer' in the traditional account can be played not only by large, classical bodies but by every physical system. Thus the value of a property is spoken of not by itself but together with which system it is relative to. This is a proposal for an interpretation; it cannot be told apart by experiment and is not accepted among physicists. The entry tells not that this is true but what it says.
