The Point and the Circumference: The Two Ends of the World

pp. 195–230

Site reading summary

Four chapters move from the name al-Hakam balancing the enmity among opposing divine names, through the world's creation between a point (the earth) and a circumference (the Throne) — with circles, pillars, and spheres between them —, from the teaching that it is God Himself who unites two opposites to the freedom of the bankrupt one who possesses nothing, and arrive at the close with the perfect human being as a mirror before the Real — neither identical to Him nor other than Him.

Read the part

Al-Hakam: Balance Among Opposing Names

After Part CXXXVIII's distinction between the Qur'an and the Furqan, Chapter 326 (Muhammadan-Mosaic, the Station of Speech and Dispute) traces the origin of every dispute within the world to the enmity among opposing divine names: "every name, then, has a counterpart in ruling and in the scale... just as al-Mu'izz and al-Mudhill are rivals to one another, so too are al-Darr and al-Nafi', and al-Muhyi and al-Mumit, al-Mu'ti and al-Mani'" (pp. 199-200). It is explained that al-Hakam (the Arbiter/scale) balances this rivalry.

Chapter 326's account of the balance runs: “In His Book God states that He placed the balance in the world to realize justice in an audible and visible manner.” In the balance the two pans are like two adversaries, “the tongue of the balance is the one who judges”; “The one who judges is never a party” (p. 199). Dost ties this to the Prophet's saying “It is not fitting to dispute in the presence of a prophet” and says: “When you see a person disputing in the world, know that he is veiled from God!”; those who dispute are the divine names, so “the divine names are the source of dispute in the world” (p. 199). The balance is a divine name: “The name al-Hakam, looking at the receptivity of the place and locus, places the right on the side of one of two opposed and contradictory names according to that receptivity” (p. 200). The difference between God and the servant is: “God raises and lowers by His will. In your balance, will has no effect” (p. 200).

Preference (Mufadala): Superiority in Knowledge and in Works

Chapter 327 presents preference (mufadala) as one of the station's knowledges: “Preference occurs in kinds: the first occurs through knowledge, the other through works.” Superiority in knowledge comes from the worth of the thing known or from the method that leads to the knowledge: one who takes knowledge from God is superior to one who takes it from a creature; some take knowledge through a means such as piety, others directly from God without a means. Superiority in works “depends at times on the works themselves, at times on time, at times on place, at times on state”. Intellect too is divided by measure: “God divided it among people by measure, giving one person a certain amount and another twice that.” The example of hijra: for the Meccans, alms given before the hijra do not reach the reward of those given after it (p. 204).

A Communication on the Basmala and the Sura of Repentance

Dost relates a communication that came to his heart: “The basmala at the head of every sura means crowning it with divine mercy. Through this, divine mercy reaches everything mentioned in the sura.” The basmala is like the sign on a ruler's decree, showing that the sura is from God. When he asked about the sura of Repentance, the answer was: “Repentance and al-Anfal are one sura, and God separated them in two.” When separated, He named it “tawba”, that is, God's turning with mercy toward servants with whom He was angry: “So God's anger is not an eternal anger but a temporal one. God is al-Tawwab” (pp. 205-206). In presenting this communication Dost says he speaks and judges “from the breath that comes to my secret from the divine and holy spirit” (p. 206).

The Point and the Circumference: The World's Two Ends

Chapter 327 (Muhammadan, the Station of Extension and Contraction) states: "when God created the world as consisting of two ends and a means between them, He made one end the point of a circle and the other its circumference... the circumference was named the Throne, the point the earth, and what lies between them was named circles, pillars, and spheres" (p. 208).

Dost says this middle is the perfect human being: “God determined things by His knowledge and brought them into being by His wisdom, creating them as two extremes. And He created a middle and intermediary joining the two extremes. […] In this in-between place the perfect human being was created. Thus he combined in himself determining — which is general — and creating — which is particular” (p. 207). God discloses Himself in two ways, one general and one particular: the general self-disclosure is that of the name al-Rahman (“The Rahman established Himself on the Throne”); the particular is each person's own knowledge of God (p. 207). Dost: “God differentiated the world only through Himself. So He is the differentiated as much as He is the one who brings about the differentiation” and “God made the witnessed His self-disclosure itself, and made the unseen a veil over it” (pp. 207-208).

Sainthood Never Ceases: The Difference Between Prophethood and Sainthood

Chapter 327 also treats the science of mufadala (superiority/preference) — the difference between superiority through knowledge and through deed, and the hierarchy of sainthood-prophethood-messengerhood. It is emphasized that the door of legislative prophethood has closed, while the door of sainthood remains ever open. The chapter again refers to Abu Yazid (p. 221): "as an example of this state, we may offer Abu Yazid. While characterized by love, what witnessing leads one not to love the thing itself?".

Through a communication (warid), Dost sets out the difference between sainthood and prophethood: “Sainthood is the first. It accompanies as second (always), is constant and does not disappear. One of its degrees is prophethood and messengership.” Today no one can reach law-giving prophethood, because its door is closed; “Sainthood does not cease in this world or the next. So sainthood is the first, the last, the manifest, the hidden (al-Awwal, al-Akhir, al-Zahir, al-Batin)”. The reason lies in the names: “One of God's names is al-Wali. He has no name ‘Prophet’ or ‘Messenger’. Therefore prophethood and messengership have been cut off, since they have no support in the divine names. Sainthood has not been cut off, because al-Wali protects it” (pp. 206-207).

The Ruling of the Name al-Warith: The Inheritance of Earth and Heaven

In Chapter 327 Dost takes up the ruling of the name al-Warith (the Inheritor): “The ruling of the divine name al-Warith is from this station, and it is a strange ruling, for the ruling of this name penetrates the heavens and the earth.” This is the indication of the verse “On the day the earth and heavens are changed”; as the earth was created before heaven at first creation, so in the changing the earth will be begun with first (p. 208). God said that the inheritance of the heavens and earth is His; the divine names exercise authority over the earth and govern it, and when the governance ends, “separation” is called death and the governed beings pass as inheritance to the name al-Warith (p. 209). The divine names are jealous: “Every divine name wishes the ruling to belong to it and the thing subject to the ruling to return to it without turning to another” (p. 209). Provision also appears here: according to the verse “He ordained therein its sustenance”, the earth is the possessor of measure and “no one dies before his provision is completed”; when provision is complete, al-Razzaq's ruling ends and al-Warith tells it “provide without measure and term” (pp. 209-210).

It Is He Himself Who Unites Two Opposites

Chapter 328 (Muhammadan, the Station of Compounds Turning into Simples in the State of Dissolution) treats the question of the uniting of opposites. First a question is posed (pp. 210-211): "opposites are learned from this way-station. Does a single existence gather them so that they become one existence? Or are they rulings belonging to the single reality demanded by the relations?" Then an answer follows (p. 221): "is it the uniting of two opposites that has rendered it impossible? The very one uniting two opposites is, in fact, He Himself".

On the dissolution of composites into simple elements, Chapter 328 says: “when composites are dissolved into their simple elements, the form itself disappears while the substance does not” (p. 213). From this station come to the Sufis “true dreams and glad tidings”; these are a part of prophethood (pp. 212-213). Dost mentions the two books the Prophet brought to his Companions — one with the names of the people of paradise, the other with those of the people of hellfire; “For the people of God and His elect He did not produce a third book, for their book was the noble Qur'an” (p. 213). Among the people of God, one class appears in the knower of “God”, one in the knower “for God”; the class between the two hands is that which speaks the verse “The seeker is I / The sought is also I” (pp. 213-214).

Dost distinguishes two more classes: “As for the other two classes, the characteristic of the first is takwin (bringing into being), while the other is taslim (submission).” When the people of takwin leave their bodies in death, the gates of heaven open for them and they rise with their spirits to their place at the Lote-Tree of the Furthest Boundary (p. 216). He explains this with the hadith that “one dirham surpassed a thousand dirhams”: one with a single dirham spends all his property and returns to God, while one with a thousand dinars gives part and leaves some to spend; “The Lawgiver did not consider the value of what is given but what the giver relies on after giving. Those who return to God are those who have exhausted everything other than God (the bankrupt, muflis)” (p. 216). Of the people of taslim he says they are in hardship and exertion in the fire of striving and discipline, “and cannot know the coolness of certainty” (p. 220).

The Bankrupt and the Serious: The Freedom of Possessing Nothing

Chapter 328 draws a contrast between the bankrupt (muflis) and the serious/committed one (jiddi, one who holds command): "the bankrupt one is Muhammadan and stationless. To him it is said: 'you possess nothing, that you might lose it.'... the serious one, however, holds command... such a person is not bankrupt" (pp. 216-218). In these lines, "possessing nothing" is presented not as a deficiency, but as the nearness to an unbounded freedom belonging to one who has nothing left to lose. The chapter uses the lengthening of the shadow toward the afternoon as an image of relief (p. 219).

Dost explains the worth of bankruptcy: “The Prophet, as the most perfect possessor of unveiling, says: ‘The serious ones are in prison.’ One in prison is delimited. The bankrupt has no act that delimits or imprisons him, and he is free of the serious one's delimitation.” The serious are at the rank of those who see the Real in things and necessarily delimit the Real by things (p. 217). He notes that the people of takwin are at the station of the address to the Prophet, “You have no share in the command”; here God made the Prophet bankrupt (pp. 217-218). When the people of takwin see that places are full and the world complete, they feel their incapacity and wish to rest; then the address comes to their secrets: “Do you not see how your Lord extends the shade?” (pp. 218-219).

The shade is tied to the bankrupt's repose: “The bankrupt rests only through God, for he has exhausted the world and cannot rest in shade.” When God wishes to give the bankrupt rest, He draws the shade toward Himself and light fills its place; the bankrupt is like a person who is cold seeking the sun. Dost: “Thus God is al-Awwal (the First) in the bankrupt's witnessing, and al-Akhir (the Last) at the end of his rapture” (p. 219). The bankrupt convey knowledge that gives life to the hearts of the people of takwin, who see that the help comes from the perfect men of God (p. 219). The difference between the bankrupt and the people of takwin is a difference of place: “While the Throne belongs to these people, the people of takwin are masters of the carpet (farsh).” The attributes of the names of transcendence belong to the bankrupt, the realities of the names of likeness to the people of takwin (p. 220).

The Close: The Mirror Analogy, the Perfect Human and the Real

Chapter 329 (Muhammadan, the Station of Blessings and Turning Toward Trial), after treating the human being's creation according to the form of God and the world's existence being bound to the human being, summarizes the relation between the perfect human being and the Real through a mirror analogy: "this is like the form of a person looking into a mirror. The form in the mirror is neither identical to the form of the one looking, nor other than it... the ruling belongs — not to the one manifesting, but — to the place of manifestation" (pp. 227-228). The chapter also treats the Qur'an's descent upon the heart and its being a barzakh between the human being and the Real.

Chapter 329 relates the human being and the world: “The Prophet says: ‘God created Adam in His own form.’ The world too was created in His form because of the human being.” Dost says that in the verse “Everyone upon the earth is perishing” it is not said “everyone in it is perishing”, and that the divine self-disclosure encompasses everyone upon the earth; when the Prophet was asked about the eclipse of the sun, he said “When God discloses Himself to a thing, that thing is humbled” (p. 222). The third ruling of manifestation and concealment appears “in seven hundred and twenty ranks”; between each two ranks there is an isthmus self-disclosure: “The isthmus rules at both extremes: it melts what is dense and makes dense what melts.” Dost: “The world is like an isthmus between eternity-without-end and eternity-without-beginning” and like the “present time” between past and future (p. 224).

On the Qur'an Dost relates: “The Qur'an's descent upon hearts is new; it does not grow old. In this respect the Qur'an is a continuous revelation.” The Qur'an became an isthmus between the human being and the Real; “The noble Qur'an appeared in the Prophet's heart as a single reality; imagination embodied and divided it, the tongue took it and turned it into letters and sound, and restricted the hearing of the ear to it” (p. 225). Speech belongs to God, the transmission (translation) to the speaker; “God's speech has continued to be recited as letters and sound from its descent until it is lifted from minds (memory) and erased from the codices” (pp. 225-226). Dost then says of the perfect human being: “The perfect human being is the very aim, for he is the manifest side of the divine form. And God is the Manifest and the Hidden”; the perfect human being subsists through God's subsisting, while the others subsist through God's causing them to subsist (p. 227). After the mirror image he gives two readings of the verse “There is nothing like the likeness of Him” (p. 228).
