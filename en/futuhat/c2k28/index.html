From the Fifty Stations of the Resurrection to the Greatest Intercession: The Day Death Itself Is Sacrificed

pp. 430–458

Site reading summary

Part XXVIII is the final part of Volume II — and, in the text's own words, of the 'Fourth Sifr': at the part's close, Dost writes, "With the completion of the twenty-eighth part, the fourth sifr is complete. The twenty-ninth part will follow it, and the thirtieth part will follow that" (p. 458) — meaning the volume of the text we have ends here, and Part XXIX continues in the next volume. Unlike the recent parts, this one holds a single chapter — Chapter Sixty-Four, "Knowing the Resurrection, Its Stations, and the Nature of Revival" — but that one chapter is as long as some entire parts. It opens with the earth and sky's transformation, the angels' descent from the heavens, the Real's three calls, a neck rising from the fire to swallow the defiant, and then, carried through a hadith, fifty stations of a thousand years each (the grave's threshold, the fiery stairs, ten stairs of reckoning, the Gathering's fifteen stations, the scale, twelve stations before God, and seven bridges over the Path). Dost then returns in his own voice to discuss the nature of revival: that death is the 'small resurrection,' that the Gathering is the collecting of particular souls into the Universal Soul, that the tailbone is the seed from which the afterlife's creation grows, and that this world, the barzakh, and the afterlife form three successive degrees of wakefulness. At the chapter's center stands the scene of the greatest intercession — from Adam to Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, each declining in turn, until Muhammad accepts. Toward the close, the seven fundamental places of the Resurrection are listed (the presentation, the record, the scale, the Path, al-A'raf, the sacrifice of death, and the banquet) — among them the scene where death itself, in the form of a radiant ram, is slaughtered, and both the people of Paradise and of Hell are told they will remain forever.

The Meaning of the Day of Resurrection and the Earth's Transformation

Dost opens the chapter by explaining where the name "resurrection" (qiyama) comes from: the day is so named because people "rise standing before the Lord of the worlds" (pp. 432-433); he notes that it is specifically the name al-Rabb that appears here, not al-Rahman, since on that day "wrath and reckoning" are also unavoidable — and yet, he adds, "His mercy outstrips His wrath" (p. 433). The scene then widens: God exchanges the earth for another earth called "wakefulness," one "upon which there is no sleep," stretching it "as one stretches a hide" — "this measure may reach from twenty-one parts to ninety-nine parts" (p. 433). The sky, meanwhile, is rolled up "like the rolling of written scrolls" and cast down onto this new earth (p. 434).

The angels then descend from the heavens — a fresh wave from each sky, each more numerous than the last. At every descent the creatures ask the same question: "Is our Lord among you?" and the angels give the same answer each time: "We declare our Lord transcendent! He is not among us, He is coming!" (pp. 433-434) — the scene repeats seven times, once for each heaven. At last the Real comes "amid dark clouds," with Hell at His left; Hell, before the tyrannical, "surges and pants with rage" — this is "the greatest fear" for everyone except "those whom the greatest fear will not grieve" (pp. 434-435).

The Real's Three Calls and the Neck That Rises from the Fire

A caller cries out three times on God's behalf — and Dost adds: "They — or I — do not know whether this call is the Real's own call, or a call issued by His command" (p. 435). The calls summon, in turn: those who spend their nights in prostration, those whom trade did not distract from remembering God, and those who kept their word (pp. 435-436) — each group is led to Paradise.

Then "a neck of fire" rises up, speaking as though it had two eyes and an intelligible tongue. It cries out three times, and each time swallows a group from those still standing — "just as a bird swallows sesame seeds" — first "the defiant tyrants," then "those who wronged God and His messenger," and finally "those who set out to create as God creates" — that is, those who fashioned images or idols to be worshipped (pp. 436-437). For this third group Dost draws a distinction: only those who made images for the purpose of worship are swallowed by this neck; those who made images as a craft, without wanting them worshipped, are not — for they, too, will not be asked to breathe spirit into what they made (p. 437).

The Hadith of the Fifty Stations: The First Three

Here Dost gives a long chain of transmission — a hadith conveyed to him by his shaykh al-Qassar, in Mecca in 599 AH, at the Yemeni Corner of the Kaaba (pp. 436-437), tracing back through Ali ibn Abi Talib to the Prophet. The hadith opens: "On the Day of Resurrection there are fifty stations, each lasting a thousand years" (p. 437).

At the first station people rise from their graves and remain "at the doors of their graves for a thousand years, naked, barefoot, hungry and thirsty"; those whose faith carries every condition intact are delivered, while those who harbored doubt wait "a thousand years in hunger, thirst, grief, and sorrow" (p. 437). At the second station the creatures stand "upon stairs of fire beneath the sun's heat for a thousand years" — "and there is no shade there but the shade of the Throne" (pp. 437-438). At the third they are driven "into light and darkness"; those whose hearts held no hypocrisy pass from darkness to light "in the twinkling of an eye," while those who acted otherwise remain in darkness a thousand years and emerge at last with blackened faces (p. 438).

The Ten Stairs of Reckoning and the Gathering's Fifteen Stations

The hadith now enters a structure of ever-expanding inquiry. First come ten "stairs of reckoning" — each a thousand years — questioning, in turn, forbidden acts, desires, the rights of parents, teaching others their religion, the rights of those under one's charge, the rights of kin, visiting close relatives, envy, deceit, and fraud; whoever is found lacking at none of them climbs to the next, and whoever reaches the eleventh (deliverance) settles "with eyes alight, heart full of joy" in the shade of the Throne (pp. 438-439). Then comes a gathering-inquiry of "fifteen stations" — questioning charity, truthfulness, enjoining good, forbidding wrong, good character, loving and hating for God's sake, avoiding unlawful wealth, avoiding wine, adultery, lying, false oaths, usury, slandering chaste women, false testimony, and calumny — whoever passes the fifteenth settles "beneath the Banner of Praise" (pp. 439-440).

In both lists — the stairs of reckoning and the Gathering's fifteen stations — Dost repeats the same pattern: whoever is found lacking remains at each station a thousand years "hungry, thirsty, sorrowful, grieved and anxious," until at last "God judges concerning him as He wills" (pp. 439-440).

The Scale, the Twelve Stations Before God, and the Path's Seven Bridges

Once the records are handed out from the right or left hand — the generous find their reading made easy and are clothed in garments of Paradise, while the miser's record is given from the left and "a piece of fire is cut off for him" (p. 440) — the sequence moves to the scale: "whoever's good deeds outweigh" succeeds, while the reverse means being held a thousand years "in grief, sorrow, sadness, torment, hunger and thirst" (p. 440). Then begin "the twelve stations before God" — questioning the freeing of slaves, the Qur'an, striving in God's cause, backbiting, talebearing, lying, the pursuit of knowledge, self-admiration, arrogance, despairing of God's mercy, feeling secure from God's plan, and the rights of one's neighbor (pp. 440-441); whoever passes the twelfth is seated before God "eyes bright, heart at ease, face radiant," and the Lord says to him, "welcome" (p. 441).

Finally the creatures proceed to the Path — "finer than a hair, sharper than a sword," "the bridges number seven" and upon each bridge there are "steep hills whose total length is a journey of three thousand years": "a thousand years uphill, a thousand years level, a thousand years downhill" (pp. 441-442). Each bridge questions, in turn, faith, prayer, alms, fasting, pilgrimage, purity, and injustice; whoever passes the seventh arrives directly at Paradise (pp. 441-442). Here the hadith's narrator breaks off, leaving its continuation — since it concerns Paradise — for another place (p. 442). At this point Dost adds: "The creation of the afterlife is not imaginal but sensory. The Resurrection, too, is a real and existing thing, sensory just as the human being's condition in this world is" (p. 442).

Gathering and Raising: Interpretations of Revival and the "Small Resurrection"

Under a "vasl" heading Dost now begins to speak in his own voice. He says people diverge on the matter of re-creation, and that some interpret the creation of the afterlife as "rational, not sensory, states" — he does not reject this reading outright, only finds it incomplete: "the holder of this interpretation has not understood that there are two modes of creation there — the creation of bodies and the creation of spirits" (p. 442). Dost then says: "A person's dying is his resurrection. But this is the small resurrection" — grounded in the Prophet's saying, "whoever has died, his resurrection has already occurred" (p. 442). The Gathering, in Dost's own formulation, is "the collecting of particular souls into the Universal Soul" (p. 442).

Dost then makes room for views discussing reincarnation (tanasukh), and says: "In these matters, everyone who holds to any of these paths has a real and sound aspect to their view" (pp. 442-443).

The Limit of Natural Lifespan and the Nature of Revival

Dost takes up a side matter: the natural sciences count the human lifespan as "one hundred and twenty years," but he says this is no fixed verdict — a person's life exceeding this by "thousands of years," or even continuing without end, could still remain "within nature" rather than outside it (p. 443). He then says: "had the Law not informed us that the span of worldly life will end and everyone will die, that there will be a re-creation, an afterlife, and an eternal settling there through the creation of the afterlife" we could not have "foreseen" any of this — death, revival, Paradise, Hell (pp. 443-444).

How revival will take place is then discussed: some believers hold it will happen through sexual union and reproduction as in the first creation — Dost reports this view as conveyed by Shaykh Abu al-Qasim ibn al-Qasi (in his book Khal' al-Na'layn), though he says he is not certain whether this is Ibn al-Qasi's own view or one relayed from "an illiterate man named Half Allah" (pp. 444-445). Others rely on a hadith: "the sky rains down something like semen, and the earth becomes pregnant" (p. 444). Dost emphasizes that the verses ("He will re-create you as He created you") point to a creation made without precedent, and that this is "without doubt a sensory creation" (p. 445). He cites a poet's verses: "The astrologer and the physician supposed: bodies will not be revived. I said to both of them: if what you say is true, I lose nothing. But if my word is true, it is you who truly lose!" (p. 444).

The Tailbone and the Three Degrees of Wakefulness

Dost discusses what seed the creation of the afterlife will be built upon: Abu Hamid (al-Ghazali) holds that the "tailbone" ('ajb al-dhanab) is the soul, while Abu Zayd al-Raqraqi proposes it is the "single/atomic substance" (cevher-i ferd) left over from this world's creation. Dost offers knowledge from his own unveiling: "The tailbone is that by which creation itself comes into actual existence... it does not wear out, that is to say it is not subject to wearing out" (p. 446). He then narrates Israfil's two blasts: the first blast touches the barzakh-forms and extinguishes them; the second comes to the form now "prepared to catch fire" — "and so the barzakh-forms catch fire with their spirits. All at once, gazing at one another, they rise" (pp. 446-447).

Right after this, Dost sets up three successive degrees of wakefulness across this world, the barzakh, and the afterlife: worldly life is a sleep; the barzakh is the state of having woken from it; but the afterlife is true wakefulness, one that "contains no sleep whatsoever." "Thus this world, relative to the barzakh, is sleep within sleep. For the barzakh is nearer to the true condition" — and the barzakh, in turn, is itself a sleep relative to the afterlife (p. 447). The Prophet's saying "people are asleep, and when they die they awaken" grounds this hierarchy.

The Greatest Intercession: From Adam to Muhammad

When the dread reaches its peak — as the stars scatter, the seas are set ablaze, and the Lord comes "amid dark clouds" — people say to one another, "Come, let us go to our father Adam" (pp. 447-448). They come to Adam and plead with him; Adam, recalling his own error, holds back, saying "today God has become angry as He has never been before" (p. 448). The same plea is carried in turn to Noah (who holds back, recalling his curse upon his people), to Abraham (recalling the "three lies" he told), to Moses, and to Jesus — each giving an answer like Adam's (p. 448). At last they come to Muhammad, "the master of humankind on the Day of Resurrection"; he says, "intercession is my task!", falls into prostration, praises God with praises inspired to him in that very moment, and then asks that the gate of intercession be opened — and God opens it (pp. 448-449).

After this scene Dost draws a distinction: Muhammad said, "I am the master of humankind," but did not say "I am the master of all creatures," which would include the angels — Dost reads this as a mark of reverence, since his authority over all creatures will in any case become manifest on that day (p. 449).

The Real's Self-Disclosure in Forms and the Test of Prostration

After the gate of intercession opens, the Real first appears to this community "in the basest of the forms in which He discloses Himself" and says, "I am your Lord!"; they answer, "we seek refuge in God from you, we will wait here until our Lord comes" (p. 450). God then asks them for a sign known between them, and discloses Himself again through that sign — that is, through a form they can recognize; only then do they say, "you are indeed our Lord" (p. 450).

Then the command to prostrate comes — everyone who prostrated sincerely in worldly life prostrates there too; but the backs of those who prostrated "out of fear or hypocrisy" turn into "a sheet of lead," and at every attempt to prostrate they fall "upon their heads" (p. 450). Dost ties this to the image of the "heel" — citing the Arab idiom "the war came out of its heel" (meaning the war intensified), he explains that the verse's phrase "heel entangled with heel" means "fears and hard matters become entangled with one another" (p. 450).

The Difference Between Rational and Religious Affirmation of Unity

Once intercession begins, everyone who performed even "a mustard seed's weight, or less," of a deed prescribed by any prophet's religion is brought out of Hell — but here Dost draws a fine distinction: "the rationalist affirmers of unity, who know God's oneness through rational proofs, remain in Hell" (p. 450) — that is, those who do not associate partners with God but are not religiously bound to any prophet, having arrived at "God is one" through reason alone. These are described as people "in whom there is not a mustard seed's worth of faith, or less" (pp. 450-451). Still, Dost adds that they too will eventually be brought out of Hell by "the most merciful of the merciful" — noting that the hadith specifically prefers the phrase "died while knowing" rather than "died while believing" (p. 451).

Dost raises an objection to himself and answers it: "but Iblis too knows that God is one" — "you are right," he says, yet Iblis "is the first of those who associate partners," and so their burden too falls upon his shoulders; perhaps he died in a state of associating partners "through some doubt that touched his thought" (p. 451). And he adds: "as for the reason he will not come out of the fire, God knows best" (p. 451).

The Seven Fundamental Places of the Resurrection: From Presentation to the Scale

Dost now resummarizes the Resurrection according to seven fundamental places, "the best-known among the places of the Resurrection": the place of presentation, the taking of records, the scale, the Path, al-A'raf, the sacrifice of death, and the banquet in the courtyard of Paradise — he adds that these match in number the seven gates of Hell and the seven gates of Paradise; an eighth gate belongs only to the "Paradise of Vision," a gate that will never open, "for the people of Hell are veiled from seeing their Lord" (pp. 451-452). The place of presentation is explained through a hadith prompted by Aisha's question: what is meant by "an easy reckoning" is presentation itself — "whoever is pressed hard in the reckoning has been punished" — deeds, like a soldier's banner, mark people out by their faces (p. 452).

The taking of records separates three groups: the believer, who receives it from the right hand; the hypocrite, who receives it from the left ("for the unbeliever has no record"); and one who receives it from behind — someone who threw the book sent down to them behind their back and sold it for a paltry price (pp. 452-453). Then comes the scale: while every deed and every act of remembrance enters the scale, one phrase alone does not — "there is no god but God" — for this phrase has "no counterpart to weigh against it on the other pan"; only associating partners could be its opposite, but the two "can never come together on a single scale" (p. 453). Then the story of "the man with the record" is told — whose ninety-nine records of wrongdoing are outweighed by a single sincerely spoken utterance of "there is no god but God" (p. 453).

The Path, al-A'raf, and the Fine Grain of Divine Justice

The Path — "finer than a hair, sharper than a sword" — has, for Dost, a counterpart in this world too: "in this world, this path is the knowledge of the Law" (pp. 454-455); he says that the correct interpretation before God on a given matter can never be known with certainty, so jurists worship "according to strong probabilities," that only one person ever arrives at the correct ruling, while other jurists arrive "not at the correct ruling, but at a reward" (p. 455). The bridge's width is not fixed either: "On the Day of Resurrection the Path appears to people in proportion to the light they possess. Thus the bridge narrows for one group while it widens for another" (p. 456) — meaning the path's difficulty is proportional to the light a person carries.

The source of the bridge's "thorns, hooks, and barbs" is also explained: they arise "from the forms of the deeds of the children of Adam" (p. 456). As for the fifth place, al-A'raf: a wall between Paradise and Hell, its inner face mercy and its outer face punishment; there stand those whose scales are perfectly balanced, possessing no advantage that would grant them entry to either Paradise or Hell — but they are delivered the moment they respond to the call to prostrate (pp. 456-457). Dost emphasizes that these people know "God will not wrong anyone by so much as an atom's weight," and precisely for this reason they "covet" God's generosity (p. 457).

The Sacrifice of Death and the Banquet

At the sixth place comes the following scene. Death — "even though it is a relation with no reality outside the mind" — is brought forth by God on the Day of Resurrection "in the form of a radiant ram." "O people of Paradise!" is called out, and they lift their heads; "O people of Hell!" is called out, and they too lift their heads. Both are asked, "do you recognize this?", and both answer, "it is death." John (Yahya), peace be upon him, comes with a knife in hand, lays death down, and slits its throat. Then a herald cries out: "O people of Paradise! You now abide forever, there is no more death! O people of Hell! You abide forever, there is no more death! This, indeed, is the day of grief" (pp. 457-458).

The people of Paradise greet death with great joy — "you were a blessed guest who came to us," they say, recalling the Prophet's saying, "death is a gift for the believer" (p. 458). The people of Hell, by contrast, try to flee from it, hoping "perhaps it will kill us, and we will be delivered from the state we are in" — but this is no longer possible (p. 458).

The part closes with the seventh place, the banquet: the food of the people of Paradise is "what remains of the liver of the whale" — the liver, "the house of blood," the source of vitality; the food of the people of Hell is "the spleen cut from a bull" — the spleen, "the house of impurities," where the body's corrupted blood collects (p. 458). Dost reads these two organs symbolically: one the promise of life's continuance, the other the source of sickness and affliction. The chapter closes with a simple sentence: "Then the people of Paradise enter Paradise: 'they shall not leave it.' 'God speaks the truth and guides to the right way'" (p. 458). In the part's final lines Dost states the book's inner structure: "With the completion of the twenty-eighth part, the fourth sifr is complete. The twenty-ninth part will follow it, and the thirtieth part will follow that" (p. 458) — the volume before us ends here.
