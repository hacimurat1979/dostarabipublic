Spiritual Things Are Circles, Sensory Things Are Spheres: The Close of Volume XI

pp. 327–375

Site reading summary

The final four chapters of Volume XI move from the "stumbling pass" where the servant's foot slips if he forgets his servanthood, through revealed law's ending while reality endures, through three kinds of opening (unveiling-sweetness-expression), to the principle of circularity restated as "spiritual things are circles, sensory things are spheres; the circle has no end," and arrive at a discussion of imitation closing with Junayd's water-vessel analogy; the volume ends quietly, with no special ceremony or summary, through the same routine closing formula used for any way-station.

Read the part

The Stumbling Pass

Following Part CXLI's sunlight analogy, the closing four chapters of Volume XI open with Chapter 338 (Muhammadan, the Station of the Stumbling Pass). After introducing the banners of praise (the 1,646 names the Prophet will use to praise God at the Praiseworthy Station on the Day of Resurrection, in banners of ninety-nine each), the "stumbling pass" (saweq 'aqaba) is described — a dangerous way-station where the Real appears in the servant's form, and the servant's foot slips if he forgets his own servanthood. The concept of equality (sawa') is also treated: the servant is the Lord's path, the Lord the servant's path.

Chapter 338 lists the banners of praise through the answer given to Dost: "These names are one thousand six hundred and forty-six names. Ninety-nine names are written on each banner. Whoever memorizes them enters Paradise." (p. 327). Recalling the Prophet's hadith, "On that day I will praise God with names I do not know today," Dost explains it as "praising God with the names that place requires" (p. 327). On the way-station's name: "It is called 'pass' ('aqaba), and 'saweq' because the feet do not stay firm on it, for it is a place that makes feet slip." (p. 328). He identifies the rank with what 'Abd al-Jabbar al-Niffari calls the "station of equality" (sawa) in his Mawaqif; the reason is "the Real's appearing in it in the servant's form" (p. 328).

Dost states how the servant must stand in this way-station: "While the servant's foot is in this way-station, his servanthood must be set before his eyes and he must witness it continually." (pp. 327-328). He describes the slip thus: "This is a difficult witnessing, because God has descended from the station of independence of the worlds to the station of asking his servants for a loan. Hence some people have said, 'God is poor.' Yet God is rich." (p. 328). The way out: "This is the divine snare one does not notice, and whoever wants to learn the path to deliverance from it must hold fast, in every state, to servanthood and its requirements." (p. 328).

Equality (Sawa): The Servant's Path, the Lord's Path; Names of Deficiency and of Perfection

Dost sets equality (sawa) between two paths: "Just as the Lord has a path, the servant has a path. The servant is the Lord's path and its goal reaches Him. The Lord is also the servant's path and its goal reaches the servant." (p. 328). Of the attributes ascribed to creatures that ordinary people call "God's condescension": "These attributes are 'names of deficiency' in the view of ordinary people, but 'names of perfection' in our view. For in the true sense nothing other than God is named." (p. 328); "Every name belonging to them is real and original for the Real, and borrowed for the creatures." (p. 329). He also recalls Abu Bakr's words, "I saw God before everything I saw, and knew Him" (p. 329).

The Door of Mercy, the Basmala and Surat al-Tawba

"God created for this way-station a door He named 'the door of mercy.'" (p. 329). Dost likens the door to intention in knowledge and ties the basmala to it: "The basmala is to the sura what intention is to the deed." (p. 329). In Surat al-Tawba: "Had God used the name the Merciful in the verse, He would have punished none of those who transgressed. But He used the name 'God.'" (p. 330); since the verse ends "Forgiving and Compassionate," "even if they are punished, the place they finally arrive at is mercy" (p. 330). Also: "The world is a letter set down for a meaning. The meaning of the letter is God." (p. 330); of whoever enters the way-station: "This is the way-station of Khidr, Moses' companion." (p. 330).

Praising God: Praise, Glorification and the Names

Dost explains praise (hamd) by distinguishing it from glorification (tasbih): "Glorification is not praising a positive state but declaring transcendence (tanzih). For God can only be praised with what is fitting for Him." (p. 331). In praising God "we are only transmitters and reciters" (p. 331); "glorification is the negation of all attributes from the Real, not an affirmation" (p. 332). He sets the limit thus: "I cannot praise You; You are as You have praised Yourself." (p. 332). On the names: God "said, 'The most beautiful names belong to Him,' and made them names, not attributes or qualities"; these are "names that came not for praise but as evidence" (p. 332). He also explains the Prophet's having the mute slave girl freed as "freeing Him from the bond of place" (p. 333).

Revealed Law Ends, Reality Endures

Chapter 339 (Muhammadan, the Station of Revealed Law's Submission Before Reality) draws a sharp distinction of endurance between revealed law and reality: revealed law comes to an end (through abrogation), while reality endures, bound to the Real's everlastingness. The perfect human being is described through a mirror metaphor: "even if a mirror is complete in its creation, it attains perfection only through the reflection of the viewer's form upon it" (p. 339) — here it is emphasized that the mirror's perfection is completed not of itself, but only when someone stands before it and looks.

Chapter 339 also separates revealed law and reality in terms of knowledge: "What is meant here is knowing God from the particular aspect in every created and existent thing. This means knowledge of reality, and the Prophet did not ask for an increase in knowledge of revealed law." (p. 338). Revealed law is "knowledge of evidence"; "The end of the path of revealed law is sensory happiness. Reality, however, is not the end of the path for the commonality." (p. 338). Of the perfect human being: "The human being looks at the Real in two ways, and for this God created two eyes for him." (p. 339). With the first eye he sees the Real as independent of the worlds; with the second he looks from the name the Merciful and "sees the Real in everything, His existence spread out" (p. 339). On the angels' prostration: "Their prostration to Adam was eternal in ruling, but for the perfect human being their prostration is everlasting and constant." (p. 340).

Three Openings: Unveiling, Sweetness, Expression

Chapter 339 offers three kinds of opening (fath) through Surat al-Fath: the opening of unveiling (the lifting of the veil), the opening of sweetness (the opening of taste/relish), and the opening of expression (the opening of speech). The chapter conveys the superiority of prophets over other revealed laws through the sun-star analogy: when the sun rises, the light of the stars becomes invisible, yet the stars do not cease to exist; one revealed law is "covered" by the next, yet not rendered false.

Dost divides the opening in Surat al-Fath into three and gives each a recipient: "The opening of sweetness was established for the Prophet through 'taste,' while the opening of expression was established for the Arabs, who were left powerless to oppose him. The opening of unveiling was established on the Prophet's behalf through the wonders shown him on the night journey." (p. 343). On the forgiveness of the future sin: "It is sin's being hidden from the Prophet. Then sin cannot find the Prophet or settle in him." (p. 343). On earlier revealed laws: "They are not void merely because they were abrogated. To believe they are void is the conjecture of the ignorant." (p. 344). On the opening of the door: "Just as prophethood is not acquired, nothing that comes out from behind the opened door is acquired or dependent on acquisition." (p. 344); "Reality is the inward of all revealed laws. Revealed laws are its forms in the world of witnessing." (p. 345).

The Sciences Listed in Chapter 339

Chapter 339 ends by listing the sciences of the way-station: "Why the world was created, and the veil that comes between it and the reason for its creation, is known from this way-station." (p. 346); "Gathering appears in three places: at the taking of the covenant, in the barzakh between this world and the next, and in the resurrection after death." (p. 346). Recalling the definition of the human being as a "speaking animal," Dost says: "The human being's specific definition is possessing the divine form. Whoever does not have this definition is not a human being." (p. 347).

The Testing of Ibn Sayyad: Dukh

Chapter 340 opens with the Prophet's testing of Ibn Sayyad: "The Prophet asked Ibn Sayyad, 'What have I concealed in my mind for you?' and Ibn Sayyad said, 'dukh.' Dukh in the dictionary means smoke." (p. 347). On the source of the testing: "Had this test come from revelation, Ibn Sayyad could not have known it, and God would have backed him up after the command He gave." (p. 348). Dost relates the saying "God taught me manners, and how well He has taught me manners," and describes the way-station thus: "This way-station is a difficult way-station in which there is divine deception and istidraj. Even the angels who know it are not safe from God's snare." (p. 348).

The Meeting of the Real and Creation, and the Lightening of Prayer

In this way-station the descending Real and the ascending creature meet: "The descending Real and the ascending creature meet in it. When the Real asks the one ascending, 'Where to?' he says, 'To You.'" (p. 349). The created one says, "Teaching by state and conduct is more perfect than learning by speech and expression" (p. 349). The incident of prayer on the night journey follows: when fifty daily prayers were made obligatory, Moses told the Prophet, "Return to your Lord; perhaps He will lighten your community's burden"; the Prophet went back and forth between God and Moses and said, "I am ashamed before my Lord" (p. 350). Dost says, "God had made the authority of ijtihad a part of revealed law for his community [...] in rulings" (p. 350), and of the verse "The word is not changed with Me": "The necessary word does not change, while the provisional word changes." (p. 351).

Spiritual Things Are Circles, Sensory Things Are Spheres

Chapter 340 (the Station of Ibn Sayyad Being Tested Concerning Surat al-Dukhan) states the principle of circularity: "spiritual things are circles, sensory things are spheres. There is no such thing as an 'end' in coming-to-be, for the circle has no end. Every part is a barzakh between two parts" (p. 352) — here the author joins the principle of circularity with the concept of the barzakh, stating that everything is situated not between two ends, but within a circular barzakh. The chapter also draws a relation between Surat al-Qadr and Surat al-Dukhan — outwardly opposed, yet in truth complementary: "one cannot grasp one's own structure, formed as it is by the union of natural opposites" (p. 348).

In the same place Dost shows that name and state change a ruling: when Malik ibn Anas was asked about the "sea pig," he said, "It was you who named it a pig. By God, God has forbidden the pig," and Dost draws the conclusion: "So while the reality is one, the ruling changes because of the change of state." (p. 352). On the barzakh: "Barzakh means the placing of a thing between two things. The present time is also so, and is called 'continuity.'" (p. 352).

Bewilderment Between Two Forms, and Prayer on the Night Journey

Dost on the perfect human being: "[...] when he sees the Real in the form in which He has clothed the perfect human being, he is bewildered between the two forms and cannot tell to which to prostrate." (p. 353). The Prophet too, carried by night on the night journey, was startled, was called to with the voice of Abu Bakr, whose voice he knew, and then was told, "Muhammad, stop. Your Lord is praying." (p. 353). He was bewildered at prayer being ascribed to the Lord, and was eased when the verse "He it is who blesses you, and His angels" was recited (p. 354). Dost explains the difference thus: "The perfect servant's prayer is not upon God but 'for' God. Whoever understands this difference joins Qur'an and Furqan." (p. 354).

The Qur'an's Descent into the Heart and the Preservation of Its Wording

"The Qur'an descends into the heart of the Prophet and the hearts of those who recite it constantly." (p. 354). Of the heart in the body: "God makes the heart in the breast a codex and a written book." (p. 355). On the Prophet's transmission: "The Prophet was not commanded to move words out of their places." (p. 356); "The meanings were not sent down into his heart without letters being composed, words arranged, verses ordered and suras determined. All of this is what is called 'the Qur'an.'" (p. 357). On the night of power he relates his own witnessing: "We saw that the night of power moves through the whole year. We saw it in Sha'ban as we saw it in Ramadan and on all its odd-numbered nights." (p. 358).

Imitation: Imitating One's Lord, One's Reason and One's Thought

Chapter 341 defines imitation as the root to which all knowledge returns: "Imitation is the root to which all theoretical and necessary knowledge, or knowledge by unveiling, returns. But they are by degrees in this." (p. 361). Three groups: those who imitate their Lord, those who imitate their own reason, and those who imitate their reason in what their thought gives (p. 361). "Imitation means limitation" (p. 362); "The way-station of imitation is the most difficult of the stumbling passes." (p. 362). The reason for imitating the Lord: "What is fundamental in the world is ignorance. Knowledge is something acquired. Knowledge is existence, and existence belongs to God." (p. 362). Then: "You imitate the Lawgiver until unveiling comes. When unveiling comes, you see the reality of the knowledge you obtained through imitation." (p. 362).

The Mother of the Book, the Mu'attila and the People of God

Dost mentions the Real as the mother of the book: "The Real is in truth the Umm al-Kitab (mother of the book). The Qur'an is one of the books. But what distinguishes it from the other books is its comprehensiveness." (p. 363). On the Mu'attila: "The Mu'attila did not deny the existence of a support; they denied what you declared as the support and left it without attributes." (p. 364). For the people of God: "The people of God must learn all the schools and religions concerned with knowing God, so that they can witness God in every form." (p. 364); "Whoever limits God has limited witnessing Him." (p. 364).

The Close: The Color of Water, the Color of Its Vessel

Chapter 341 (the Station of Imitation in Secrets, the volume's final chapter) divides imitation into three kinds (imitating one's Lord / imitating one's reason / imitating one's own opinion) and enumerates the "He-you" relation through the views of six different communities. The chapter approaches its close with Junayd's water-vessel analogy: "when Junayd was asked about gnosis and the gnostic's known (the Real), he said, 'the color of water is the color of its vessel'... if the vessel is square, the water appears square; if pentagonal, pentagonal; if circular, the water appears in that form" (p. 365).

Dost gives the reason for Junayd's saying: "He said this so that you may recognize the one you know; you judge what you know according to yourself. So you have not known anyone other than yourself." (p. 365). Of the water: "As water is found in vessels, it is also found outside the vessel with its own definition and reality, and being in vessels does not make it lose the name 'water.'" (p. 365); "Manifestation belongs not to reality but to forms. Reality is always unseen; its form is seen." (p. 365). He lists the views of different communities on the goal of the paths ("you," "He," "He in you," "you in Him," "the two hands") and adds: "All that we have mentioned is on the straight path." (p. 366).

The Generality and the Specification of Ascription

Dost opens up the subject of ascription: "God is the possessor of absolute existence, and there is no limitation that bars Him from limitation." (p. 367). Against the Jews and Christians who say "We are the sons of God and His beloved," and those who say "The angels are God's daughters," he relates: "All the ties ascribed to God are one. Why did you single yourselves out for Him apart from others?" (p. 367). This part of the chapter ends: "So everyone who has a divine support is saved. To deny them all is the height of blindness." (p. 369).

Wasl: The Cosmic Specification, Pharaoh and Retribution

In the wasl titled "A Cosmic Specification," Dost says: "The divine distinction that brings felicity is different from the divine distinction that gives the perfect form. Yet both may come together in some persons." (p. 370). On Paradise: "Paradise is not the place for such a quality, so it is not a place of vicegerency. It is a place of sainthood." (p. 371). On Pharaoh: "Pharaoh knew that Moses was telling the truth." (p. 371); he shows the words "Today We shall save you in your body" to be an aspect of God's mercy toward His servants (p. 371). On retribution: "Retribution lasts for a set period with God; mercy follows it. For the unending, eternal ruling belongs to mercy." (p. 372). He ties this knowledge to Hudhayfa ibn al-Yaman: "The Prophet's companions called him 'the keeper of the secret' because he knew this knowledge." (p. 372).

The Close: The Volume's Quiet End

Chapter 341 continues with the story of Pharaoh and Moses (the effect of divine power, the hidden faith within Pharaoh) and a list of sciences, before ending with no special ceremony or summary at all, through a routine way-station closing formula: "these are some of the sciences this way-station contains. God speaks the truth and guides to the right path." A single sentence follows immediately: "the Twenty-Second Sifr has ended." While Chapters 338 and 339 carry the "known from the Muhammadan rank" label, Chapters 340 and 341 do not.

Chapter 341 lists the sciences of the way-station through a long "this is known from this way-station" list. Among them: "The difference between imitation and theoretical inquiry" (p. 373); "The reason Satan does not leave the human being" and the Prophet's saying that "God helped him against Satan and his Satan became Muslim" (p. 374); "Prophetic tawhid" (p. 374); of Jonah's people: "Their repentance also lifted the punishment of this worldly life from them." (p. 374); "The ranks and qualities of the intercessors" (p. 375).
