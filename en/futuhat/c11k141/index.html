The Circumference of the Circle Protects Existence: The Center-Circle Relation

pp. 276–326

Site reading summary

Four chapters move from making relative nonexistence concrete through the example of Zayd's return home, through the Qur'an's fresh descent upon the heart with every reading, to the reciprocal mirror relation between the Real and the believer, to the teaching that "the circumference of the circle protects existence," to a threefold classification of paths (existence-nonexistence-barzakh), and arrive at the close with the honeycomb's hexagonal geometry and the analogy of sunlight spreading equally to all.

Read the part

Relative Nonexistence: Zayd's Return Home

Following Part CXL's proof of circularity, Chapter 334 (Mosaic, the Station of the Renewal of the Nonexistent) makes concrete the difference between relative and absolute nonexistence through an example: "Zayd, while at the market, 'was not found'; he returns to the house he was in" (p. 277). The chapter also states a principle of manifestation: "God has never manifested twice in one form, nor to two persons in a single form" (p. 278).

On whether accidents are renewed after they cease to be, Dost says according to unveiling: “According to unveiling, nothing is repeated. Everything is in a state of — not renewal but — re-creation. If the new is called ‘renewal’, it is because of the strong resemblance between them.” Of night and day one says not “renewed” but “two new things”; and of one, eleven, twenty-one, a hundred and one: “Rather, eleven in itself is a single reality, as are twenty-one, a hundred and one and a thousand and one” (pp. 277-278). From here Dost turns to the Sufis' saying: “God does not disclose Himself twice in one form, nor to two persons in one form”; “God is engaged in a matter — indeed in matters — in each day of the breaths, the smallest of days” (p. 278).

The Color of Water Is the Color of Its Vessel: The Qur'an's Renewed Descent

Chapter 334 continues with the short answer given to Junayd's question, "what is gnosis?": "the color of water is the color of its vessel" (p. 279). The chapter argues that the Qur'an descends upon the heart differently with every reading: "one whose understanding is the same in two readings is deceived" (around p. 285). The story of Musa al-Sadrani speaking with the serpent encircling Mount Qaf, and Abu Madyan's fame reaching even the inhabitants of heaven, is also recounted in this chapter (pp. 283-284).

Dost describes the Qur'an's descent upon the heart: “The Qur'an's being sent down upon the hearts of those who recite it is a continually renewed matter. Everyone who recites it recites it in a new way.” The hearts of reciters are like a Throne for the Qur'an's descent; “Every heart has a Qur'an” (p. 278). The heart carries the Qur'an as character: when 'A'isha was asked about the Prophet's character she said “His character was the Qur'an.” Dost relates that the Prophet, at a verse of blessing, asked God for His bounty; at a verse of chastisement, took refuge in God; at a verse of a story, took heed; at a verse of ruling, carried out the ruling (p. 279). Of the one who merely pictures the letters in imagination and transmits them with the tongue: “Such a person earns the reward of transmission, not the reward of reciting the Qur'an”; the Prophet spoke of a people who recite but what they recite does not pass their throats (pp. 279-280).

Dost opens the verse “Fear God, and He teaches you” thus: understanding is understanding what the speaker intends by the speech. “According to this special understanding, the Qur'an's descent upon the heart is the Real's reciting to the servant. An understanding of the Qur'an from God means the servant's reciting to the Real” (p. 281). A servant whose insight is illumined gains a new understanding of the verse in every recitation; “One whose understanding is equal in two recitations is deceived. One who gains an understanding in every recitation is a gainer and has attained mercy” (p. 281). If the Qur'an descends upon the heart from the name Lord it descends delimited, and the heart becomes a “noble”, “mighty”, “glorious” throne; if from the name al-Rahman it descends undelimited. Dost shows this from the verses: “Lord of the Mighty Throne”, “Lord of the Noble Throne”, “Possessor of the Glorious Throne”; so too the Qur'an is mentioned as mighty, noble and glorious (pp. 281-282).

Dost lists the result of the Qur'an's descending upon the heart with the attribute of majesty: “it gives it awe, majesty, modesty, vigilance, presence, fear, brokenness, lowliness, lack, constriction, guardedness, attentiveness and the feeling of exalting God's signs” (p. 283). The heart onto which the noble Qur'an descends is one of nobility: though in need, the person prefers the Real to himself, labors to meet the needs of all people, believer or not, “responds to evil with good, to sin with pardon, to error with forgiveness” (p. 285). Dost: “The most effective act by which a servant may acquire the attribute ‘noble’ is noble conduct such as binding himself to the Real's command and obeying Him” (p. 285). God looks in the world only at the human being (p. 286). Dost addresses the reader: “You must know what you are reading, to whom you are reading and who is listening as you read!” (p. 286).

Brotherhood: The Real-Believer Mirror

Chapter 335 (Muhammadan-Mosaic, the Station of Brotherhood) treats brotherhood at two ranks: the "highest brotherhood" between divinity and the fixed archetypes, and the brotherhood of faith among believers. The chapter contains a well-known poem: "people have spoken beliefs about God / I have believed in all that they believed" (pp. 289-290). Brotherhood is made concrete through a reciprocal mirror relation: "when the believing human being becomes the mirror of the Believer [al-Mu'min, a divine name], the Real looks upon him" (pp. 294-295).

Chapter 335 opens brotherhood with the brotherhood established at Medina: after the Hijra, as the Prophet made brotherhood among the Companions, he took 'Ali's hand and said “My brother is 'Ali”; God said “The believers are brothers” and made faith their father. Moses, when sent to Pharaoh, also said “make my brother my helper, make him a partner in my affair” (p. 288). Dost: “there is a sound and valid brotherhood between the station that unites all the divine names effective in the possible beings (divinity) and the station that gathers the receptivities of the possible beings' capacities (the rank of the fixed entities). These two stations are two brothers of one father, and each supports the other” (p. 288). On knowing God: “The perfect knower is one who knows God in all the forms in which He discloses Himself and descends. The one who is not a knower recognizes God only in the form he believes in, and denies Him when He discloses Himself in another form” (p. 290).

Dost takes up the saying “Incapacity to attain perception is itself a perception”: one who holds this says that knowing God is the incapacity of the person who proceeds from theoretical reflection; for Dost the right view is that God can be known and seen. On praise he mentions a poet's line: “Do not mind that we praise you as ‘righteous’! / You are above our praise”; what he himself says is: “If we praise you as ‘righteous’ / It is you who praises, not we” (pp. 291-292). Dost: “Our praise of God is renewed, for in every moment we are in, our knowledge of God is renewed and we praise God with new knowledge” (p. 292). When a dispute arises between the Real the Believer and the created believer, God commands the scholars of unveiling “Reconcile your brothers”; the scholars say to the human believer, “You must know that the Real the Believer knows Himself better than you do” (p. 293).

The place of causes is set out thus: “So causes are like instruments for the creator. Creating and the created thing are therefore ascribed to the doer, not to the instrument.” The word “Be” is an instrument through which the Real brings into being (pp. 295-296). Looking at Moses's “Make him a partner in my affair” and “Close my gap with him”, Dost says mutual help is this: “As long as the servant is helping his brother, God is helping the servant” (p. 296). God made believers brothers; He did not establish brotherhood between believer and unbeliever; “A believer does not shun a believer, a believer does not kill a believer, for he possesses faith” (p. 297). Among the chapter's knowledges he also lists: it is told that natural scientists produced from human semen, by a certain measure and time, a being in human form, which lived a year and could open and close its eyes but could not speak (p. 298).

The Circumference of the Circle Protects Existence: The Center-Circle Relation

Chapter 336 (Muhammadan, the Station of Plants Pledging Allegiance to the Pole of the Time), in its opening poem, makes a brief reference to the Cloud (Amâ): "He descends from His Throne / and to His Throne He had descended from the Cloud / without modality or distinction" (p. 299). The chapter contains this passage: "the circumference of the circle protects existence. Existence's relation to the point from which it issued is like the relation of the encompassing circle's existence to encompassment" (p. 301). The chapter also extends the concept of allegiance down to the level of nature (plant, animal, mineral), and describes the human being as a "tree" (tasha'jur, conflict) created from opposites (p. 303).

Chapter 336 ties allegiance to the single one of the age: “General allegiance can be given especially to the single one of the age (wahid al-zaman). For the single one of the age is the one who appears in beings with the divine form.” When Abu Yazid was asked about people touching him for blessing, he said: “They are not touching me; they are touching an adornment with which my Lord has adorned me” (p. 299). Abu Madyan too gave the example of the Black Stone and said “I am that very stone” (pp. 299-300). On almsgiving Dost distinguishes the Sufis' view from his own unveiling: “When the beggar opens his hand to receive alms from the giver, the Real puts His hand upon the beggar's hand. So before the alms fall into the beggar's hand […] they fall into the hand of the Rahman”; the Rahman increases it until it is the size of Mount Uhud (p. 300).

The manner of allegiance is described thus: “When God appoints one person to look after the world, called the pole, the single one of the age, the ghawth and the caliph, He sets down a couch for him in the rank of likeness and seats him upon it.” God clothes him with all the divine names he requires and commands the world to give him allegiance “in joy and in grief, we will hear and obey” (p. 301). The highest assembly (Mala' a'la) is in constant joy and does not know the taste of grief (pp. 301-302). As each spirit enters his presence to give allegiance, the Pole asks it a matter about God, “Do you say such-and-such?”, and answers by relating one aspect of knowledge about God (p. 302). The order of allegiance: first the first intellect, then the universal soul, angels, the spirits that govern bodies, the jinn and the offspring; the exceptions are the great angels devoted entirely to worship and, among humans, the Afrad (p. 302).

Dost distinguishes the relation between the outward head of state and the pole: “The pole of the age may at times himself be the imam. As examples, Abu Bakr […] At times the pole of the age is not the head of state.” In that case the outward caliph, without being aware of it, is one of the deputies of the inward pole (p. 303). Headship is an offer: “Whoever asks for an office is left to it. To whomever an office comes without his asking, God assigns an angel to help him” (p. 305). On the permissible Dost notes: “If a head of state (imam) to whom you have given allegiance to hear and obey gives a command concerning anything permissible, obeying him is obligatory and opposing his command is forbidden. In that case the ruling on the permissible act becomes obligatory” (p. 307).

Why are plants the subject of allegiance? Dost: “plants are the intermediate world between mineral and animal and have the ruling of an ‘isthmus’. […] The isthmus is the mirror of the two extremes; whoever sees the isthmus sees the two extremes” (p. 307). The human body is like rootless grass and becomes a “tree” through the spirit breathed into it (p. 308). Dost describes the difference between the imam who takes knowledge from God and one who takes it from thought: “The greatest veil between God and the servant is the servant's knowledge arising from the self and his taking his knowledge from his thought and theoretical power”; he relates Abu Yazid's saying: “You take your knowledge from the dead […] We take our knowledge from the Living who does not die” (p. 309). At the end of the chapter Dost notes that when they reached this station in Fez, in the year 591, before it had occurred, they learned that the believers would be victorious over the unbelievers (p. 311).

Three Paths: Existence, Nonexistence, Barzakh

Chapter 337 (Mosaic, the Station of the Prophet's Standing Before Part of the World) enumerates six qualities particular to the Prophet: the treasures of the earth, comprehensive speech, being sent to all humanity, instilling awe from a month's distance, the licitness of spoils, and the earth being made a place of prostration. The chapter offers a threefold classification of paths: the path arriving at the Real's existence (believers/polytheists), the path of nonexistence arriving at no end at all (deists), and the barzakh path (the gnostics' path, characterized by neither existence nor nonexistence).

Chapter 337 begins with the Prophet's rank: “On the Day of Resurrection the Prophet says ‘I am the master of people’ and shows his perfection by reason of the comprehensiveness of his law.” According to the saying “I was a prophet while Adam was between water and clay”, the other prophets became prophets when they were sent (p. 312). The Praised Station “means opening the door of intercession for angels and for those besides them”; the Prophet is the first to intercede, and the Wasila is his (pp. 312-313). On the forms of works: the works of the felicitous see a way on their right to walk, which brings them to their owners; “the works of the wretched produce for them different, contradictory, tangled paths and they cannot tell which path will bring them to their owners” (p. 313). One who knows the stars understands that they were placed to find the right way (p. 314).

Dost describes the mujtahids of this community: “While God gave them legislative authority, He gave the Prophet's community the ‘authority of ijtihad’ in determining rulings, and recognized their right to rule according to the conclusion their ijtihad brought them to.” A mujtahid who errs is given one reward; “if his ijtihad hits the ruling the Prophet gave, he earns two rewards: the first is the reward of ijtihad, the other the reward of hitting the mark” (p. 315). As to his place on the Day of Resurrection, Muhammad stands before al-Hakam and al-'Adl; “His station in paradise is the Wasila. All the paradises issue from it. The Garden of Eden is the place of ‘abode’” (p. 316). The Prophet's station in knowledges is “to encompass the knowledge of all scholars who know God, who came before or after him” (p. 317).

The Close: The Honeycomb and Sunlight

Chapter 337 closes by declaring the number six "the most perfect number," supporting this with the example of the honeycomb: "God is the most perfect of numbers... God revealed to the bee, 'take houses from the mountains and the trees'... and it made its comb hexagonal" (pp. 316-317). The chapter closes with the analogy of sunlight spreading equally to all: "the Prophet is like sunlight: it spreads its rays over the earth. One who remains in the shadow of a wall... hidden from the rays, is one who refuses the light's spreading upon him and turns away from it. The sun has not obstructed him" (p. 320).

Dost unfolds the six characteristics one by one. The first is the treasures of the earth: the Prophet Joseph asked to be put in charge of the treasuries saying “I am a knowing guardian”; God says “The treasuries of everything are with Us, and We send it down only by a known measure” (p. 317). The second is the comprehensive words (jawami' al-kalim): “The Prophet was given knowledge of the infinite […] a single word he says becomes words” (p. 317). The third is his being sent to all people: “The Prophet was sent to all people (kaffatan)”; when the jinn heard the Qur'an they told their people “We have answered the one who calls to God” (p. 318). The fourth is “being supported by casting fear into his enemies from a month's distance”; the month is the measure of the moon's traversing the degrees of the sphere (p. 320). The fifth is booty: “Desire is a fire in a person that demands what he craves”; with earlier prophets a fire descending from heaven burned the booty (p. 321). The sixth is the earth being made a mosque and pure: “One who sits in mosques is one who sits with God” (p. 321).

On miracles Dost says: “The Prophet is only a warner and one who conveys what he is commanded to convey.” That he shows a miracle proving his truthfulness is not something within his power but God's bounty. The Qur'an is a miracle for the Arabs; it is understood that the unlettered Muhammad could know the information he brought only through God's informing (pp. 322-323). God assigned to him all kinds of revelation, giving him the sound dream and the casting into the heart and the ear (p. 323). Among the station's knowledges are listed the verse “For each of you We have appointed a law and a way; had He willed, He would have made you one community”, the ruling on the jizya, and Abu Yazid's saying “My grip is stronger” (pp. 324-325). Toward the end Dost says: “Here I do not mention all the knowledges, even if I knew them; I only point to some aspects […] this station contains” (p. 326).
