The Vision of Paradise, the Secret of the Law, and the Six Ranks of the Testimony of Faith

pp. 13–49

Site reading summary

Part XXIX does not simply continue where our text of Volume II left off — it opens a new volume (Volume III) entirely: in the text's own words, this is the beginning of the "Fifth Sifr." Across three chapters, after the two previous parts' material on Hell and Resurrection (Part XXVII's Hell/isthmus, Part XXVIII's Resurrection/revival), Chapter Sixty-Five, the part's longest chapter, describes Paradise — its ranks, its three kinds, and the scene of the Vision (ru'ya). Chapter Sixty-Six investigates which divine Name lies behind the outward authority of the revealed Law — here there is a council scene in which the possible things wander among the divine Names seeking to pass from non-existence into existence (al-Bari', al-Qadir, al-Murid, al-'Alim, the name Allah, al-Rabb, al-Mudabbir, al-Mufassil). Chapter Sixty-Seven takes apart the testimony of faith — "there is no god but God and Muhammad is God's messenger" — examining the difference between knowledge and belief, the logic of negation and affirmation, and the six different ranks from which this single sentence can be uttered (by one's self, by one's attribute, by one's Lord…). The part closes, in the text's own words, with "God speaks the truth and guides to the right path," handing off to Part XXX.

Two Paradises: Sensory and Spiritual, and the Pleasures of the Rational Soul

The part turns to Paradise with a four-line poem, after the previous two parts' material on Hell and the Resurrection. From the outset Dost says Paradise is not one thing: "there are two paradises: the sensory paradise and the spiritual paradise. Reason knows them both together" (p. 13). The world divides in parallel — the subtle world / the dense world, or the unseen world / the visible world. The rational soul (nafs al-natiqa) has a bliss drawn from knowledge and gnosis, while the animal soul has a bliss drawn from sensory pleasures — eating, drinking, sexual union, beautiful forms; but Dost insists this sensory pleasure, too, actually belongs to the rational soul — rejecting the claim that "an animal, too, could take pleasure from the face of a beautiful woman" (p. 13).

The Four Names Paradise Longs For, and Four Human Ranks Before Paradise

Dost cites the hadith "Paradise longs for Bilal, Ali, Ammar, and Salman" and unpacks each of the four names in turn: Bilal from the root of wearying/growing weary of illness; Salman from emerging into wellness from pain and sickness; Ammar from the disappearance of pain through being filled/inhabited; and Ali points to Paradise's rising above the Fire through this self-disclosure (p. 13). "How fine is the harmony among these names!" Dost says (p. 13). He then divides humanity into four ranks before Paradise: those who desire and are desired (prophets, perfected saints), those desired but not desiring (people of states, overtaken by awe), those who desire but are not desired (sinning believers), and those neither desiring nor desired (those who deny the Resurrection) — "there is no fifth to these four classes" (p. 14).

Three Paradises — Specific Grace, Inheritance, Deeds — and the Many Ways of Ranking Excellence

Dost divides Paradise into three kinds: first the "paradise of specific divine grace" — for children who die before the age of accountability, the insane, and those of the interval between prophets (p. 13); second the "paradise of inheritance" — dwellings prepared for the people of Hell, which believers instead inherit (p. 13); third the "paradise of deeds" — a graded paradise entered in return for one's works (pp. 13-14). He grounds this in the hadith where Bilal's "performing ablution and praying two rak'as" is said to have let him "overtake" the Prophet in Paradise: every act of worship, even every abstention from a disliked act, has its own particular paradise (pp. 14-15). He then shows how many axes excellence runs along: age (priority in devotion), time (Ramadan, Friday, the Night of Power), place (the Sacred Mosque > Medina > al-Aqsa), state (praying in congregation), and the deed itself — "prayer is a more excellent deed than removing something harmful from the path of passersby" (p. 15).

Dost's Dream: The Two Missing Bricks in the Wall of the Kaaba

Having explained that the creation of the afterlife differs from that of this world — "spirituality predominates over corporeality" — Dost cites the Prophet's hadith: "my place among the prophets is like that of a man who built a wall and left one brick missing; I am that missing brick" (p. 16). He then recounts his own dream: in 599 AH, in Mecca, he saw the Kaaba built of gold and silver bricks; in two rows of the wall (gold above, silver below) two bricks were missing; "suddenly I found myself set in the place of those two bricks... I was the very likeness of those two bricks" (p. 16). He reads the dream as pointing to a place among the prophets resembling the Prophet's own — within his own class, that of the saints — a hope of being the "seal of sainthood": "I hope that I will be the one with whom God brings sainthood to its close" (pp. 16-17).

Eden and al-Wasila: A Hundred Degrees, Eight Paradises, and Four Classes of Those Who Enter

Dost says the paradises of deeds number "a hundred degrees" — just as the Fire, too, has a hundred layers (p. 17). The highest paradise is Eden, "the capital of Paradise"; Firdaws, Khuld, Na'im, Ma'wa, Dar al-Salam, and al-Muqama follow it (p. 17). Al-Wasila is the highest degree within Eden — allotted to the Prophet solely through his community's prayer (p. 17). The degrees of Paradise total "five thousand one hundred and five degrees", though Dost adds that this is only the portion the people of unveiling agree upon; the true number may be greater (pp. 17-18). He then divides those who enter Paradise into four classes: prophet-messengers, saints, believers, and "the knowers who know through rational proofs that there is no god but God" (p. 19) — here Dost stresses there are only two ways of knowing God: unveiling, and rational proof/inference; "there is no third" (p. 19). These four classes will occupy four distinct stations when they see the Real at the White Dune: messengers/prophets on pulpits, saints on cushions and thrones, those who know by reason on chairs, and imitative believers standing before them (p. 19).

The Vision Scene at the White Dune and the Divine Address

After the feasts, foods, and robes — "the like of which they had neither seen nor imagined, whether in this life or in the paradises of deeds" (pp. 19-20) — everyone proceeds to the White Dune, but they take their places there "not according to the measure of their deeds, but according to the measure of their knowledge of God" (p. 20). A light flashes; all fall prostrate; the light penetrates their bodies and the subtle parts of their souls, so that "each person becomes wholly eye, wholly ear" — as Moses heard his Lord's speech with all his limbs (p. 20). Between the Real and His servants stand veils of might, greatness, and majesty; once lifted, God appears "through the Names al-Jamil and al-Latif" (pp. 20-21). Dost then transmits, in full, a long hadith related by Abu Bakr al-Naqqash — God's direct address to His servants: "I am your Lord, whom you worshipped, loved, and feared without seeing Me... now ask of Me! Do not fear, and do not be ashamed!" (pp. 21-22). At the address's end, the servants ask only to gaze upon the Real's "honored face" and for His good pleasure; God answers, "that My good pleasure with you shall endure" (pp. 22-23) — Dost asks, "could there be a sweeter word or a more delightful glad tiding than this?" (p. 23).

The Imagined Bliss: When Longing Is Fulfilled in Paradise, and Two Kinds of Fire

Dost describes a form of mercy directed at the people of Hell: God at times cools the Fire for them, but then afflicts their insides with the thought, through the faculty of imagination, of the good deeds they neglected — "the suffering they feel together with this imagining is more severe than the suffering of the sensory fire" (pp. 23-24). He sums this up in two lines: "Fire is of two kinds: the first is fire wholly made of flame / the other is fire as a meaning that reaches the spirits" (p. 24). For the people of Paradise the reverse holds: a person imagines or longs for a bliss, and then, according to that imagining, finds himself within that very bliss — "a bliss arising from the paradise of specific grace and its own bliss" (pp. 24-25). Dost supports this with the hadith about a poor man who longs for the good deeds — almsgiving, freeing slaves — that a wealthy man performs but which his own poverty prevents him from doing: "these two men are equal in reward" (p. 25).

The Council of the Names: The Possible Things' Plea for Existence, from al-Bari to al-'Alim

The chapter opens with two verses — "had there been on earth angels walking about in peace, We would have sent down to them from heaven an angel as messenger"İsrâ 17:95 and "We do not punish until We send a messenger" (p. 27) — and Dost immediately reminds us that the outward multiplicity of the divine Names is really only a matter of relations, the Real's Essence being one qua Essence (pp. 27-28). He then moves to the passage in which the possible things, in their state of non-existence, wander among the divine Names seeking existence. The Names first turn to al-Bari; al-Bari says, "this request should be directed to the Name al-Qadir" (pp. 28-29); al-Qadir, in turn, says "I am under the encompassing of the Name al-Murid" and passes it on (p. 29); al-Murid says his own knowledge depends on al-'Alim's ruling (p. 29).

The Name Allah: The Rank of Gathering, and the Exception of al-Wahid

The Names finally gather in the name Allah. The name Allah tells them, "I am the name that holds all your realities together, just as I am the proof for the One named" (p. 29), and enters the presence of the sacred Essence to convey the possible things' request. The Essence answers: "In Myself, by My very essence, I am One. The possible things request not Me in My absoluteness, but only My rank... the divine Names belong not to Me but to the rank" (pp. 29-30). There is a single exception: "the Name al-Wahid alone stands outside this; al-Wahid belongs to Me alone. No Name, no rank, and no possible thing can share with Me in that Name's reality, in any respect" (p. 30).

al-Rabb Steps In: al-Mudabbir, al-Mufassil, and Wisdom-Based Laws (Nawamis)

Once beings emerge into outward existence and begin to press upon one another (one Name "crushing" another), the possible things realize they need a "presiding Name" — one who will set a limit for both themselves and the Names (pp. 30-31). Al-Rabb takes on this task, appointing two viziers: al-Mudabbir and al-Mufassil — alluding to the verse "He directs the affair (tadbir), He details the signs (tafsil)" (p. 31). Al-Rabb then establishes a twofold system to set the world in order: one is "wisdom-based governance" (nawamis) — laws that intelligent people, in the absence of a revealed Law, put in place unknowingly through an inspiration, protecting property, life, and lineage; but those who established these laws "did not know that there was such a thing as the afterlife", and had "merely invented a kind of monasticism" (p. 32). The other is revealed Law proper, taken up in the next section.

The Limit of Reason, the Coming of Revelation, and Ibn Rushd's Testimony

Dost describes how a group of people, proceeding by theoretical reason, arrives at "al-Awwal" — a principle "needing nothing, having no likeness" — but can go no further than this (pp. 32-33). Then, when one among them stands up and says, "I am the prophet God has sent to you," these people neither reject nor accept him at once — they examine his proofs; seeing that he is "unacquainted with the knowledge produced by [rational] reflection," that is, that he possesses knowledge he did not reach by their own rational methods, they come to believe him (p. 33). Dost sets apart from this the disputatious sort who pass for "intelligent" today, but who "mock religion" and whose "hearts are filled with love of the world," and criticizing them (pp. 34-35). He closes the chapter with his own testimony: he relates that he came upon a few people who followed the path; that one of their elders (in the translator's parenthesis: Ibn Rushd) witnessed the knowledge opened to Dost through being alone with God, "without any research or reading," and said, "praise be to God that I was present at a time when I saw one to whom God grants mercy and teaches knowledge from His own presence" (p. 35).

The Difference Between Knowledge and Belief: Angels, Knowers, and the People of the Interval

The chapter opens with a six-line poem — a chain of testimony beginning with God's own witness to Himself in pre-eternity, then the angels, then the knowers, then the Prophet (p. 37). Dost draws attention to why the verse — "God, the angels, and those endowed with knowledge who uphold justice bore witness that there is no god but He"Âl-i İmrân 3:18 — specifically says "those endowed with knowledge": "not 'those endowed with belief,' because testimony to God's oneness does not arise from a report, which is what belief would be" (p. 37). That is, angels and knowers testify to God's oneness not because they heard it from a prophet, but through direct knowledge (one through self-disclosure, the other through rational reflection). Belief, by contrast, only comes into play once a prophet has arrived — which is why Dost says that those who lived in the interval between prophets (before any prophetic call reached them) and knew God's oneness through knowledge — "like Quss ibn Sa'ida" — will enter Paradise "with certainty and without doubt," though counted neither as believers nor as those "followed" by others (pp. 39-40).

The Science of Sand-Lines, the Stars, and a Comparison with Prophetic Knowledge

Dost uses a comparison to explain how prophecy is verified: the difference between experts who draw conclusions from the movements and conjunctions of the stars (sand-line diviners, practitioners of the science of lines) and the degrees of knowing God. Such people's knowledge resembles "the customary causes that everyone accepts, and whose acceptance no one is charged with unbelief for" — even where the observer has "inner certainty," this remains conjecture because of the matter's inherent obscurity (pp. 40-41). He cites the Prophet's hadith on the "science of lines" (said to have been revealed to Idris): "whoever's line matches [the prophet's line], that is he" — meaning that even though this is a form of knowledge, "no definitive judgment is made by it" (pp. 41-42). Against this he sets "those who call to God with insight" — the prophet and his heirs: theirs rests not on conjecture but on certain knowledge (p. 41).

"There Is No God But God": The Logic of Negation-Affirmation, and Six Ranks of Utterance

Dost analyzes the logic of the testimony of faith: "the negation of a negation is an affirmation... the nonexistence of nonexistence is existence" (p. 44). "La ilaha" (there is no god) negates not something that does not exist, but the "gods" the polytheists invented, which in reality carry no divinity — "in truth the polytheist worshipped nothing but God; his error lay in associating that worship with one to whom it did not belong" (p. 45). The chapter also shows that this single sentence can be uttered from six different ranks: by one's self (from the Real's self-disclosure to itself — "the oneness of essence"), by one's attribute (through knowledge), by one's Lord (seeing that the Real is identical with existence itself), by one's Lord's attribute (from the lord-and-vassal relation), by one's state (turning to God out of desperation), and by one's ruling (uttering it purely because the Lawgiver commanded it, in imitation) (pp. 45-47). Dost remarks: "none of these classes is characterized by belief" — only the last, "the one who says it by ruling," is, in the proper sense, the imitative believer (p. 47).

The Union of the Two Testimonies, the Building-Metaphor of Islam, and Sunna versus Innovation

Dost explains why the testimony to prophecy is joined to the testimony of God's oneness: "the one God the Lawgiver proclaims is not the same as the God reason's own unifying grasps" — that is, the God to whom the Law calls can be characterized by attributes reason alone could never accept, such as "descending, sitting on the Throne, hesitating," because this is knowledge the Prophet brought (p. 48). He relays the Prophet's hadith "Islam is built on five things" as a structure: the testimony that there is no god but God is the heart of the matter, the testimony to Muhammad's prophethood the doorkeeper; the text first counts prayer as the right side, almsgiving the left, fasting the front and pilgrimage the back, then says "perhaps the front is prayer, for prayer is light," placing prayer in front, almsgiving on the right, pilgrimage on the left and fasting at the back (pp. 43-44). He closes the chapter, and the part, with the difference between sunna and innovation: whoever calls something "innovation" that the Lawgiver called "sunna" has not followed the sunna; for "following is better than producing" — "producing" means bringing something forth with no prior example, which belongs to God alone ("He who created the heavens and the earth without any precedent") (p. 49). The part closes with the sentence "God speaks the truth and guides to the right path"; the text states directly, "the Twenty-Ninth Part has ended; the Thirtieth Part will follow" (p. 49).
