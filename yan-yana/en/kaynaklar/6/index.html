  • Penrose 1996 — Penrose, On Gravity's role in Quantum State Reduction. Gen. Relativ. Gravit. 28, 581-600
    In brief, what does it say?

    The proposal that describes quantum state reduction (collapse) by tying it to the difference in the geometry given to space-time by two mass distributions.

    Penrose’s starting point is the measurement problem: quantum theory can describe a system as a superposition of several states, but a measurement gives a single outcome. According to Penrose, this requires quantum theory and general relativity to be considered together. His reason is this: the superposition of a mass located in two separate positions means a superposition of two different space-time geometries.

  • Procopio 2015 — Procopio, Experimental superposition of orders of quantum gates. Nat. Commun. 6, 7913
    In brief, what does it say?

    In an operation, doing A then B and doing B then A often give different results. Does quantum theory allow these two orders to exist together? In this experiment, which gate a photon passes through first is tied to the state of a second photon, so that 'the two orders together' is realized. In this setup, whether two operations give the same result when their order is swapped can be learned with fewer trials than by applying each operation one by one many times; this situation, known as a theoretical advantage, is demonstrated in the laboratory. This is an experimental result. The authors say that this can be read as a 'superposition of causal orders'; but this reading is an interpretation. What gives one pause is that the order of events can also behave like a quantum possibility.

  • Proietti 2019 — Proietti, Experimental test of local observer independence. Sci. Adv. 5, eaaw9832
    In brief, what does it say?

    The scientific method rests on facts that are independent of who observes and are accepted by everyone. In quantum mechanics, however, the objectivity of observations is not so clear; in Wigner's famous thought experiment two observers seem to live different realities from one another. Can these realities be reconciled independently of the observer? Recently an extended version with four observers was set up, which made it possible to test this question. The team realized this version in a six-photon experiment and violated the relevant Bell-type inequality by five standard deviations. This is an experimental finding. The interpretive part is conditional: if the assumptions of locality and free choice are kept, quantum theory must be interpreted as observer-dependent. So the result, without giving up one of the assumptions, strains the idea of a 'fact that is the same for everyone'.

  • Pusey 2012 — Pusey, On the reality of the quantum state. Nat. Phys. 8, 475
    In brief, what does it say?

    The quantum state is the basic mathematical object of the theory. Yet physicists have not agreed on what it actually represents. One view says that a pure quantum state corresponds directly to reality. Another view has long held that the state represents only information about some aspect of the system. The paper gives a theoretical result: any model that counts the quantum state as mere information about an underlying physical state, and that assumes independently prepared systems have independent physical states, must make predictions that contradict quantum theory. This is not an experiment but a mathematical result; its validity depends on these two assumptions. What gives one pause is that approaches saying 'the state is only information' are closed off under these conditions.

  • Ringbauer 2015 — Ringbauer, Measurements on the reality of the wavefunction. Nat. Phys. 11, 249
    In brief, what does it say?

    Quantum mechanics describes nature with extraordinary success, but what the wave function is remains unclear: does it represent our limited knowledge about a system, or does it correspond directly to reality? Earlier theoretical results said that if an objective reality exists the wave function must be real, but they rested on contested assumptions. This work takes another path without those assumptions and, with single photons, places an experimental bound on how much information interpretations can explain of quantum phenomena. The finding: in three- and four-dimensional states, no information interpretation can fully explain the limited distinguishability of non-orthogonal quantum states. This is an experimental result. The interpretive part is conditional: if the idea of an objective reality is accepted, the result strengthens the view that the wave function corresponds directly to reality. The debate is still going on.

  • Ritchie 1991 — Ritchie, Realization of a measurement of a "weak value". Physical Review Letters 66, 1107-1110

    This paper is the first experimental demonstration of the weak value concept proposed theoretically in 1988. The experiment is done in an optical setup: the polarization of the light is preselected, a crystal shifts the light beam very slightly depending on polarization, and the beam is then postselected on another polarization. The measured quantity is the shift of the beam.

  • Robertson 1929 — Robertson, The uncertainty principle. Phys. Rev. 34, 163
    In brief, what does it say?

    In 1927 Heisenberg had said that the position and the velocity (momentum) of a particle cannot be known at the same time with arbitrary precision. Robertson takes this idea from a single pair and ties it to a general rule: for any two measurable quantities that do not 'commute' with each other, the product of their uncertainties has a lower bound. That is, the more sharply one is known, the blurrier the other remains. Here 'uncertainty' does not mean the crudeness of the measuring instrument but the spread of measurement results for the same state. This is a result of the theory, an inequality proved by mathematics; it has been tested many times by experiment. What is disputed is whether this concerns nature itself or our knowledge; Robertson does not enter this question. What gives one pause is not 'something we do not know' but two certainties that do not exist at the same time.

  • Rovelli 1996 — Rovelli, Relational quantum mechanics. Int. J. Theor. Phys. 35, 1637
    In brief, what does it say?

    Rovelli asks: does quantum theory describe the state of a system 'on its own,' or the relation of one system to another? His proposal is this: the properties of a thing exist not absolutely but relative to another system that observes it or interacts with it. For the same event, two different observers, each in accordance with the rules, may say different things; this is the strange-looking part. The author argues that the unusual features of quantum theory follow naturally from this idea of 'being relative to a viewpoint.' This is not an experiment; it is a proposal about the interpretation of the theory, and it is as disputed as the other interpretations; no experiment has decided between one and another. The attractive and unsettling side of the approach is that it puts the sentence 'it is so relative to this' in place of the sentence 'the world is thus.'

  • Rovelli 2009 — Rovelli, Forget time. arXiv:0903.3832 (Found. Phys. 41, 1475, 2011)
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper argues that the best way to understand quantum gravity is to build a picture of the world in which the notion of time plays no role. The author summarizes this standpoint: in a fundamental description 'we have to forget time'. He explains how this can be done in classical theory and in quantum theory. The idea is to develop a formalism that treats dependent and independent variables on the same level. In short, the author's proposal is to interpret mechanics not as a theory of the evolution of variables in time but as a theory of the relations between variables. This is a proposal of theory and standpoint; not an experiment, and the author's own advocacy. What gives one pause is that time may be, fundamentally, not a precondition but something derived from relations.

  • Rozema 2012 — Rozema, Violation of Heisenberg's measurement-disturbance relationship by weak measurements. Phys. Rev. Lett. 109, 100404
    In brief, what does it say?

    The idea Heisenberg first stated was this: the more precisely we measure something, the more we disturb it as we measure. This 'measurement-disturbance' relation is in many places taught together with the uncertainty principle; yet what has been rigorously proved is the relation in which the uncertainties within a system itself (for example the spreads of position and velocity) are tied to each other. Rozema and colleagues followed the polarization of a photon, a particle of light, before and after with a method called 'weak measurement,' which hardly disturbs the system, and extracted the error and the disturbance separately. In the end they saw that the relation in Heisenberg's original form was exceeded, while the corrected relation proposed later by Ozawa remained valid. This is an experimental result. Why it gives one pause: the widely told principle does not turn out to be true as it stands; what is firm and what is disputed must still be kept apart. What weak measurement means is also a separately debated subject.

  • Rugh 2002 — Rugh, The quantum vacuum and the cosmological constant problem. Stud. Hist. Phil. Mod. Phys. 33, 663
    In brief, what does it say?

    The cosmological constant problem arises where general relativity and quantum field theory meet, and is counted among the fundamental problems of modern physics. The paper recounts the historical and conceptual origin of this problem; the problem is closely tied to the concept of the vacuum in quantum field theory. The authors critically discuss how the problem rests on the idea of a 'physically real vacuum energy'. They also lay out which relations between general relativity and quantum field theory are assumed for the problem to be well defined. This is not an experiment; it is a conceptual and historical study, that is, at the level of interpretation. What gives one pause is that whether the vacuum is counted as a real thing carrying energy determines the problem itself.

  • Schlosshauer 2005 — Schlosshauer, Decoherence, the measurement problem, and interpretations of quantum mechanics. Rev. Mod. Phys. 76, 1267
    In brief, what does it say?

    According to quantum theory, before it is measured a system can be in a state in which several possibilities enter the calculation together (superposition). Yet when we measure, we always see a single result; this mismatch is called the measurement problem. Schlosshauer's paper is a review of the phenomenon called decoherence and of how much it helps with this problem. Decoherence is this: a small system inevitably interacts with its environment (air, light, heat), and because of this the traces by which the joint presence of possibilities could be captured, that is, interference, are erased very quickly. This is a matter of experiment and calculation and is well understood. The paper also asks whether this explains why a single result appears, and examines the main interpretive approaches (Copenhagen, many worlds, hidden variables and others) one by one within this frame. The point the article reaches is that decoherence explains why results look classical, but does not by itself close the question 'why only one'; that part depends on interpretation.

  • Schlosshauer 2013 — Schlosshauer, A snapshot of foundational attitudes toward quantum mechanics. Stud. Hist. Phil. Mod. Phys. 44, 222
    In brief, what does it say?

    The foundations of quantum mechanics, through experimental and theoretical work, gave birth to quantum information science, but fundamental questions are still fiercely debated in the physics community and there is no agreement on the main issues. This work is neither a theory nor an experiment but a survey: 16 multiple-choice questions were put to 33 people attending a conference on the foundations of quantum mechanics. The participants included physicists, philosophers and mathematicians. The authors extract the common views and the strong, moderate and weak correlations between answers. What emerges is a snapshot of views in the field at that moment; that is, a picture drawn from a sample of 33 people. It should not be read as a general verdict as it stands. What gives one pause is that even experts in this field still do not meet at the same place.

  • Schlosshauer 2019 — Schlosshauer, Quantum decoherence. Phys. Rep. 831, 1
    In brief, what does it say?

    According to quantum theory, before it is measured a system can be in a state in which several possibilities come together (superposition). In the everyday world, however, we always see the table in a single place. This review article describes a process called 'decoherence': the system inevitably interacts with its environment (air, light, heat), and the environment carries a kind of trace about the system; because of this, the interference between the possibilities disappears and the system begins to look like something classical. Decoherence can be calculated and has been measured in experiments; this part is solid. What is disputed is whether it explains 'why a measurement gives a single result'. According to most authors it does not explain this by itself; it only explains why the interference is not seen. The article is a review that also speaks openly about this limit.

  • Schrödinger 1926 — Schrödinger, An Undulatory Theory of the Mechanics of Atoms and Molecules. Phys. Rev. 28, 1049
    In brief, what does it say?

    Schrödinger summarized, in English, his wave equation and how the energy levels of the hydrogen atom are found with it (a summary of the four-part series 'Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem' in Annalen der Physik).

    This paper is an English summary of the work Schrödinger published in German in 1926 in four parts. The starting point was this: why the energy of atoms can take only certain values was being explained at the time by rules called 'quantum conditions' that were added from outside. Instead of adding these rules from outside, Schrödinger proposed to show that the solutions of a wave equation are, by themselves, well-behaved (finite and single-valued) only for certain values. Much as a taut string can vibrate only at certain frequencies.

  • Shalm 2015 — Shalm, Strong loophole-free test of local realism. Phys. Rev. Lett. 115, 250402
    In brief, what does it say?

    A Bell test asks this: could nature be working by a rule in which everything is 'local' and 'predetermined'? Local reality means that the effect of a thing does not spread faster than light and that it already has a definite value before being measured. In older Bell experiments there were small 'loopholes' that allowed the result to be fitted into this rule. This work used entangled photon pairs. The parties were placed so far apart that not even light could travel between the measurements; the measurement choices were made with fast random number generators; the detectors were efficient enough. Thus the main loopholes that could save local reality were closed at the same time. By the authors' calculation, the result makes local reality untenable even at a very small probability (the paper gives p-values on the order of 10 to the power of minus 9 and 10 to the power of minus 7). This is an experiment and it is firm; what is disputed is which interpretation the conclusion drawn from it should be told in. What gives one pause is that the intuition we express as 'it was definite even before being measured' was really put to the test and did not hold.

  • Shor 1994 — Shor, Algorithms for quantum computation: discrete logarithms and factoring. Proc. 35th Annual Symposium on Foundations of Computer Science (FOCS 1994), 124–134; full version: SIAM J. Comput. 26 (1997) 1484–1509, arXiv:quant-ph/9508027
    In brief, what does it say?

    A paper giving polynomial-time algorithms on a quantum computer for factoring integers and computing discrete logarithms.

    The paper's starting question is whether the computer is a universal computing device: can a classical computer simulate any physical computing device with at most a polynomial loss of time? Once quantum mechanics is taken into account, this is uncertain. The paper takes up two problems: factoring integers and finding the discrete logarithm. Both are considered hard on a classical computer and are the basis of many cryptographic proposals.

  • Shor 1995 — Shor, Scheme for reducing decoherence in quantum computer memory. Phys. Rev. A 52, R2493
    In brief, what does it say?

    Quantum computers hold information in superposition, and this state is disrupted by the smallest interaction with the environment (decoherence). This seemed to make long computations impossible. Shor proposed a quantum counterpart of classical error-correcting codes: by spreading a single quantum bit over several physical bits, one detects and corrects a disturbance arising in one bit without reading the information. The idea is to measure only the question 'is there an error?' without looking at the information itself. The paper shows that this route works under the assumption that each bit is disturbed separately. This is a proposal of theory and method; it was tried experimentally in the following years. What gives one pause is that, although we cannot copy quantum information, a way of protecting it has been found.
