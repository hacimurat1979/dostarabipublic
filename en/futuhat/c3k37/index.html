The Speech Inside Prayer: The Takbir, the Turning of the Face, and the Reading of the Fatiha

pp. 297–346

Site reading summary

Part Thirty-Seven continues the discussion of "The Secrets of Prayer" opened in Part Thirty-Six — still no new chapter begins, the same Chapter 69 continues. But this part moves at a different pace than the previous one's twenty-six short FASILs: it consists of fewer but much longer and denser stopping points of hidden material. The part opens with the debate over the imam's and congregation's intention and the takbir, then enters the tawajjuh prayer ("I have turned my face toward the One who created the heavens and the earth") — where Dost explains at length why the phrase "I have turned..." is attributed to the servant by the Real's own command, and how the turning can be read across seven distinct "states" (from God, with God, to God, alongside God, in God, for God, before God). It then enters the part's largest section, the verse-by-verse reading of the Fatiha: here prayer is read as a mutual conversation (munajat) woven around the holy hadith "I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant" — each verse receives a response from the Real. Embedded within this section is a long story of a boy who learns to read the Qur'an with an ever-deepening awareness of presence, and dies within three days. The part closes — without foreshadowing the next — with the debate over recitation and supplication during the bowing (ruku), and the hidden note that the tashahhud derives from the word "presence" (shuhud).

The Imam's and the Congregation's Intention

The scholars of the Law disagreed over whether the congregation's intention must match the imam's. Dost's own view is that this correspondence is not required — because intention is a hidden work of the heart, while being an imam is realized through something perceptible (actually leading the prayer). Since what the Lawgiver detailed were acts perceived by the senses, He did not include intention — being the heart's act — within that detailing.

For Dost, the divine vastness makes it impossible for the Real to disclose Himself to one person twice, or to two persons in the same way. Whoever knows this also knows that the congregation's intention connects to the imam's only through a shared set of acts — "each has what he intended." For what is intended in the self-disclosure is a grace flowing from the discloser to the one disclosed to; the servant's own aim is to take pleasure and gain knowledge through that disclosure.

The States Within Prayer

Prayer consists of a set of words and acts, but their ruling depends on states — for all worship rests on states. Dost illustrates this with two examples: when Imam Malik was asked about the hippopotamus, he ruled it "forbidden"; when told "but is it not a sea fish," he answered, "yes, but you called it a pig" — the name determines the ruling. When forbidden wine turns to vinegar, it loses the name "wine," because the state that made it wine has vanished; the substance stays the same, yet the ruling shifts from forbidden to permitted.

Dost adds that on this point the outward and the hidden share the same ruling — "for the hidden interpretation of a ruling has, for the one who learns the matter from the religion, itself been made by the Law."

The Takbir in Prayer (Magnification)

The scholars of the Law split into three positions over which of prayer's takbirs are obligatory. Dost's hidden reading ties this to a condition: magnifying God is obligatory in every state, "but for this to be possible depends on the human being seeing himself." If a person sees nothing besides God — not even a being belonging to something besides God — the takbir is not obligatory; for against whom would God's greatness even be declared?

Dost speaks of those who claim to see no one besides God in seer, seen, and seeing alike; more general than this is what occurs in the state of fana (the effacement of the servant's human existence). Against this, if a person sees the Real through the most beautiful names — that is, through the relations among the names, the differences of rank between them — the takbir becomes unavoidable; for "the realities of the names occur by degrees" (al-Hayy governs all the names, while al-'Alim has a wider scope than al-Murid and al-Qadir). Whoever separates prayer from the rest of worship holds only the opening takbir obligatory — through it, one becomes aware of being restrained from whatever would remove him from this particular state called "prayer."

How the Takbir Is Recited in Prayer

The scholars disagreed over whether the takbir's wording must be exactly "Allahu akbar," or whether other forms carrying the same letters of magnitude (kaf, ba, ra) would suffice. Dost's preference is to hold closely to the Sunna: since no other wording came from the Prophet, what suits us best — whether or not we know that meaning — is to follow the very wording the Lawgiver chose.

The reasoning behind this is: "If the Law has chosen a particular wording from among words of the same meaning for a verbal act of worship, then, according to those who know God, it has intended and observed, among the synonymous words, a further meaning particular to that wording."

Prayer's Tawajjuh: The "I Have Turned My Face to You" Supplication

The legal debate concerns whether the tawajjuh prayer is obligatory. Its words run: "I have turned my face toward the One who created the heavens and the earth. I am not one of the idolaters... My prayer, my devotion, my death, my life are for God, Lord of the worlds; He has no partner; I have been commanded thus, and I am the first of the Muslims."En'âm 6:163 Dost's own preference is this: the place of tawajjuh is not the obligatory prayers but the night vigil (tahajjud). In the obligatory prayers, between the takbir and the recitation, he prefers instead: "O God, distance me from my errors as You have distanced the East from the West" — holding that this is what the Sunna shows.

The Pauses of the One Who Prays

Dost counts three pauses within prayer: after the opening takbir and before beginning to recite; when the Fatiha ends; and after reciting, before going into the bowing. In the hidden reading, this holy hadith appears: "I have divided the prayer in half between Myself and My servant." The Prophet says: "Worship God as though you see Him." The one who prays "stands face to face" with his Lord in his qibla.

Munajat (mutual address), in Dost's words, comes from a verbal pattern (mufa'ala) that is "the act of two subjects" — prayer is one such act. When the servant says "praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds," God is the "listener" of this word; the servant, out of the reverence owed before the Real, falls silent to see what He says. "Attending to what the one you are conversing with says is a matter of reverence. To interrupt his speech... is to show disrespect."

Reciting the Basmala at the Start of Recitation

The scholars disagreed over whether the basmala within the Fatiha and other surahs should be voiced or silent, and in which rak'a. For Dost, reciting the "isti'adha-basmala" ("seek refuge in God from the accursed Satan"Nahl 16:98) when beginning to recite is obligatory — since this rests on a direct divine command. As for the basmala within the Fatiha and other surahs, "what is obligatory for the one who prays is to recite what comes easily from the Qur'an" — if the basmala comes easily, he recites it; if not, he bears no responsibility.

In the hidden reading, Dost connects the basmala — through the act of eating — to the verse "eat of that over which God's name has been mentioned," tying it to the report that one standing behind the imam "should give him food if he asks for it." Since the Qur'an is God's speech, whoever recites it — even without this name being explicitly stated — names God al-Mutakallim, the Speaking.

Recitation in Prayer: The Gathering Nature of the Qur'an and Prayer

The scholars disagreed over whether recitation in prayer is obligatory and how much of it, with Dost holding — alongside the majority — that reciting the Fatiha in every rak'a is required. His hidden reasoning proceeds through language: the word "Qur'an" means, in its linguistic root, "that which gathers"; prayer, too, is a gathering act. The servant can speak with his Lord only through His speech and the surah that gathers that speech (the "mother," umm) — that is, the Fatiha, for it is "umm al-Qur'an," that which gathers the Qur'an.

Dost extends this idea of gathering by likening standing (qiyam) to the written letter Alif: "the Alif is the root of the letters. All the letters... emerged from the Alif." In the same way, standing is a movement that gathers within itself all of prayer's elements — bowing, prostration, and sitting — "though sitting, in one respect, resembles standing, since sitting is half of standing." For this reason, according to Dost, the Fatiha's recitation remains obligatory even in the shortest prayer, the single-rak'a witr.

The Manner of Reciting in Prayer: "I Divided It Between Us"

Dost says that the one who prays (musalli) is, by linguistic root, "second" — but he immediately adds that this secondness is relative, not real: "what is meant is the secondness of faith" (occurring at its two points, testimony and prayer). He then turns to a detailed reading of the holy hadith: when God says "I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant," He names Himself first, then His servant; He says "between Me," then "and My servant." Yet in prayer it is the servant who speaks first — the servant saying "praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds," and only then does "God says..." follow.

Dost's emphasis is this: this is not a numerical firstness-secondness; "what is intended is to make known the ranks." Just as God does not "come forth" in respect of intellects or existence, the servant's words coming first does not require the Real to be second. What stands before the servant is the Real, and the servant addresses Him in munajat; "one converses with the Real not through words, but by possessing presence and awareness before Him." If the servant lacks presence (awareness), then it is only his tongue — not his reality or his existence — that says "praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds." God then says: "My servant's tongue has praised Me, not the one I commanded to speak with Me."

A Further Word on the Tawajjuh Hadith: "Purify Your Garment"

The Prophet's prayer continues: "O God, purify me of my errors as a white garment is purified of dirt." Dost traces the root of this request to the verse "purify your garment." In the hidden reading, the garment is the heart — the heart that houses the Real, as in the holy hadith "the heart of My servant has contained Me." The gnostic sees that if he sets out to purify his own heart by himself, this will only soil it further — "like one who cleans the filth on his garment with urine." The purification called for is to withdraw from the self and hand every matter over to God.

Here there is a small vasl that does not appear on the given fasl/vasl map: "the Rest of the Prayer" (appearing in the text, due to OCR, as "Duani Kalan Kısmı"). Here the Prophet's prayer "purify my errors with water, snow, and rain" is interpreted: water as life and vitality; snow (berd) as "the heart's coolness" — meaning happiness; and bered as the stillness of a fire once extinguished. Dost illustrates this with a poem — about camels unburdened, whose release cools the fire in some hearts while making others weep — and ties it to the thought that "everyone has a friend and an enemy": whoever knows God says "only God and I exist," nullifying everyone else before God — which, for Dost, is "true friendship."

The Most Perfect Prayer of Tawajjuh: Seven States

Dost offers an extended reading of why the tawajjuh opens with the words "I have turned my face": the servant, out of the reverence owed to being present before Him with awareness, attributes the face to himself — by his Lord's own command. "God has declared that He is the 'tongue' His servant speaks with" — meaning the servant does not produce these words on his own; the Real teaches them. A thing's "face" is its essence and its reality; through this utterance, the servant "raises up" his own essence toward the Real.

Dost says the tawajjuh can be read across seven distinct states: "the tawajjuh begins from God (min); its help and support rest with God, are with Him (bi); its aim and outcome are toward God (ila); it accompanies and watches, being together with God (ma'a); it hopes and desires, being in God (fi); it seeks nearness, being for God (li); it relies and trusts, being before/upon God (ala)."

The prayer's closing words run: "I belong to You and to You I shall return... whatever I am, You are. For I have gained existence only from You." It continues: "You are blessed... You are transcendent... I ask Your forgiveness and I turn back to You."

The Reading of the Fatiha in Prayer: Munajat

Dost first distinguishes three ranks within the refuge sought in the isti'adha-basmala. At the lowest rank, the servant seeks refuge not from what harms him, but from what does not suit his temperament. Higher up is the servant who says "I seek refuge in Your good pleasure from Your wrath" — watching not his own share but his Beloved's contentment. At the highest rank is the gnostic who says "I seek refuge in You from You" — for he has no existence of his own as himself; the One sought and the refuge itself are both Him. Dost calls this "the refuge of unification."

As for the basmala: when the servant says "in the Name of God, the All-Merciful, the Especially Merciful,"Fâtiha 1:1 God says "My servant has remembered Me." The Fatiha then proceeds verse by verse, each receiving a response from the Real: "al-hamdu lillah (praise belongs to God)" -> "My servant has praised Me"; "ar-Rahman ar-Rahim" -> again "My servant has praised Me" (this time through the quality of mercy); "Maliki yawm ad-din (Master of the Day of Judgment)" -> "My servant has exalted Me" or "My servant has taken refuge in Me." Dost explains at length that requital occurs not only in the hereafter but in this world too — even in pains as small as a flea's bite or a stumble — these pains being, insofar as they are grasped by the intellect, an atonement for forgotten sins.

As for the verse "iyyaka na'budu wa iyyaka nasta'in (You alone we worship, You alone we ask for help),"Fâtiha 1:5 Dost observes here: this verse comes as a barzakh (an intermediate rank) between the part of the Fatiha belonging to God and the part belonging to the servant — "the earlier part of the Fatiha belongs to God, while what remains belongs to the servant. This verse we are examining, however, is shared." The servant also uses the plural pronoun here ("we worship," "we ask") — because the eye, the ear, the tongue, the hand, the heart, each has its own worship; prayer is a worship in which all the human being's organs, inward and outward, take part together. Dost makes this concrete through the example of a boy (see the story below) who tested himself against this very verse.

The part continues with the verse "ihdina 's-sirat al-mustaqim (guide us on the straight path)" — here Dost recalls the name al-Hadi and the verse "my Lord is upon a straight path" (Surah Hud): the straight path is the very path upon which the Real Himself stands, and the servant necessarily follows the Real on that path. The Fatiha closes with "amin," spoken together by the angels and the servant's inward self (his spirit) — ending in the sentence: "the servant is raised up, the Real becomes his tongue, and the Real recites His own speech to the servant."

The Story of the Boy Who Read the Qur'an

Dost brings the verse "iyyaka na'budu" to life through a story he relates from his shaykh, Abu Bakr Muhammad b. Khalaf b. Saf al-Lakhmi. A boy would spend his nights reading the Qur'an until morning. The shaykh told him: "as you spend the night reading, imagine Me before you; do not forget Me." The next morning the boy said that despite the whole night, he had not even finished half the Qur'an. The shaykh then said: "picture in your qibla a Companion who heard the Qur'an from the Prophet himself" — that night the boy managed only a quarter. On the third night, told to "picture before you the Prophet to whom the Qur'an was revealed," he could read no more than a single juz'. On the fourth night, told to "think that you are reciting before Gabriel, who brought the Qur'an down into the Prophet's heart," he managed only a few verses.

On the fifth night, the shaykh said: "repent, glorify God, and know that the one who prays is speaking with his Lord." That night the boy fell ill; when the shaykh went to visit him, the boy said, weeping: "until last night I did not know I was a liar. When I began the Fatiha and reached the verse 'You alone we worship,' I saw that my own soul did not confirm me... I shrank from lying before God, and could not go into the bowing until dawn broke." On the third day the boy died; when the shaykh went to his grave and asked after his state, he received this answer from the grave: "I am now alive with the Living One / He called me to no account for anything." According to the one who transmits the story, the shaykh returned home, took to his bed, fell ill from the effect of the boy's state, and died not long after.

Recitation and Supplication in the Bowing: The Intermediate Rank

In the debate over whether reciting the Qur'an during the bowing is permissible, there is agreement that the bowing is the place for glorification ("Subhana Rabbi al-'Azim"). Dost explains why this glorification is tied to the name ar-Rabb: this name attaches not in an absolute sense but relative to the one who glorifies — "everyone's knowledge of God comes by degrees... each person glorifies the lord he believes to be his Lord." This is why, among the Qur'an's three most frequent principal names — Allah, Rahman, Rabb — Rabb is the one nearest, the most "personal," to the servant.

The hidden reading takes up standing, bowing, and prostration in turn. While standing, the servant seeks his spirit's principle — "God has said of spirits, 'you are the exalted ones.'" While prostrating, he seeks the body's principle of creation — water and earth. The bowing becomes, between the two, "a middle rank": just as "the existence the possible being acquires from the Real is something in-between the Necessary Being in itself and the possible in itself." Dost says this in-between existence is neither the possible itself, nor other than the possible, nor identical with the Real, nor other than the Real — carrying both aspects at once.

As for supplication during the bowing, Dost sees two different rulings as possible, tied to two different modes of witnessing: for the one whose witnessing is turned toward the Real, supplication is disliked (though not forbidden) — since the bowing represents the quality of "self-subsistence" with which the possible being is sometimes characterized; for the one whose witnessing is turned toward creation, supplication is permitted — "this is how the Sunna has been transmitted" — and this is Dost's own preference as well.

The Tashahhud in Prayer: Presence and Witnessing

The scholars disagreed over whether the tashahhud (the testimony recited while sitting in prayer) is obligatory. Dost draws attention to the word's root: tashahhud is the reflexive (tafa'ul) form of "shuhud" (seeing, presence). Shuhud means presence. Since the human being is commanded to have presence (to be aware) within prayer, the tashahhud is necessary in prayer — and "attaining presence is not valid unless it rests upon knowledge."

Here Dost compares two sources of knowledge: the intellect's own theoretical reasoning (which varies from person to person — "people's views about the deity differ widely") and what the prophet has conveyed through the Qur'an and the well-attested Sunna. The intellect can, on its own, establish God's existence, His oneness, the possibility of sending prophets — yet God has characterized Himself with certain qualities we could never accept by reasoning alone. Dost's preference is therefore clear: "standing before the Real in the tashahhud and in prayer with divine knowledge... is more correct than standing before God with the views of the intellect."

The part closes with these words: "After this, we shall take up the expressions found in the tashahhud of prayer... Part Thirty-Seven has come to an end. Part Thirty-Eight will follow it." The interpretation of the tashahhud's own wording is left for the next part.
