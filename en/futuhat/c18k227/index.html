Sifr 37 / Chapter 560 and the End of the Book — Counsels, Seeking Refuge and Prayers

Vol. XVIII, Thirty-Seventh Sifr / Chapter 560 (continued) and the End of the Book, pp. 321–450

Site reading summary

The last sifr of the Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya and the close of the book. Without opening a new chapter, the Thirty-Seventh Sifr continues the counsels of Chapter 560: Ibn Arabī first lists his own counsels on worship and dealings and says his aim is a worship that is "complete and without deficiency," outside the scholars' disagreements; he then transmits the Prophet's counsels to ʿAlī and Abū Hurayra, sayings of the righteous such as Dhū'l-Nūn, Fuḍayl, Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, divine counsels from the Qur'an, sacred hadiths and the Torah, and counsels to rulers —including his own letters to Sultan Kaykāwūs. The End of the Book contains formulas of seeking refuge and well-known prayers, a dream he saw in Mecca in 599, and the colophon stating that the book was completed in the year 636 in its second, thirty-seven-volume copy.

The Opening of the Sifr and Ibn Arabī's Own Counsels

The Thirty-Seventh Sifr opens no new chapter heading: after the basmala it begins directly with units titled “Counsel” and continues the counsels of Chapter 560; the previous sifr had closed with the sentence “The thirty-sixth sifr of the Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya has come to an end” (p. 320). In the first pages Ibn Arabī addresses the reader in the second person singular and lists short injunctions on worship, everyday conduct and dealings with others: not to be a guardian, envoy or witness among rulers; not to curse the wind, for “the wind is from the Breath of the All-Merciful” (p. 323); not to declare any sinner who faces the qibla an unbeliever; when food and prayer are ready together, to eat first; even if fear and hope are equal, to let hope and a good opinion of God prevail (p. 324); to beware of debt, overeating and anger; to follow the imam, and as imam to take account of the weakest in the congregation (p. 329); to give zakāt with the intention of delivering a right to the agent of its owner; to give the People of the House a gift rather than alms (p. 326). Ibn Arabī says that, in matters on which the scholars disagree, he points to the way that steps outside the disagreement, and he states its aim: “When you do this, the worship you perform becomes complete and without deficiency, such that there is no disagreement about it. This is my aim in the counsels.” (p. 331)

Among these injunctions Ibn Arabī places short reports and his own experiences. One of the Abdāl, walking through the air with his companions, wished to descend to a green garden to make ablution and pray; he fell from among the company and came down below their rank; Ibn Arabī says, “See what an important secret lies here!” (p. 325). He tells how a leading man of a city with leprosy, whom the doctors had declared incurable, was healed by rubbing himself with black seed and honey on the advice of Saʿd al-Suʿūd, a hadith scholar from Ible (p. 327). He relates that one night, fighting off sleep in prayer, he noticed he was saying bad words to himself instead of reciting, so he left the prayer and slept (p. 331); and that after reciting the prayer for a halting place he was stung by several scorpions without feeling pain, and that he does not know whether this came from the prayer, from two pieces of lead in his garment, or from both (p. 332). Counselling to throw dust at one who praises you to your face, he reports that his shaykh ʿAbd al-Ḥalīm al-Ghammād said of a mounted man, “A dust on a mount upon the dust” (p. 333). He then sets out the counsel to treat everyone according to rank and degree in a long list naming beings one by one: God, by fulfilling the covenant of lordship; animals, by attending to their needs because they are mute; trees and stones, by not using them for a needless purpose; the earth, by praying upon it; the divine names, by taking on their character traits. The list arrives at the sentence “So treat all beings with sincere counsel” (p. 335) and at the principle that “the basis of the matter is to give every rightful owner his right” (p. 336).

Prophetic Counsels: to ʿAlī and to Abū Hurayra

Under the heading “Prophetic Counsels” Ibn Arabī transmits the long testament the Prophet addressed to ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭālib with the words “O ʿAlī!” (pp. 336–344). The testament opens with sets of three signs —three signs each of the believer, of the affected, of the oppressor, of the hypocrite in show, of the dissembler and of the lazy— and, through comparisons that run “There is no poverty more severe than ignorance, no wealth more generous than intellect … no worship like reflection,” passes to a series naming the bane of each thing: “The bane of speech is lying, the bane of knowledge is forgetting, the bane of worship is showing off” (p. 337). Then come suras to be recited (the ten blessings of Yā Sīn, al-Dukhān, the Throne Verse, al-Ḥashr, Tabāraka, al-Sajda, al-Ikhlāṣ, al-Baqara), everyday prayers from eating to travelling and from seeing the new moon to looking in a mirror, the story of the words with which Adam repented (p. 342), and funeral prayers for a man, a woman and a child; what the believing servant gains on reaching forty, sixty, seventy, eighty, ninety and a hundred years of age is enumerated. The testament ends with the sentence “O ʿAlī! Heed my counsel: you are upon the Real, and the Real is with you” (p. 344).

Turning to the Prophet's counsels to Abū Hurayra, Ibn Arabī says he will mention of them “the part God dictated to my pen, with which I build the letters that point to meanings,” and he inserts a poem he wrote addressing the servant who lit his lamp: “Light the lamp, perhaps I may see / and set in order the meanings written on the leaves” (pp. 352–353). The counsels begin with saying “Bismillāh wa'l-ḥamdu lillāh” at ablution, at meals, on mounting an animal or boarding a ship, and on putting on a garment; they proceed through bearing harm patiently, an hour of justice being “better than two years of worship spent in night prayer and daytime fasting” (p. 354), not persisting in sin, night prayer, prompting the dying with the testimony of faith, caring for the orphan and the widow, guiding the stranded and the blind, and not publicising a wrong one has suffered (pp. 354–362). When, to the counsel “Have mercy on all God's creatures,” Abū Hurayra replies “I have mercy even on the flies in the water,” the Prophet says three times, “God has mercy on you” (p. 361). In another counsel the Prophet describes a people to come at the end of time, whom people will take for prophets on seeing their states, who prefer hunger to satiety and thirst to water, and he weeps out of longing for them (pp. 363–364). Ibn Arabī transmits, with a long chain he heard in Fez, one more report from Abū Hurayra: good neighbourliness makes one a Muslim, good companionship a believer, fulfilling the obligations a worshipper, and contentment with God's apportioning an ascetic (pp. 372–373).

Counsels from the Righteous, from Jesus and from Monks

The heading “Counsels of the Righteous” opens with sayings of Dhū'l-Nūn al-Miṣrī, who is also the righteous figure most often quoted throughout the sifr: “You should keep company with someone who reminds you of God when you see him” (p. 345); “Do not, while busying yourself with people's faults, remain unaware of your own” (p. 346); an enumeration running “… is not intelligent” (pp. 350–351); counsels set out in fives to Ibrāhīm al-Akhmīmī (pp. 364–365); and to a youth, “Gird on the weapon of blame” (p. 372). Ibn Arabī relates that something like one of Dhū'l-Nūn's sayings happened in his own circle: when the dead brother of his friend ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Ustādh said in a dream, “Only one who knows Him can see Him,” ʿAbd Allāh came to Seville, said “I have come to you so that you may make God known to me,” and stayed with them until he attained knowledge “by way of unveiling and witnessing —not by way of rational proofs” (p. 344). Also in this cluster are Jesus' saying likening this world and the next to the places where the sun rises and sets (p. 345), Luqmān's counsel to his son, “Sit with the scholars and cling to their knees” (p. 351), a saint's explanation that sees the cause of sin in the first glance (pp. 351–352), and Jesus' words to scholars and jurists, “You have turned toward the path of the Hereafter, but you do not walk toward it” (p. 352).

Under the heading “A Monk's Counsel to a Muslim Gnostic” a monk withdrawn into his monastery and a gnostic converse (pp. 347–350). The monk describes his relation with his Lord as “in the worst possible state”: the commands are heavy, the reward is deferred until after death, “the road is long, its thorns are many, there is bewilderment, there is fear of despair.” The gnostic says, “Our Lord gave us a great advance before we began to act”; he recounts man's creation from earth and from a drop and his being endowed with senses, intellect and speech, the blessings of this world being mixed with opposites such as light and darkness, night and day, and the creation of the Hereafter being “a creation not after any model”; and he says they count worship as “a blessing, a grace, a pride and an honour.” Also among the counsels transmitted are a monk near Damascus who speaks counsels and poems to a traveller on several mornings in a row and finally says, “You have kept me from worshipping my Lord” (pp. 374–376); ʿAbd Allāh al-Mughāwir of Niebla, who, faced with the woman who had sought his protection, pressed his desire between two stones and said, “O my soul! On one side the Fire, on the other disgrace!”, and his five counsels to Abū'l-Ḥasan al-Ishbīlī (p. 370); a scholar of the path who says that a person's most sincere friend is the one who does not let him say “I will, I shall; I hope, perhaps, it will be” (pp. 376–377); and ʿAlī b. Khaṭṭāb al-Jazarī, who saw the Real in a dream and heard that the gratitude of “one who answers with kindness someone who has done him wrong” is accepted (p. 378).

Divine Counsels: the Qur'an, Sacred Hadiths, the Torah

Ibn Arabī defines one counsel as “beneficial and true divine counsels consisting of the Real's commands and prohibitions addressed to His servants” in the Qur'an, and lists some of them “to remember, to awaken heedless hearts, and to draw blessing from His speech” (p. 378): beginning with Sūrat al-Baqara, verses in the form of command and prohibition are lined up one after another (pp. 378–381). He also counts God's mentioning in His Book the qualities He praises and blames as “a counsel from God” (p. 381), and explains them: those who believe in the unseen; the unbeliever whose heart and hearing are sealed; the hypocrite who shows one face to the believers and another to the unbelievers; those who asked for the vegetables the earth grows instead of manna and quails, descending from the high to the low by the command “Go down to a town”; hearts harder than stone, for from some stones rivers gush forth and some roll down for fear of God (pp. 381–383). He also counts pardon in retaliation, the bequest, and not interpreting the ambiguous verses according to speculative thought among the divine counsels for which there is an explicit text (pp. 384–385).

The divine counsels gathered from sacred hadiths open with the words “I am the One most free of need of being associated with” (p. 385). Among them are the hadith in which, at the Resurrection, God shows the servant who demands his right from his brother cities of silver and palaces of gold and says they are possessed “by forgiving your brother” (pp. 385–386); the twelve phrases Kaʿb al-Aḥbār found in the Torah and hung around his neck —“While everything wants you for itself, I want you for yourself, and you flee from Me” (p. 386)—; God's saying to Moses, “I sit with the one who remembers Me” (p. 387); and the words repeated three times, “I am not a Lord who causes hardship” (p. 390). A man inspired in the ancient ages of prophecy pleads, saying he was created without being consulted and left between opposing forces; God's answer is “I gave you commands only so that you would learn that you have a Lord and a God,” and He teaches the servant which prophet's words to say in which situation: lā ḥawla when strained, the words of return in calamity, the words of Adam and his wife on slipping into sin, of Abraham in perplexity, of Moses on erring, of Joseph on turning away from sin, of David in trial, of Jesus on seeing sinners (pp. 393–396). Also among these counsels are God's response at each verse to the servant reciting al-Fātiḥa (p. 406), the card bearing the testimony of faith that outweighs ninety-nine registers of sins in the balance (p. 408), and the hadith about the Qur'an memoriser, the rich man and the martyr: “These three are the first to be cast into the Fire on the Day of Resurrection” (p. 409).

Counsel to Rulers and the Letters to Kaykāwūs

A long stretch of the sifr transmits counsels given to rulers. Fuḍayl b. ʿIyāḍ reminds Hārūn al-Rashīd, who has come to his door at night, that ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz counted the caliphate an affliction, transmits Sālim b. ʿAbd Allāh's counsel “Fast from this world, and let your breaking of the fast be death,” and refuses the hundred dinars the caliph offers, saying “I show you the way of salvation, and you answer me like this” (pp. 366–369). Ḥasan al-Baṣrī tells Ibn Hubayra, governor of Iraq, “If you fear God, God will protect you from Yazīd b. ʿAbd al-Malik; but Yazīd b. ʿAbd al-Malik cannot protect you from God” (p. 401); ʿAbd Allāh al-ʿUmarī tells Hārūn at al-Ṣafā that each of the crowd will be called to account for himself, but he for all of them (pp. 402–403); a man at the circumambulation describes to al-Manṣūr how, under a ruler who shuts his gates and draws a veil between himself and the wronged, oppression multiplies in a chain through his officials, and says “Caliph! Islam cannot endure like this” (pp. 403–404); ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz relates, on returning from a funeral, that the grave called out to him and told him what it does to the dead (pp. 410–412).

Ibn Arabī also places his own writings among these counsels. To ʿIzz al-Dīn Kaykāwūs, sultan of the lands of Rūm, he wrote a reply in verse to one of his letters: “O ʿIzz al-Dīn, who makes God's religion mighty, hear us / Few are the kings who heed advice” (p. 401). Under the heading “Testament and Counsel” he transmits his reply to a letter Kaykāwūs wrote in the year 609: the reply states that the sultan's letter has reached “his (spiritual) father who prays for him, Muḥammad b. ʿArabī” and opens with the hadith “Religion is sincere counsel” (p. 435). Ibn Arabī reminds the sultan that God has made him His deputy in His lands, that he is “His shadow extended over the earth,” and that justice is in his favour and in his subjects', while oppression is against him; he counts the lifting of the conditions ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb imposed on the protected peoples —not building new churches and monasteries, ringing bells softly, not resembling Muslims in dress and names, and the like— as the hardest thing for Islam and the Muslims, and tells the sultan to reflect well on the letter listing these conditions (pp. 436–437). The poem at the end of the letter is built on the sultan's title: if he exalts religion he will be, like his name, ʿIzz al-Dīn; if not, “Muzill al-Dīn (the one who abases religion)” (pp. 437–438). Ibn Arabī also relates that in one session he presented to the ruler of Aleppo, Ghāzī son of al-Malik al-Nāṣir Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn, a hundred and eighteen needs of people, all of which were met, and that he asked pardon for a man whose execution had been decided, saying “God knows I know of no sin in the world that could stand before my pardon … and you are the source of aspiration,” and the man was pardoned (p. 416).

Ibn Arabī's Memories and the Last Pages of the Sifr

In the last stretch of the sifr Ibn Arabī relates several more memories. One night in the year 592 in Seville, when they stayed at the house of Abū'l-Ḥusayn b. Abī ʿAmr b. al-Ṭufayl and the guests had stiffened and fallen silent out of respect, he recommended to the host his own book al-Irshād fī Kharq al-Adab al-Mukhtār, stretched his feet toward him and said, “Rub my feet a little”; everyone understood what he meant and relaxed (p. 415). In a memory he begins with “in my time of ignorance,” he says that while travelling with his father between Carmona and Palma a herd of wild asses he had resolved not to hunt did not raise their heads until he reached them, and that he understood the reason only on entering “this path, that is, the path of God”: “The resolve I held within not to harm the animals had passed into the animals” (pp. 417–418). Of himself he says, “I never punished anyone who needed to be taught manners while I was angry,” and describes how he acted when lending money (p. 417). He relates that in Ceuta, to a righteous man's advice “You must be gentle,” he answered, “As long as the capital is safe there is no problem,” meaning religion by the capital (p. 419). He also reports that his shaykh Abū Madyan sent word to Abū'l-Ḥasan b. al-Daqqāq, who had stopped attending his gatherings, “My devil is the adversary of your devil, but we are friends as before … Do not let our egos come between us!” (p. 398), and the verses his Qur'an-reading teacher Abū Bakr Muḥammad b. Khalaf al-Lakhmī used to recite in his mosque in Seville (p. 442).

The last pages of the sifr consist mostly of titled prophetic counsels and turn around the world, death and preparation: “Be in this world like a traveller or a guest, and count yourself among the dead” (p. 425); this world being a day of action without reckoning, “and tomorrow you will be in a day of reckoning without action” (p. 430); the three classes of the community according to their relation to wealth (p. 427); the angel of death standing at the door of every house five times a day (p. 430). These are followed by Shiblī's saying “If you want to look at the world with all its filth, look at the rubbish heap” (p. 431); the story in which Moses, caught between a dove that took refuge on his shoulder and the bird chasing it, resolves to cut flesh from his own thigh and the bird reveals itself as his Lord's messenger (pp. 431–432); the report of Junayd falling into ecstasy on Mount Sinai and a monk becoming Muslim through his answers (pp. 439–440); ʿĀʾisha enumerating the ten noble traits of character and saying “Their head is modesty” (p. 441); and the story of a wayfarer who speaks of the angel of provision bringing him bread every day (pp. 442–443). The sifr ends with two lines Ibn Arabī introduces with “We too said these verses”: “All people are, in truth, a word each / Then let that word be the finest word heard” (p. 444).

The End of the Book: Formulas of Seeking Refuge and Well-Known Prayers

Under the heading “End of the Chapter / End of the Book” Ibn Arabī compiles “Some Formulas of Seeking Refuge (with God) and Well-Known Prayers” (pp. 445–450). First come the prayers of everyday states: a moment of distress, entering and leaving the mosque, entering and leaving the privy, the end of a meal, sneezing, lying down in bed and waking from sleep, morning and evening, rising from a gathering. After the prayer for sleep Ibn Arabī sets sleep beside death: “Thus sleep is a small death. The place one passes to after death and the place one passes to in sleep are the same”; the level is the same, “and that is the level of the barzakh”; “Waking is like being raised on the Day of Resurrection,” and “rising from one's bed is one with rising from the graves” (pp. 445–446). He says he heard the prayer in the last verses of Sūrat al-Baqara (“Our Lord, do not take us to task if we forget or err…”) from the Prophet in a dream: he saw the dream in 599 in Mecca, between the Ajyād gate and the gate given as Ḥazwara (OCR “Haruze”); the reciter Muḥammad b. Khālid al-Ṣadafī al-Tilimsānī had recited this prayer after reading Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī to him. In the same dream he asks about a triple divorce pronounced in a single utterance; the Prophet says it counts as three, and of the scholars who count it as one he says, “They judged by the report that reached them, and they were right”; Ibn Arabī writes that he understood from this that the rulings of all mujtahids are to be affirmed: “Every mujtahid is right.” The one who objected in the dream with a raised voice is said to have been Iblīs. In another dream the same year he asks whether the word qurūʾ in the verse means menstruation or purity; the Prophet repeats his answer three times (pp. 446–447).

Then, saying “To return to our subject, the matter of prayer,” Ibn Arabī moves to a long prayer of seeking refuge (p. 448): forgiveness of errors and ignorance, the setting right of religion, this world and the next; refuge in God from the trial of the grave, from the Antichrist, from incapacity and laziness, from debt, from hunger and treachery, from the bad companion; and “O God! I seek refuge in You from You. I cannot count the praises due to You. You are as You have praised Yourself” (p. 448). The prayer widens into a request for mercy that includes his parents, family, relatives, neighbours, all Muslims who know him and who do not, and “those who think well of me and those who do not”; Ibn Arabī says, “O God! I have given my wealth, my honour and my blood in charity to Your servants. In these matters I claim no right from them, neither in this world nor in the next,” invokes blessings on the Prophet, and lines up the Qur'anic “Our Lord” prayers one after another —including those of Abraham, Zachariah, Job, Jonah and Noah (pp. 449–450). The last prayer of the book is this: “O God! Draw the reins of our hearts toward You, make us among those who rely on You in all their affairs … Make us among those who are guided and who are a means of guidance; do not make us among those who go astray and lead astray” (p. 450).

The Colophon: the Year 636 and Thirty-Seven Sifrs

The book ends with a colophon: “This chapter has come to an end, in the most concise and summary form, by the hand of its author, with the ending of the book. This copy is the second copy in my hands.” According to the record, the chapter that is the end of the book was completed in the year six hundred and thirty-six, on the twenty-fourth day of the month of Rabīʿ al-Awwal, early on Wednesday morning; Dost Muḥammad b. ʿAlī b. Muḥammad Ibn al-ʿArabī al-Ṭāʾī al-Ḥātimī wrote it in his own hand. The record says “This copy is thirty-seven volumes” and states that this copy contains some additions compared with the first; Ibn Arabī writes that he endowed the first copy to his son Muḥammad al-Kabīr (“His mother is Fāṭima bint Yūnus b. Yūsuf —who is the emir of the Two Sanctuaries—”), and adds that he also endowed it to his children and to “all believers in the east and the west, on land and at sea.” The record closes with blessings upon Muḥammad, his family and all his companions (p. 450).
