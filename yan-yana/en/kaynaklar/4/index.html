Sources, part 4/8 (G–K)

This section is not a reading summary. It is an attempt to read Dost's words side by side with the findings of physics that do not fit reason; it is our own reading frame. We do not say “the same” or “confirmed”: we only ask, and we say where they part ways. The text is our own rendering of a Turkish original.

  • Gorini 1976 — Gorini, Completely positive dynamical semigroups of N-level systems. Journal of Mathematical Physics 17, 821-825
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper in which a separate team reached the same result for N-level systems.

    Gorini, Kossakowski and Sudarshan examine the completely positive dynamical semigroups of N-level (finite-dimensional) quantum systems. The work was published in the same year as Lindblad's paper, independently of it.

  • Greenberger 1989 — Greenberger, Going Beyond Bell's Theorem. M. Kafatos (ed.), Bell's Theorem, Quantum Theory and Conceptions of the Universe, Kluwer, p. 69-72
    In brief, what does it say?

    Greenberger, Horne and Zeilinger showed that in a four-particle quantum state the assumptions of local reality contradict definite results ('Going Beyond Bell's Theorem').

    The paper's starting point is a difference between Bell's theorem and EPR's reality criterion. EPR counted cases in which we can predict the value of a quantity with certainty as 'elements of reality'. Bell's theorem, on the other hand, does not contain this case of certain prediction; it is a statistical inequality. The authors show that if a more complex model is used, locally determinate models will not work even in cases where certain prediction is possible.

  • Greiner 2002 — Greiner, Quantum phase transition from a superfluid to a Mott insulator in a gas of ultracold atoms. Nature 415, 39
    In brief, what does it say?

    At absolute zero temperature thermal fluctuations freeze; only quantum fluctuations remain. The question: can these microscopic fluctuations carry the whole of a many-particle system into another phase? In the experiment, a Bose-Einstein condensate made of repelling atoms was placed in a three-dimensional lattice built from light. As the depth of the lattice was increased, the system passed from a superfluid to a phase called a 'Mott insulator'. In the superfluid phase each atom is spread over the whole lattice and there is long-range phase coherence. In the insulator phase, by contrast, a whole number of atoms sits settled in each lattice site, there is no phase coherence across the lattice, and there is a gap in the excitation spectrum. The team was able to make the transition between these two phases in both directions. This is an experimental finding; what gives one pause is that a single atom being spread over the whole and its being settled in one site can be seen as two phases of the same system.

  • Grover 1996 — Grover, A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search. Proc. 28th Annual ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing (STOC '96), 212–219
    In brief, what does it say?

    A paper giving a quantum algorithm with O(√N) steps for search in an unsorted database.

    The author explains the problem with a telephone directory example: N names are arranged at random and one wants to find the number of a given person. For a fifty percent probability of success, a classical algorithm (deterministic or probabilistic) must check at least N/2 names.

  • Gurney 1928 — Gurney, Wave Mechanics and Radioactive Disintegration. Nature 122, 439
  • Hafele 1972 — Hafele, Around-the-World Atomic Clocks: Predicted Relativistic Time Gains. Science 177, 166
    In brief, what does it say?

    General relativity says that time runs slowly where gravity is strong, and special relativity says that a fast-moving clock falls behind. The question: can this knowledge be tested with real clocks on a laboratory scale? In October 1971 Hafele and Keating flew four cesium atomic clocks around the world on commercial airplanes, once eastward and once westward. This paper calculates from the flight paths what the theory predicts: the clocks going east should fall slightly behind the reference clocks on the ground, and those going west should go ahead. The predictions are on the nanosecond scale, that is, one billionth of a second. This paper is only the prediction; the observed results are given in a separate accompanying paper. What gives one pause is that time itself runs differently according to where and at what speed the clock is. This is not an interpretation but a measured fact.

  • Hafele 1972 — Hafele, Around-the-World Atomic Clocks: Observed Relativistic Time Gains. Science 177, 168
    In brief, what does it say?

    The theory of relativity says that a clock that moves and a clock that sits at height will run at a different rate from a clock on the ground. The question: can this be measured at airliner speeds and with real clocks? Hafele and Keating carried four cesium atomic clocks around the world on commercial flights in October 1971, once eastward and once westward, and compared them with the Naval Observatory's time scale. On the eastward trip the flown clocks lost about 59 nanoseconds (plus or minus 10), and on the westward trip they gained about 273 nanoseconds (plus or minus 7). This two-directional difference agreed well with the prediction of the theory of relativity. Here the measurement is the experiment; which part of which effect comes from speed and which from altitude is a theoretical calculation. What gives one pause is that it has been shown with tangible clocks that time is not a single flow that is the same for everyone.

  • Harrigan 2010 — Harrigan, Einstein, incompleteness, and the epistemic view of quantum states. Found. Phys. 40, 125
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: does the quantum state represent reality itself, or our knowledge about reality? The authors make this distinction precise and place hidden-variable models of quantum theory into a new classification. They show that in models where the quantum state is counted as 'a real thing', nonlocality can be demonstrated with an easier argument than Bell's theorem. This is a work of theory and history, not an experiment. They point out that the same argument is also found in Einstein's incompleteness claim going back to 1935. From this they conclude that Einstein was seeking a completion in which quantum states represent only information; this is their own claim, a historical interpretation. What makes the source valuable is that it describes the distinction 'is the state information or reality' explicitly.

  • Hawking 1974 — Hawking, Black hole explosions?. Nature 248, 30
    In brief, what does it say?

    Hawking reported that, by looking at the behavior of quantum fields around a black hole, he found that the black hole emits thermal radiation.

    The title of the paper ends with a question: 'Black hole explosions?'. It is a short Nature article and the first announcement of the result; the detailed calculation was published a year later in a separate paper.

  • Hawking 1976 — Hawking, Breakdown of predictability in gravitational collapse. Phys. Rev. D 14, 2460
    In brief, what does it say?

    Hawking argued that in the process of black hole evaporation a pure quantum state would turn into a mixed state and predictability would break down.

    The paper questions the consequence of the 1974 radiation calculation. If a black hole forms and evaporates completely, the detailed quantum state used at the start becomes unreadable from the final state.

  • Heisenberg 1927 — Heisenberg, Über den anschaulichen Inhalt der quantentheoretischen Kinematik und Mechanik. Z. Phys. 43, 172
    In brief, what does it say?

    In this 1927 paper Heisenberg derived for the first time the uncertainty relation, which states that paired quantities such as position and momentum cannot be determined at the same time with arbitrary precision. He explained it with a thought experiment: if we try to measure the position of a single electron with a gamma-ray microscope, the light strikes the electron and changes its momentum in an uncontrollable way. The more sharply we measure the position, the more the momentum is disturbed. Whether this limit is merely a defect of measuring instruments or the structure of nature itself has been put differently in later years depending on the interpretation; the relation itself is a firm part of the theory. We do not have the paper's own summary; the generally known framework is given here.

    This summary was written, because we could not reach the source's own abstract, only from the general knowledge we are sure of; it should be checked against the source.

  • Hensen 2015 — Hensen, Loophole-free Bell inequality violation using electron spins separated by 1.3 kilometres. Nature 526, 682
    In brief, what does it say?

    Einstein and his colleagues had asked how the measurement results of two distant particles can agree so well; Bell, in 1964, had calculated which limit an ordinary explanation called 'local hidden variables' cannot exceed. In this experiment a team in Delft entangles the electron spins of two defects in diamonds 1.3 kilometres apart and measures them. In all earlier experiments there were gaps ('loopholes') that allowed the result to be rescued by an ordinary explanation; here two of them are closed together. The result is a violation of the Bell bound: correlations that cannot be given by an ordinary, local calculation. This is an experimental result and it is firm. How it is to be interpreted (action at a distance, or giving up something about reality) is an open debate; the experiment says only 'there can be no local hidden variables', it does not say that there is communication.

  • Higgs 1964 — Higgs, Broken Symmetries and the Masses of Gauge Bosons. Phys. Rev. Lett. 13, 508
    In brief, what does it say?

    Higgs showed how gauge bosons can acquire mass through symmetry breaking.

    Gauge theories derive forces from a symmetry principle; but in these theories the gauge bosons come out massless. The weak force has a short range, so its carriers must be heavy. Another obstacle is the Goldstone theorem: when a symmetry is spontaneously broken, massless scalar particles were expected, and these were not observed. Higgs’s question is whether this obstacle can be overcome.

    This summary was written, because we could not reach the source's own abstract, only from the general knowledge we are sure of; it should be checked against the source.

  • Hong 1987 — Hong, Measurement of subpicosecond time intervals between two photons by interference. Phys. Rev. Lett. 59, 2044
    In brief, what does it say?

    What happens if two photons arrive at a half-silvered mirror from the two sides at the same time? By an ordinary calculation four possible outcomes are considered. According to quantum theory, however, if the photons are completely identical, the cases in which the two come out from separate exits cancel each other; the two always come out together from the same exit. Hong, Ou and Mandel measured this: when the small difference between the arrival times of the two photons is changed, the number of simultaneous signals at two separate detectors collapses when the photons coincide exactly. The width of this collapse gave how 'long' the photons are in time, about a hundred femtoseconds (on the order of one ten-quadrillionth of a second). This is an experimental result and it is very firm. What gives one pause is that two separate particles, when they cannot be told apart from each other, are obliged to 'behave together'.

  • Horodecki 1996 — Horodecki, Separability of mixed states: necessary and sufficient conditions. Physics Letters A 223, 1-8
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper that gives the necessary and sufficient conditions for telling whether mixed states are entangled (separability).

    The paper asks whether mixed quantum states are separable. A separable state is one that carries only classical correlation between the two parties. Non-entangled states are exactly these.

  • Horodecki 1998 — Horodecki, Mixed-State Entanglement and Distillation: Is there a "Bound" Entanglement in Nature?. Physical Review Letters 80, 5239-5242
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper that shows there are two separate kinds of entanglement: distillable (usable) and nondistillable ('bound') entanglement.

    The paper asks this question: can every entangled mixed state be converted into a pure pair (a singlet) that can be used in tasks such as teleportation? This conversion is called distillation; it is done with local operations and classical communication alone.

  • Horodecki 2009 — Horodecki, Quantum entanglement. Reviews of Modern Physics 81, 865-942
    In brief, what does it say?

    The Reviews of Modern Physics review that brings together the characterization, detection, distillation and quantification of entanglement.

    The review gives a compact account of the basic aspects of entanglement: characterization, detection, distillation and quantification. The authors discuss the various faces of entanglement through Bell inequalities, entropic inequalities, entanglement witnesses and quantum cryptography, and show the connections among them.

  • Itano 1990 — Itano, Quantum Zeno effect. Phys. Rev. A 41, 2295
    In brief, what does it say?

    If we 'look' at something very often, we fail to let it change: the quantum Zeno effect. Theory says that the transition of a system from one state to another can be prevented if it is measured frequently. Itano and colleagues held beryllium ions in a trap, cooled them with a laser, and while driving the transition between two energy levels with a radio wave, 'measured' it frequently with short light pulses. The more frequent the measurement, the more the transition was suppressed; the result agreed well with the calculations. This is an experiment. What gives one pause is that measurement is not only taking information but affects the course of the system; whether this is 'the collapse of the wave function' or something else is a matter of interpretation.

  • Jacques 2007 — Jacques, Experimental realization of Wheeler's delayed-choice gedanken experiment. Science 315, 966
    In brief, what does it say?

    In a setup that a photon enters through two paths, it can appear both as a wave (through both paths together, interference) and as a particle (which path it took is known); which one we will see depends on the choice of setup. John Wheeler had asked 'what if the choice is made after the photon has entered the setup?' Jacques and colleagues carried this out with single photons: the choice of open or closed configuration was made by a random number generator and took place at a moment so separated from the photon's entry that no communication at the speed of light was possible. The result agreed with the prediction of quantum theory: even a late choice determined the outcome. That is the experiment. What gives one pause is that the photon seems not to have decided 'what it is' before the entry; its meaning varies from interpretation to interpretation, and no one has to say 'we changed the past'.

  • Jaffe 2005 — Jaffe, Casimir effect and the quantum vacuum. Phys. Rev. D 72, 021301
    In brief, what does it say?

    It is said that even in what we call empty space, quantum fields have a lowest energy called 'zero-point energy.' In the Casimir effect, two metal plates very close together attract each other in a vacuum, and this attraction has been measured. It is often said: this attraction is proof that zero-point energy is real. Jaffe objects to this view. He shows that the Casimir force can also be calculated, without any appeal to zero-point energy, as a relativistic and quantum interaction of the charges and currents in the plates. Moreover, the known result appears not to depend on the fine-structure constant, the measure of the strength of the interaction, but in fact if that constant goes to zero the force goes to zero as well. This is a theoretical dispute; the measured force is not disputed, what is disputed is what it 'proves.' Why it gives one pause: a famous experiment is not condemned to a single interpretation, as it is often told.

  • Jarzynski 1997 — Jarzynski, Nonequilibrium Equality for Free Energy Differences. Phys. Rev. Lett. 78, 2690
    In brief, what does it say?

    Jarzynski derived an exact equality that gives the equilibrium free-energy difference from the distribution of work done in a rapidly driven system.

    The free-energy difference gives the least work needed to take a system slowly (in equilibrium) between two configurations. Real experiments, however, are usually done quickly, and each repetition gives a different work result: work is a random variable.

  • Johansson 2012 — Johansson, QuTiP: An open-source Python framework for the dynamics of open quantum systems. Comput. Phys. Commun. 183, 1760
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was: is it possible to calculate, with a tool anyone can use, how quantum systems interacting with their environment (open systems) change over time? The authors presented a framework called QuTiP, written in Python, object-oriented and open source. The user defines states and operators with a quantum object class; they can set up any system they want, including time-dependent Hamiltonians, and give it to different equation-of-motion solvers. The paper describes the structure of the tool and examples of use. The four small calculations on the site's "Compute and See" page were made with this framework. Since it is a software paper, it does not report a physical finding; it only documents how the calculation is done.

  • Joos 1985 — Joos, The emergence of classical properties through interaction with the environment. Z. Phys. B 59, 223
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was this: if according to quantum theory the smallest things can carry two states at once, why does a speck of dust on a table, or a cat, never look like that? Joos and Zeh calculated that a body inevitably 'talks' with its surroundings, that is, with light, air and temperature. Every photon or molecule that comes from the surroundings and strikes the body carries away a trace of where the body is. Because of this trace, the interference between the body's states of being 'here and there together' becomes invisible in a very short time. This is called decoherence: the state in which two possibilities enter the calculation together is dispersed into the environment and lost. This is a theoretical calculation and was later supported by experiments. But why a single result is realized, that is, why the measurement gives 'only one', this calculation does not explain by itself; this is still a debate among interpretations.

  • Jönsson 1961 — Jönsson, Elektroneninterferenzen an mehreren künstlich hergestellten Feinspalten. Z. Phys. 161, 454
    In brief, what does it say?

    Electrons are thought of as particles; but do they also behave like waves, that is, when they pass through two narrow slits do they give an interference pattern like water waves? In 1961 Claus Jönsson sent a beam of electrons through very thin, artificially made slits and saw light and dark stripes characteristic of waves on the screen opposite. Electron diffraction had been shown earlier with crystals; here, the very slit arrangement known from light was built with electrons. This is an experiment; the interpretation comes later. What gives one pause is this: each electron arrives at the screen as a single point, but when many electrons accumulate, the wave pattern emerges. What it means for this pattern to count as 'passing through both slits together' is still a disputed matter of interpretation. (For detailed numbers, the article should be consulted.)

  • Kapitza 1938 — Kapitza, Viscosity of Liquid Helium below the λ-Point. Nature 141, 74
    In brief, what does it say?

    Kapitza measured the viscosity of liquid helium below the λ-point and reported that he found it very small; the name ‘superfluidity’ for the phenomenon is associated with this note.

    Helium-4 passes, below about 2.17 K (the λ-point), into a separate state called liquid helium II. Below this temperature it was known to behave like a liquid that conducts heat unusually well and seeps from the walls of vessels through thin cracks. Kapitza’s question was simple: what, in fact, is the viscosity of this liquid, that is, its internal resistance to flow?

  • Kaufman 2016 — Kaufman, Quantum thermalization through entanglement in an isolated many-body system. Science 353, 794
    In brief, what does it say?

    That a hot cup of tea comes to the temperature of its surroundings over time is something we know, but how can a perfectly isolated quantum system, if it starts in a completely definite (pure) state, behave as if 'heated'? Kaufman and colleagues examined, with a quantum microscope, six rubidium atoms placed in the sites of a lattice set up with light. When they suddenly switched on tunneling between the atoms, the whole of the six atoms stayed in a pure state; but when small parts of two or three atoms were looked at, those behaved in accordance with a thermal distribution. The source of the effect is entanglement: the parts are tied to each other in such a way that, even if the whole is definite, a part by itself looks uncertain. This is an experimental result. Why it gives one pause: the parts of a fully known whole look random. How we shall tie this to the foundation of statistical physics is separately debated.
