Sources, part 2/8 (B–D)

This section is not a reading summary. It is an attempt to read Dost's words side by side with the findings of physics that do not fit reason; it is our own reading frame. We do not say “the same” or “confirmed”: we only ask, and we say where they part ways. The text is our own rendering of a Turkish original.

  • Binnig 1982 — Binnig, Tunneling through a controllable vacuum gap. Appl. Phys. Lett. 40, 178
    In brief, what does it say?

    In quantum physics a particle can pass to the other side of a barrier that seems impossible to cross; this is called tunneling. Binnig and colleagues left a gap between two metal surfaces, measured the current between them, and were able to vary the gap in a controlled way. In earlier attempts an insulating layer was placed in between; here for the first time tunneling was seen across an adjustable 'gap.' The finding: the resistance to the current increases exponentially as the gap widens; that is, when the gap opens a little, the current falls quickly. This is an experiment; it is the observation, in a gap, of a behavior expected beforehand on theoretical grounds. This setup later became the basic foundation of the scanning tunneling microscope, which made it possible to see individual atoms on surfaces. Why it gives one pause: one can pass, with a measurable probability, beyond a barrier that cannot be crossed by classical thinking.

  • Bohigas 1984 — Bohigas, Characterization of Chaotic Quantum Spectra and Universality of Level Fluctuation Laws. Physical Review Letters 52, 1-4
    In brief, what does it say?

    The paper that suggests that the level fluctuations of chaotic quantum spectra are universal.

    Bohigas, Giannoni and Schmit examine the statistics of the quantum energy levels of a classically chaotic system. The levels of a quantum system are like points on a ruler; the real question is how the spacings between neighboring levels are distributed.

  • Bohm 1952 — Bohm, A suggested interpretation of the quantum theory in terms of hidden variables. I. Phys. Rev. 85, 166
    In brief, what does it say?

    In the usual quantum account the state of a particle is specified only by a wave function and only probability is given for measurement results. Bohm asked: is this account 'complete', or are there 'hidden variables' that we cannot see for now? In the picture he proposed every particle always has a definite position; this position follows an influence in the form of a 'guiding wave' directed by the wave function. The paper shows that this gives exactly all the predictions made by the usual theory; that is, this is not an experiment but a proposal for an interpretation, and it cannot be told apart by the same experiments. This picture allows a continuous and definite description, but it has a price: a structure that binds distant particles instantly, that is, a nonlocal one, is needed. In conclusion the paper gives one pause in that it shows that quantum theory does not have a single reading. Which interpretation is right is an open debate even today.

  • Bohr 1913 — Bohr, On the constitution of atoms and molecules. Phil. Mag. 26, 1
    In brief, what does it say?

    Bohr treats the hydrogen atom as a system with definite energy levels and proposes that the emitted light is determined by the differences between these levels.

    According to Rutherford's model of the atom, an atom consists of a small nucleus and electrons orbiting around it. Classical electrodynamics requires that an orbiting charge radiate continuously, lose energy and collapse into the nucleus. Yet atoms are stable, and the light emitted by gases comes in sharp lines at definite frequencies.

  • Bohr 1928 — Bohr, The Quantum Postulate and the Recent Development of Atomic Theory. Nature 121, 580-590
    In brief, what does it say?

    Bohr first explained the quantum postulate and the idea of complementarity in his 1927 lecture at Como, Italy (Nature 121, 580).

    This piece is the printed version of the lecture Bohr gave at a congress in Como in September 1927. Bohr's question is how we are to use the concepts of classical physics (position, momentum, energy, wave, particle) when describing events at the atomic level.

  • Bohr 1935 — Bohr, Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality be Considered Complete?. Phys. Rev. 48, 696-702
    In brief, what does it say?

    Bohr replied, under the same title, to the claim of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen that the quantum description is incomplete (Phys. Rev. 48, 696).

    Bohr's reply was published in the same journal and under the same title shortly after the EPR paper appeared. EPR's question was this: if, without disturbing a system in any way, we can predict with certainty the value of a physical quantity by measurement, then there is a reality corresponding to that quantity. If this criterion is accepted, a link set up between two particles that have moved apart shows the quantum description to be incomplete.

  • Bohr 2007 — Bohr, Niels Bohr. "Anyone who is not shocked by quantum theory has not understood it." Barad 2007, p. 254 reports it; the exact source is uncertain and variants exist
    In brief, what does it say?

    This is not a paper but a saying attributed to Niels Bohr: whoever is not shocked by quantum theory has not understood it. The saying is reported by Barad (2007); its source is a secondhand report coming via Wikiquote, and its exact source is not fixed; there are different ways of putting it. For this reason it is not verified that the attribution belongs to Bohr; we mention it not as Bohr's definite word but as a saying attributed to him. The saying is neither an experiment nor a theory; it summarizes a stance often voiced by those who work on quantum physics.

  • Born 1926 — Born, Zur Quantenmechanik der Stoßvorgänge. Z. Phys. 37, 863
    In brief, what does it say?

    Born treated the problem of a particle scattering off an atom with wave mechanics and proposed that the square of the wave function gives a probability (Z. Phys. 37, 863).

    Born's paper is about scattering, that is, a beam of particles striking an atom and being dispersed in various directions. Until then Schrödinger's wave mechanics had been used for stationary states inside the atom; Born asked whether the same equation could also describe particles flying freely and striking an obstacle.

  • Bose 1924 — Bose, Plancks Gesetz und Lichtquantenhypothese. Zeitschrift für Physik 26, 178-181
    In brief, what does it say?

    Bose derived Planck's radiation law by counting photon states, without appealing to classical electrodynamics.

    In 1900 Planck had proposed a law for the spectrum of thermal radiation that fitted experiment, but the derivations up to that time also appealed in part to classical electrodynamics. Bose's question was: can we obtain the law with an argument that only counts light quanta?

  • Bousso 2002 — Bousso, The holographic principle. Rev. Mod. Phys. 74, 825
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question: how much information fits into a given region of space? Physicists studying black holes arrived at something surprising: the information a region can carry is limited not by its volume but by the area of the surface that encloses it. According to the paper, this limit is about 1.4 times 10 to the power of 69 bits per square meter. The author describes how this limit was found, the role of the quantum properties of black holes here, and to which regions it can be applied by means of a construction called 'light sheets'; he says the limit has been tested in many examples. This is a theoretical review, not a direct measurement. The side the author states plainly: the reason for this relation between geometry and information has not yet been explained. The idea called the 'holographic principle' suggests that its root lies in the degrees of freedom of a unified theory of spacetime and matter, but this is a proposal. What gives one pause is that it is the surface, not the volume, that decides.

  • Bousso 2011 — Bousso, The multiverse interpretation of quantum mechanics. Phys. Rev. D 85, 045007
    In brief, what does it say?

    Bousso and his colleague argue that two separate 'many' ideas, the many worlds of quantum mechanics and the multiverse of cosmology, may in fact be the same thing. Their starting point is that decoherence, the modern counterpart of the 'collapse' of the wave function after measurement, depends on which degrees of freedom we count as the 'environment'. The authors propose that, taking as the basis the region a measurement event can causally reach (the causal diamond), the boundary of this region determines the environment in a natural way. They argue that to fully verify the probabilities of quantum predictions, it must be possible to perform an infinite number of identical experiments and every outcome must be definite. In diamonds with a finite surface, holographic entropy bounds make this impossible; so such a full verification could be possible only in certain regions of infinite extent ('hats'). This is a proposal, not a measured finding. The notable side is that it forces an everyday question such as the definiteness of a measurement to be thought about in the same frame as the geometry of the universe.

  • Bouwmeester 1997 — Bouwmeester, Experimental quantum teleportation. Nature 390, 575
    In brief, what does it say?

    Is it possible to 'send' a quantum state somewhere else? Bouwmeester and colleagues re-created the polarization state of a photon in another photon without physically carrying that photon (quantum teleportation). For this they put two photons into an entangled state; the sender side made a special joint measurement and communicated the result through a classical channel; at the receiver the state was re-formed according to this information. The original state is destroyed in this process. This is an experiment. What gives one pause is that the whole information of the state reappears elsewhere through communication no faster than the speed of light. It is important why this does not mean 'matter is being teleported', that is, that only the state is transferred.

  • Brunner 2014 — Brunner, Bell nonlocality. Rev. Mod. Phys. 86, 419
    In brief, what does it say?

    What Bell showed in 1964 was that the results of quantum theory cannot be explained by any local theory: the relation between the measurement results of two distant particles can be stronger than the relation that could be given by an order that determines each one's result in advance and is not affected from afar. This paper does not report a new experiment; it is a review. It tells how, over the last twenty years, especially thanks to quantum information science, this subject has become a field in its own right: how the relation is defined, how it is measured, with what tools it is studied. It notes that this 'non-locality' also has concrete uses in information processing. What is left to the reader: the part settled by experiment (that the inequalities are exceeded) and the interpretive part (what this means) must be kept apart. The review lists the mathematics of the theory and the various interpretive options.

  • Budroni 2022 — Budroni, Kochen-Specker contextuality. Rev. Mod. Phys. 94, 045007
    In brief, what does it say?

    The question was: is quantum theory compatible with classical models that say the outcome of a measurement is independent of the other measurements that can be made together with it? The Kochen–Specker theorem gives the answer: it is not. If you try to assign, for every measurement of a system, a definite and context-independent value before the measurement, you fall into contradiction for some sets of measurements. This review article (a survey published in a peer-reviewed journal) gathers the theorem, its various forms, its experimental tests and its quantum information applications (for example, using contextuality as a resource in quantum computing). What gives one pause here is that the result does not say the measured property is not definite on its own before measurement; it says only that assigning a context-independent value is not possible; which of the two to give up depends on interpretation.

  • Busch 2014 — Busch, Colloquium: Quantum root-mean-square error and measurement uncertainty relations. Rev. Mod. Phys. 86, 1261
    In brief, what does it say?

    The Heisenberg uncertainty principle is often explained as 'when we measure something, we touch it and disturb it.' This paper shows that this account confuses two separate things: the error of a measurement and the disturbance a measurement causes to the system. The authors examine two separate ways of measuring 'error.' In one, two quantities are measured together and the difference between them is looked at; in the other, the distributions that come out of two separate rounds of measurement are compared. The result calms a debate that has gone on for years: Heisenberg's first statement does not hold in the way we assumed in some cases, but the idea that 'there is a price between information and disturbance' survives in another, more careful form. So this is a work of theory and definition; it is not an experimental result. What gives one pause is that even the most repeated sentence of quantum physics turns into a matter of definition when read carefully.

  • Casimir 1948 — Casimir, On the attraction between two perfectly conducting plates. Proc. K. Ned. Akad. Wet. B51, 793
    In brief, what does it say?

    Casimir calculated the attractive force between two perfectly conducting parallel plates from the zero-point energy of the vacuum.

    Casimir asks how two uncharged, perfectly conducting plates, parallel to each other, behave in empty space. In classical electrodynamics no force is expected between them. Casimir, however, counts the allowed modes of the electromagnetic field between the plates: the field’s boundary conditions at the conducting surfaces allow only certain wavelengths to fit between the plates. Outside there is no such restriction.

  • Chatrchyan 2012 — Chatrchyan, Observation of a new boson at a mass of 125 GeV with the CMS experiment at the LHC. Phys. Lett. B 716, 30 (CMS Collaboration)
    In brief, what does it say?

    The CMS collaboration, independently and in the same period, reported a new boson around 125 GeV.

    CMS, the second experiment asking the same question as ATLAS, works with the Compact Muon Solenoid detector at the LHC. Its data are about 5.1 fb⁻¹ at 7 TeV and about 5.3 fb⁻¹ at 8 TeV.

  • Chou 2010 — Chou, Optical Clocks and Relativity. Science 329, 1630
    In brief, what does it say?

    The theory of relativity says that the rate of clocks changes with motion and with gravity. For a long time one had to look for this at speeds near the speed of light or on satellites. Chou and colleagues, using extremely precise optical atomic clocks, showed that these effects can be measured on the ground, at everyday scales as well: a clock moving at a few meters per second runs slower, and a clock raised by about one meter also runs at a different rate. This is an experiment and it has turned out consistent with the predictions of relativity. The paper itself rests on measurement rather than interpretation. What gives one pause is that our intuition that 'time flows the same for everyone' is disturbed in a measurable way even by a height difference of one meter. The details we use here are only those in the publication abstract.

  • Ciampini 2018 — Ciampini, Experimental signature of quantum Darwinism in photonic cluster states. Physical Review A 98, 020101
    In brief, what does it say?

    The experiment that first measured the traces of Quantum Darwinism in a small 'environment' model built with photons.

    The previous two works were theory; this paper is an experiment. The team sets up in the laboratory a system in which the 'environment' consists of a few photon pieces. The source they use is a hyperentangled source that entangles the polarization and the path information of photons at the same time; the entangled photons form a 'graph state' (cluster state), that is, a multipartite state connected like a network.

  • Clauser 1969 — Clauser, Proposed experiment to test local hidden-variable theories. Phys. Rev. Lett. 23, 880
    In brief, what does it say?

    Einstein and his colleagues thought that quantum theory might be incomplete, that particles carry definite properties even before measurement, and that distant measurements do not influence each other instantly (the idea of local hidden variables). In 1964 Bell had shown that this view has a measurable limit. In this paper Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt put the same idea into a form that can be carried out in a real laboratory: two photons, in separate places, are passed through filters set to different directions, and the agreement of the results is counted. Any theory with local hidden variables has an upper bound for this agreement count; quantum mechanics, on the other hand, predicts a number that exceeds this bound. The paper is an experimental proposal and reports no result; the idea is that whichever one nature chooses can be seen by experiment. What gives one pause is that this question stops being philosophy and becomes measurable. The experiments later mostly supported the quantum side; what this means is still open to interpretation.

  • Crooks 1999 — Crooks, Entropy production fluctuation theorem and the nonequilibrium work relation for free energy differences. Phys. Rev. E 60, 2721
    In brief, what does it say?

    Crooks derived a more general fluctuation theorem and briefly proved the Jarzynski equality as a consequence of it.

    Few exact relations are known for nonequilibrium processes. One is the fluctuation theorem, which places a condition on the distribution of entropy production; the other is Jarzynski's work equality. This paper brings the two together under the same roof.

  • Davisson 1927 — Davisson, Diffraction of electrons by a crystal of nickel. Phys. Rev. 30, 705
    In brief, what does it say?

    Davisson and Germer scatter an electron beam from a nickel crystal and observe that the electrons are concentrated at certain angles.

    In the early 1920s de Broglie had suggested that a wavelength might exist for the particles of matter as well. Davisson and Germer's work is one of the first examples of an experimental test of this proposal. The aim of the work is to measure whether, when an electron beam is scattered from a crystal surface, the electrons come back in all directions or only in certain directions.

  • de Broglie 1927 — de Broglie, La mécanique ondulatoire et la structure atomique de la matière et du rayonnement. J. Phys. Radium 8, 225-241
    In brief, what does it say?

    De Broglie proposed a picture in which the particle is guided by a wave; he also presented the same idea at the 1927 Solvay Congress (J. Phys. Radium 8, 225).

    De Broglie's idea of matter waves, proposed in 1924, had led to Schrödinger's wave equation. The 1927 paper takes up the relation of 'wave mechanics to atomic structure and radiation' and proposes a picture that holds wave and particle together.

  • Degen 2017 — Degen, Quantum sensing. Reviews of Modern Physics 89, 035002
    In brief, what does it say?

    A Reviews of Modern Physics review that introduces the basic principles, methods and concepts of quantum sensing from an experimentalist's point of view.

    The review defines quantum sensing as the use of a quantum system, of quantum properties or of quantum phenomena to measure a physical quantity. Historical examples mentioned include superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs), magnetometers based on atomic vapors, and atomic clocks.

  • Deutsch 1985 — Deutsch, Quantum theory, the Church–Turing principle and the universal quantum computer. Proceedings of the Royal Society A 400, 97–117
    In brief, what does it say?

    A paper that formulates the Church-Turing hypothesis as a physical principle and introduces the notions of the quantum Turing machine and the universal quantum computer.

    The paper's starting point is this: the Church-Turing hypothesis says that everything that is "computable" can be computed by a Turing machine. Deutsch argues that there is a tacit physical claim underlying this hypothesis, and writes it out explicitly as a principle: "every finitely realizable physical system can be perfectly simulated by a universal model computing machine operating by finite means."

  • Dieks 1982 — Dieks, Communication by EPR devices. Phys. Lett. A 92, 271
    In brief, what does it say?

    Dieks's short paper starts from this question: can entangled particle pairs serve faster-than-light communication? A proposal in this direction hoped to read out by duplicating the state at one end of the pair. Dieks showed that a perfect copy of an unknown state is incompatible with the rules of quantum mechanics; so the proposal does not work. The result coincides with the result Wootters and Zurek published in the same year. This is one of the reasons entanglement cannot be turned into faster-than-light communication. We do not have the paper's own summary; the generally known framework is given here.
