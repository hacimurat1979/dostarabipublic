A Continuous Descent Toward the Center: The Circle, the Highest of Forms

pp. 274–314

Site reading summary

Four chapters move from the purpose of the world's creation (the same apple tasting differently with every bite) through a cosmogony running from the First Intellect down to the elements, arriving at a teaching, in Dost's own words, that 'the circle is the highest of forms' and that 'the entire world is in continuous descent toward the center, and this descent is nothing other than seeking the Real'; the close treats the exchange of glory and abasement's garments on the Day of Resurrection and a threefold classification of human beings.

Different Tastes from the Same Apple: The Purpose of the World's Creation

Chapter 294 (spoken from the Mosaic rank, yet bearing the way-station name "the Meccan Muhammadan Way-Station") opens with this question: "does the animal share with the human being in this creation, or is this creation particular to the human being?" (p. 275). The chapter treats the purpose of the world's creation as proof leading to knowledge of the Real; the author offers an analogy in his own words: "A person eating a single apple finds a different taste with every bite. Upon finishing the apple, the taste found in the final bite is yet another. The apple is the same apple" (p. 278) — an image likening the endless divine relations to a single reality tasted differently at different moments. The chapter contains a couplet: "Things are distinguished by their opposites / Nonexistence distinguishes existence, existence distinguishes nonexistence" (p. 279).

The Way-station's Questions, and the Human Being in the Qur'an

In Chapter 294 Dost says the way-station contains knowledge of the world's rank with God, and lists its questions: does non-existence have a rank deserving reverence for its own sake; do those created from the wretched and the angered have a revered rank with God; what is the reason for God's exalting the world; does the created world have an attribute of loftiness by which it is known (pp. 274-275). The way-station also contains knowledge of the creation of the human being: does the animal share in this creation; why is the word "human" in the Qur'an mostly mentioned with blame, weakness, or deficiency? Examples of blame are "Surely man is in loss" and "Surely man is ungrateful to his Lord"; of weakness, "We created man from an extract of clay" and "We created man in hardship"; and of praise followed by blame, "We created man in the best of forms" followed by "then We reduced him to the lowest of the low" (p. 275).

The Purpose of the World's Creation, Limitless Relations, and the Increase of Knowledge

On the world's place with God Dost says: "God did not create the world out of need of it. God created the world as a proof for knowing Him." Thereby what was lacking in the rank of existence and knowledge is completed; God gained from creating the world no attribute of perfection He lacked before (p. 275). Since the relations between God and the world are limitless, the creation of the possible things is also endless; creation is continuous in this world and the next, gnosis comes about continuously, and for this reason the Prophet was commanded to ask for an increase of knowledge. This increase is not about beings but "an increase of his knowledge of God according to what He brings into being in the world"; every new thing created in the world yields knowledge of a divine relation that appears (p. 276). Answering the objection that the division of reason limits the genera, he says the eye would divide the visible and the ear the audible by their own power; "When any faculty that has some capacity makes a division, what lies outside its power also lies outside that division" (p. 277).

The Ambiguous Verses, the Humbled Faces, and the Divine Oaths

After the verses "They encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills" and "They cannot encompass Him in knowledge," Dost explains the verse "Faces will be humbled (before God)": faces are the essences of beings and the realities of existents, for "the face of everything means its essence"; whatever God creates in the world He creates in its perfection, and this perfection is its face (p. 278). Had the ambiguous verses not been sent down, it would not be known that something ambiguous exists in God's knowledge; God has made clear to us that there is something known and something only God can know (pp. 278-279). In the divine oaths, God sometimes swears by His own name, saying "By God," and sometimes by creatures, as in "By your Lord," "By the scattering winds," "By the star," "By the sun"; in Dost's view the creatures are outwardly put in the place of names, and what is hidden is the names (p. 279). He explains "By the heaven and the One who built it" as "by the builder of heaven," and says that in "By the Lord of heaven and earth" the letter "and" indicates partnership (pp. 281-282).

The Excellence of Non-existence, and Transcendence

Dost explains the excellence of non-existence: the worth of pure non-existence is "only that it is a proof of existence"; limited non-existence is of a nature able to receive existence and in this respect is superior to absolute non-existence; absolute non-existence remains as it is, out of jealousy for the divine rank, rather than share with itself the attribute of existence or take a name given to God (pp. 279-280). The Real has appointed glorification (tasbih) for existents, which is transcendence (tanzih); transcendence is an attribute of non-existence, and by describing Himself with it the Real honored absolute non-existence: "Your Lord, the Lord of might, is exalted above what they describe." In the state of non-existence the attribute of pre-eternity belongs to it, and "none among things other than God knows God better than absolute non-existence" (p. 280). On the verse "We created you when you were nothing before," he finds that the human being must remain, in existence too, without objecting and submitting to the flow of destiny, and that his honor lies in returning in his existence to his non-existence (p. 280).

Reverence for the Signs, Punishment, and Mercy Preceding Wrath

On the verse "Whoever honors the signs of God, that is from the piety of hearts," Dost says the signs are marks and proofs, and that the verse speaks of the piety of "hearts," not of "souls" or "spirits," because the human being passes from state to state at every breath; to notice this change is arduous, and only the strong perfected ones attain this state (p. 281). Some knowers, regarding reverence for these states as lawful, included everything other than God in felicity; one who looks this way sees no one in punishment. Dost says God has announced that both abodes will be filled and that they will stay there eternally, but that there is no explicit text that punishment, meaning pain, will be perpetual; he recalls Abu Yazid's words, "except the pleasure I found in torment" (pp. 282-283). He explains mercy's preceding wrath through a horse race: "So the one who breasts the rope is the one who has passed"; "Mercy has preceded wrath in God's creatures. So mercy is ahead in both worlds by God's generosity"; the saying "My mercy has preceded My wrath" is true, and so is the verse "I will fill Hell with jinn and humans" (pp. 283-284).

Cosmogony: From the First Intellect to the Elements

Chapter 295 (Muhammadan, the Station of the Rank of the Sublime Numbers) offers a cosmogonic schema: an ordered descent from the First Intellect to the Soul, the substance of Dust (haba'), body/the Throne, the Footstool, the Sphere of the Zodiac (constellations), the Sphere of the Fixed Stars (mansions), the four heavens, and finally the elements (fire-air-water-earth). The Cloud (Amâ) is here technically situated as a cosmological "place": "the rank of what lies above it, and the Cloud below it — the Cloud being that above and below which there is no air — belongs to the name the Lord" (p. 289). The source of starlight, too, is made concrete through an everyday example: "there are sparks in flint, and it ignites, and from it a lamp comes into being... He made the sun a lamp" (pp. 293-294).

The Index of the Way-station's Sciences and the Story of the Serpent

In Chapter 295 Dost gives the index of the way-station's sciences: the science of flashes (lawa'ih), the entry of the feminine into numbers, knowledge of why the Manichaeans went astray, the heir's pardoning of blood in a blood-feud and the right of the slain, the science of reason, the science of consideration, where the astrologers take their knowledge from and its superiority over other sciences, the difference between a saint's miracle and a deceptive gift, and the reason for loving human beings and animals other than God (pp. 285-286). He relates the story of the animal connected to the science of stars: of a serpent, neither small nor large, found at Silves in the west of al-Andalus, eating its upper part gives the science of the stars, its middle the science of plants, and the part after the tail the science of the waters hidden under the earth; Abdallah b. Abdun, the Emir's secretary, cut the serpent with a single blow into three equal parts, and he and his brothers ate them (p. 286). Love has two causes, relation and beneficence; relation is nearer when it is directed to God, since the human being was created in the divine form (p. 286).

The Cherubim, the First Intellect, the Soul, Dust, and Nature

Dost describes the cherubic angels, who have been carried out of themselves in God's awe and have no knowledge of anything other than God; one of them is assigned to the First Intellect and, among humans, to the Singular (Afrad), and the Pole's help comes from the First Intellect (p. 287). The First Intellect is the first existent in the world of writing and recording and was created without precedent; from it, with no interval of time, the Soul issues by derivation: "The Soul is the Preserved Tablet, in which everything that will occur in this world until the Resurrection is written"; from the Soul came forth dust (haba'), the dark substance, and God brought nature into being as an intelligible rank between the Soul and dust (p. 287). Everything God creates by means of a particular cause comes to be through a manifestation special to that being, and the cause does not know it; the verse "I will breathe into it" and "it becomes a bird by God's leave" are cited in this connection (pp. 287-288).

The Throne, the Footstool, the Atlas Sphere, and the Twenty-eight Mansions

Dost says the first form to appear in body is the circle, explains the alif's containing all letters as it passes through the points of articulation, and says the first form after circularity is the triangle and that forms go on without end (p. 288). God holds the body in dust; nature is for the Soul like the craftsman's tool. He named the body the Throne, made it the source of mercy, and settled upon it with the name the All-Merciful; for as long as the world lasts He created four bearers in act and four in potency, which at the Resurrection together make eight (pp. 288-289). The Footstool is not a form within the Throne but another form in dust, and the two feet hang down to it from the Throne; limited and absolute mercy are divided, the single word of the Throne is split into report and command, command into the obligatory, recommended, and permitted, and prohibition into the forbidden and disliked (p. 289). In the circular body below the Footstool, with no stars, the twelve measures known as the zodiacal signs are determined; the sphere in the middle of the Atlas sphere is the sphere of the fixed stars, the place of the twenty-eight mansions, which Dost lists, from al-Nath, al-Batin, al-Thurayya, and al-Dabaran to al-Risha (p. 290). The astronomers' knowledge ends at the Atlas sphere, and in truth at the sphere of the stars; Dost records, "Today we are in the year six hundred and thirty-four" (p. 291).

The Elements, the Stars, the Engendered Glorifying God, and Idris

After saying that earth is the end of the void and the last point of density and darkness, and that the world's motion arises from love and affection, Dost describes the element water: "Water is the first existent among the elements"; the earth thickens from it, the part that warms is air, and the part that warms from that is fire (the ether sphere), and "for us the root of the elements is water"; on this the philosophers advanced six views, five mistaken and one in agreement with the people of unveiling (pp. 292-293). The vapor rising from the water sought the greatest sphere, was blocked by the sphere of the stars, turned back and condensed, and God split it into the seven heavens; sparks flying from the ether sphere produced the stars, and the verse "He made the sun a lamp" is cited (p. 293). Night is the darkness of the earth that blocks the sun's light from spreading; the moon is by origin without light, and God extinguished its light (p. 294). In each sphere a world of the same genus as the sphere's nature has been arranged, named angels; everything — dry, moist, hot, cold, inanimate, plant, animal — glorifies God in its own language (p. 294). The first to teach people the science of the stars, having received it from God, was Idris (p. 295). The chapter closes with the stars being "subjected by His command" and the story of the donkey reported from al-Qushayri: to a man who struck it, the donkey said, "Leave him! For he is striking his own head" (pp. 295-296).

The Circle, the Highest of Forms: A Continuous Descent Toward the Center

In Chapter 295, Dost first declares the geometric supremacy of the circle: "the first form to appear in body is the circle, and it is the highest of forms. The circle, among forms, is like the letter alif among letters, and it contains all forms within it" (p. 288); he then adds that a body beneath the Footstool is likewise circular: "the Real created, in rank below the Footstool, another circular body, and brought it into being with the circularity of a heavenly sphere, without a star" (p. 290). He then explains the world's motion through the concept of the center: "the entire world is in continuous descent toward the center. This seeking is a seeking of gnosis, and the center is that in which its own affair will settle... its descent in search of the center, then, is continuous. This state is what is expressed as seeking the Real. The Real, then, is what it seeks" (p. 292).

The Degrees of Paradise, the Depths of Hell, and Fitting Recompense

In Chapter 296 Dost says the degrees of Paradise equal the depths of Hell: if a person fulfills a command, he obtains in Paradise a degree specifically fixed for that deed, and if he does not, there is a corresponding depth in Hell; "If a stone were thrown from that degree in Paradise, it would fall on the line of level of that depth in the Fire." He cites in this connection the verse "He looked and saw him in the midst of Hell," the example of the two men in the Sura of the Cave, and the verse in the Sura of the Ranks, "One of them said, 'I had a companion' " (p. 297). In a sound sacred hadith it is reported that on the Day of Resurrection God reminds His servant of the five pillars of Islam, and that one who believes in some and denies some joins the unbelievers; the same measure applies to all commands and prohibitions of the law, to speech, to creed, and to leaving deeds (p. 298). In the figure are given the forms of the degrees in Paradise, the depths in Hell, and the Heights — the wall whose inside is mercy and whose outside is punishment (pp. 298-299).

Dost shows from verses that divine recompenses are of the same kind: "They plotted, and God plotted," "God mocks them," "Today the believers laugh at the unbelievers," "They forgot God, so He forgot them"; for this reason the recompense is called "fitting recompense" (pp. 299-300). It is reported that the arrogant will be raised like particles and trampled underfoot by people. When the polytheist asks the recompense for his good character and deeds, God says to him, "When you did these deeds, I repaid you with such and such favors I bestowed on you," and the polytheist confirms this; Dost calls this matter "the question of inheritance between the people of Paradise and the people of Hell" and says he will treat it in the sections on Paradise and Hell (p. 300).

Manifestation in the Act, and Isma'il b. Sawdakin's Reminder

Among the sciences of the way-station Dost counts knowing the Real's asking His blessed servants about the ranks of the wretched, and the two kinds of knowledge — what is known only through taste and what is grasped by the power of thought (p. 301). He relates a matter to which Shams al-Din Isma'il b. Sawdakin al-Tawri drew his attention: is manifestation in the act possible? In his view "obligation to do something does not come from a knower and wise one who says 'do' and 'act' to someone he knows will not do or act"; the servant must have some relation to the act, and by a relation of this measure manifestation in the act is accepted. Ibn Sawdakin's reminder is this: since the Real created the human being in His form, the act is an attribute of the human being, and "if the act were stripped from him, he would not be in the form of God"; Dost says how glad this reminder made him and notes that a master may sometimes learn from his student (pp. 301-302). The way-station also contains the method of politeness in teaching, that is, teaching gently without the student's noticing (p. 302).

The Exchange of Glory and Abasement's Garments

Chapter 296 (Mosaic, the Station of the Transfer of the Fortunate's Qualities to the Unfortunate in the Hereafter) treats the symmetrical structure of paradise's degrees and hell's depths. The chapter describes the exchange of glory and abasement on the Day of Mutual Loss and Gain: "when the Day of Resurrection comes, the unbeliever's glory and joy are stripped from him and clothed upon the believer. The believer's abasement and fear — his garment during worship in worldly life — are stripped from him and clothed upon the unbeliever on the Day of Resurrection" (p. 301).

The Glorification of Every Form, and the Serpent on Mount Qaf

In Chapter 297 Dost explains the verse "Everything glorifies its Lord with praise": every form in the world glorifies its Creator with a particular praise inspired in it; the corruption of a form is the very appearance of another form in the same substance, and the time of the passing away of forms is the time of the coming into being of others (pp. 303-304). He says the whole world other than humans and jinn has the characteristic of unveiling what is hidden from the human senses, and that the stones', plants', and animals' knowing the Prophet's prophethood arises from this (p. 304). According to what Yusuf b. Ahlaf al-Kumi related in the year 586, Musa al-Sardani greeted the serpent that encircles the ocean at Mount Qaf; the serpent asked after Shaykh Abu Madyan and said, "When God takes someone as a friend, He makes his name heard by our essences and sends down his love to us, to the earth, and to our hearts. Then every stone, soil, tree, or animal knows and loves him" (pp. 304-305).

The "More Than" Form, the Names of the Names, and the Face

Dost says all creatures other than the human being are in the station of humility and lowliness, while the human being may rise to claim grandeur against God; Iblis was haughty toward Adam but not toward God; when such a claim became settled in Pharaoh and his people, God placed the "more than" form in existence (p. 305). This form is of two kinds, blamed (Pharaoh's claim) and praised ("the most merciful of the merciful," "the best of creators") (pp. 305-306). The way-station also contains the science of names and the reverence shown to what is named: the name by which God calls Himself "Allah" in Arabic is "Huda" in Persian, and these are "the names of the names"; we are forbidden to travel into enemy territory with a Qur'an copy because the copy has a power of indication (pp. 306-307). Saying the face of the perfected servant is more worthy of reverence, he relates that the Prophet said to a man who struck his slave's face, "Beware of the face! For God created Adam in His form"; "The face of a thing means that thing's essence" (p. 307).

Creating and Decreeing: A Difference Among Names

Chapter 297 (Muhammadan, the Station of Praising the Leveling of Man's Clay at the Highest Rank) treats the contrast between the human being's self-aggrandizement (through the formula "more than," the example of Pharaoh) and the Real's greatness through mercy; it then draws a fine distinction among the divine names: the names al-Khaliq and al-Qadir represent "creating," while al-Mudabbir/al-Mufassil/al-Muqaddir represent "decreeing" (p. 307) — two distinct categories of divine act, separated both in wording and in meaning.

Shibli, the Mercy Hidden in Forgetting, and the Concessions

Saying that one confined and bound by the authority of the reason of obligation is not addressed with the fulfillment of his aims, Dost gives the example of Shibli: it is related that Shibli was brought back to lucidity at the times of prayer, and after prayer his state of rapture again overcame him; he reports al-Junayd's words, "Praise be to God, who prevents evil being spoken about Shibli […]" (p. 309). He explains the mercy God hides in the forgetting that occurs in the world: sins and errors are determined in God's knowledge; had they been committed in a state of openness and unveiling, there would be exaggeration in the lessening of shame before God. The Prophet's hadith: when God wishes to carry out His decree and determination, He takes away the minds of the people of intellect, and when He has carried them out, He gives their minds back so that they take heed; also "Error and forgetting have been lifted from my community" (p. 310). One who breaks his fast through forgetting must still make it up even if he does not sin, because God fed him and gave him drink; the hadith is also recalled in which the Prophet said of the one who asked, "Is the pilgrimage every year?" that had he said "yes" it would have become obligatory (p. 310). Saying that the jurists multiplied rulings through cause, analogy, and juristic preference, and withheld the mercy of concession from ordinary people, he relates the Prophet's words: "God has given you a charity. Take the charity He gave" (p. 311).

Hidden and Open Remembrance; Knowledge as Recollection

Dost explains the difference between the Real's knowledge of what a human being hides and of what he declares: the Real has an unseen and a locus of manifestation; by the name the Inward the Real remembers His servant within Himself, and by the name the Outward He remembers him in an assembly of angels or an assembly of divine names; what is more hidden than the secret is known only to the Real: "So knowledge concerns three things: the open, the secret, and what is more hidden than the secret" (p. 312). God placed in the human being the knowledge of everything, then set a veil between him and that knowledge, which is shared by the whole world; the verse "We are nearer to him than his jugular vein" is cited (pp. 312-313). What the servant knows is a recollection: "The human being's knowledge is always recollection"; Dhu'l-Nun al-Misri is given as an example, and God made His creatures forget this knowledge as He did the covenant (p. 313). The way-station also announces that each divine name is named by all the divine names ("Call upon God or call upon the All-Merciful") and that each substance contains all the realities of the world; Dost says this knowledge was given to him apart from the community, and that Abu'l-Qasim b. Qasi wrote the same view in his Khal' al-Na'layn (p. 314).

The Close: Three Classes of Human Beings and Everyday Analogies

The close of Chapter 297 ends with a classification dividing human beings into three classes: "we, then, are three classes: the first class are those whose thought is sound and whose sight is sharp. The second class are those with a veil over their eyes... some of us are blind and cross-eyed... there is no fourth class" (p. 308). Another passage within Chapter 295 explains through an analogy how meaning takes on different "forms" at different ranks (rational/sensory/imaginal): "they [meanings] are like the chameleon, which takes on the color of whatever it rests upon" (p. 292). Chapter 294's example of a horse race (pp. 283-284) illustrates the teaching that mercy "outpaces" wrath: a horse with a wide but slow stride eventually overtakes one with a narrow but fast one.
